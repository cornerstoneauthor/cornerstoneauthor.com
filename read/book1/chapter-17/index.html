CHAPTER 17

NOT YET

By the next dawn, the vale was awake before the mist had left the river. The news of Stony Creek had spread from hearth to hearth before sunrise, and every longhouse was talking of wolves and the high passes.

Bren came out of the mill-shed while the sky was still gray. His shoulder was stiff, but his eyes were clear. He found Harold and two older trappers gathered in the central lane, an old piece of tanned sheepskin spread out on a flat bench. Charcoal lines marked the crags. Bren let out a slow breath; he knew then that Ashvale wasn't going to turn its back.

Harold spent the morning checking the details. He calculated the march days, the weight of dry grain needed per man, and the mountain tracks with the familiarity of someone who had measured every stone twenty years ago. Aether and Kaelen stayed at his elbow, hauling pack-harnesses from the stores and sharpening stakes.

At noon, Bren pointed to a narrow notch between two charcoal lines on the sheepskin. Before he could speak, Kaelen planted a finger on the leather.

"The dry creek?" Kaelen asked.

Bren blinked. "You've taken meat there?"

"No," Kaelen said, tracing the ridge line upward. "But if the packs are moving south along the shale, they won't stay on the high crag. There's no water until the lower pool. They'll use this draw to reach the bottom."

Bren stared at the boy, then nodded slowly. "Aye. That's where we lost the hound."

Aether looked at the sheepskin, seeing only black grease and old creases, so he remained quiet and kept oiling the leather straps. Harold gave Kaelen a brief, unreadable glance from beneath his heavy brows, then returned to the map.

Later, while the sun was hanging low over the western pines, Kaelen found his father behind the forge, checking the hemp ropes.

"I'm going with the line," Kaelen said.

Harold didn't stop his hands. He ran his palm down a twelve-foot length of twisted hemp, feeling for rot. "Not yet."

Kaelen’s face went rigid. "Why?"

Harold laid the rope down, his large, scarred hand resting flat on the timber. His eyes met his son's with the weight of an anvil. "Because I said not yet."

Kaelen stood there for three breaths, his jaw set so tight the muscle bunched under his ear. Then he turned and walked out of the yard without another word.

Aether found him by the river at twilight, hauling the heavy willow baskets into the skiff. Kaelen worked with unnecessary force, slamming the wicker down against the thwarts.

"He refused?" Aether asked.

"Aye."

"Did he give a reason?"

Kaelen coiled a line with sharp, jerking movements. "He said 'not yet'. That's all he ever gives."

Aether watched the dark water gurgle against the boat’s stem. "He doesn't explain because he thinks we'll understand once we're on it."

They walked back in the dusk. Near the gate, Aether stopped. "You should be on that trail, Kaelen. You know the ridge signs better than half the men he's taking."

Kaelen looked at him, his expression softening slightly in the shadows. "You want to go too."

Aether didn't deny it. The image of Stony Creek—the injured men, the cold fear Bren had brought down the mountain—had lodged itself behind his eyes. "Aye," he murmured. "I want to see it."

"He won't let either of us cross the boundary stone," Kaelen said, turning back toward the lane.

That night, Aether took an iron pot of mutton broth to the mill-shed. Bren sat on his bedding, his skin looking less gray in the lamp-light. They spoke for an hour. Bren talked about the great elm that grew in the center of Stony Creek, the old widow who baked the rye-loaves with cumin, and the three children who always threw stones at the crows during the hay-haul.

Aether listened. The charcoal marks on the sheepskin began to gather faces: an old widow at an oven, children in a hayfield, a great elm over a village lane.

Long after the village went dark, Harold remained in the lean-to. He checked the iron tips of the hunting spears, his fingers moving automatically.

He paused, his eyes falling on a corner of the bench. A small pack sat there, neatly bound with grease-lines, two spare bowstrings and a hunting horn laid exactly where a man’s left hand could find them in the dark. It was Kaelen’s gear, sorted with perfect hunter’s economy.

Beside it lay a second bundle. It was loose and somewhat clumsy—the dried strips of beef wrapped in broad leaves instead of linen, the hemp rope coiled backward, and three heavy stones meant for a sling that were far too large for a long march. But it had been cleaned with great care. That was Aether’s pack.

Harold stood over the two bundles for a long time, the wind-bell over the forge door clinking softly in the midnight draft. He closed his eyes. He remembered another man standing over a dark pack twenty winters ago, telling a boy that the road was too steep.

He reached out, touched the loose knot on Aether’s bundle, and let out a long, slow sigh into the dark.
