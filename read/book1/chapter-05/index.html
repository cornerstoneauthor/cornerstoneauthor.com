CHAPTER 6

The Thaw

Winter, stubborn as it was, broke at last.

The first sign came not from the sky or the forest, but from the roofs.

Water.

Aether woke one morning to the faint sound of dripping beyond the longhouse wall. At first he thought he had imagined it. Then he pushed open the timber door.

Plip.

A drop fell from the eaves.

Plip.

Another struck the dark earth below.

Long spears of ice still hung from the roofs, but water ran from their tips.

Aether stepped outside.

The air smelled different.

Wet earth.

He had almost forgotten the smell.

Snow still covered the ridges, but here and there dark ground had begun to show through. Along the edge of the forest, the first traces of green were returning.

Spring had come to Ashvale.

And with it came work.

Fences damaged by winter had to be repaired. Seed stores were opened and inspected. Paths were cleared. Roofs patched. Woodpiles moved. Tools sharpened.

Winter demanded that people survive.

Spring demanded that they prepare to survive another year.

Harold began leaving the longhouse before dawn. Some days he returned carrying fresh-cut timber. On others he took Kaelen into the high ridges to repair the small hunting shelters along the mountain lines.

Kaelen had been particularly pleased with himself lately.

Harold had finally given him sole charge of several deadfalls along the eastern draw.

It was not much territory.

To Kaelen, it might as well have been a kingdom.

One afternoon he came back carrying two fat winter hares.

He dropped them onto the bench beside Aether.

“Look at those.”

Aether looked.

“My own line,” Kaelen said. “My own kill.”

Aether lifted one of the hares and judged its weight.

“Good size.”

Kaelen grinned.

“My da says if I keep this up, he'll let me track the northern timber alone in two winters.”

Aether put the hare down.

“Doesn't that frighten you?”

Kaelen frowned.

“What?”

“The northern timber.”

“No.”

Aether looked at him.

Kaelen sighed.

“All right. Sometimes.”

“The wolves?”

“Aye.”

“Whiteouts?”

“Aye.”

“Getting lost?”

“Aye.”

Aether waited.

“Then why do it?”

Kaelen picked up the hares.

“Because the larders don't fill themselves.”

He slung them over his shoulder.

“Someone has to walk the timber.”

Then he headed toward the smokehouse.

Aether watched him go.

He and Kaelen had grown up beneath the same roof, eaten at the same table, and walked the same valley.

Yet they seemed to look at the world in entirely different ways.

Aether wanted to understand it.

Kaelen wanted to know how to live in it.

***

Since the wayfarer had left Ashvale, Aether had begun keeping a record.

He collected flat scraps of pine from the woodsheds and used charcoal from the hearth to copy the markings he found.

First came the lines from the Old Stone Road.

Then the shapes carved into the fallen pillars east of the village.

Finally, from memory, he copied what he could of Garan's wooden carving.

His drawings were poor.

Some lines were too thick. Others wandered where the originals had been straight. More than once he rubbed a section away and began again.

But he kept at it.

He copied every curve he could find. Every cluster of dots. Every angular mark.

One afternoon, Garan found him lying on his stomach beside the Old Stone Road, a pine board in front of him and charcoal blackening his fingers.

The old man stopped nearby.

Aether did not notice him until he sat back to compare his drawing with the stone.

“What are you making?” Garan asked.

“A record.”

Garan came closer.

“For what?”

Aether looked down at the fading mark in the stone.

Spring water had gathered in its grooves.

“I don't want to forget it.”

Garan said nothing.

“And some of these are already hard to see,” Aether continued. “If I don't copy them now, maybe next winter they'll be gone.”

The old man looked from the stone to the boards beside Aether.

“Good.”

Aether glanced up.

Garan rarely praised anything so quickly.

“That's all?”

Garan smiled.

“What more do you want?”

“I don't know.”

“Then good will have to do.”

He turned to leave, then stopped.

“Keep the record, lad.”

Aether looked at the charcoal in his hand.

“Why?”

Garan glanced back.

“Because memory changes things.”

Then he continued toward the village.

Aether watched him for a moment before bending over the stone again.

***

Spring advanced.

Snow became water. Frozen streams broke open. Mud replaced ice along the paths, and the smell of pine grew stronger with every warmer day.

One evening, Aether and Kaelen sat beside the Old Stone Road.

Aether had spread several of his pine boards across his knees. Kaelen sat beside him shaving a cedar shaft for an arrow.

Neither spoke.

Aether moved one board beside another, comparing the markings.

Kaelen's knife stopped.

“Aether.”

“Hm?”

“Give me that.”

Aether looked up.

Kaelen pointed with the arrow shaft.

“The board.”

Aether handed it over.

Kaelen studied the markings.

“What?”

Kaelen ignored him.

He placed the board on the ground and pointed to one long curving line.

“Look.”

“I've looked at it a hundred times.”

“No. Look at it like this.”

Kaelen traced the curve with the end of the arrow.

“This bends like White-Water Fork.”

Aether frowned.

“What?”

“The river.”

“I know what White-Water Fork is.”

“Then look.”

Kaelen traced the line again.

Aether did.

The resemblance was there.

Kaelen pointed to another mark.

“And this.”

“A straight line.”

“A ridge.”

Aether looked from the drawing toward the mountains.

Kaelen tapped three dots clustered near the crossing of the lines.

“These could be camps.”

Aether stared at the board.

“Or houses,” Kaelen added.

Something shifted in Aether's mind.

He pulled another board closer.

Then another.

Curves.

Straight lines.

Clusters.

Until that moment, he had treated each marking as a symbol—as if every line concealed some ancient word he did not know how to read.

But what if they were not words?

“What if it's a map?” Aether whispered.

Kaelen shrugged.

“Looks like one.”

Aether spread the boards across the ground.

“Wait.”

He moved two of them together.

A line on one continued almost perfectly onto the next.

His pulse quickened.

He turned another board.

Another line joined.

“Kaelen.”

His brother had already gone back to shaving the arrow.

“What?”

“Look at this.”

“I am looking.”

“No, you're not.”

“I already told you it looks like a map.”

Aether stared at him.

“How are you not more interested in this?”

Kaelen blew a curl of cedar from his knee.

“Because I don't know where it goes.”

Aether looked back down.

Roads.

Rivers.

Perhaps settlements.

The carvings had not become less mysterious.

They had become useful.

That somehow made them more astonishing.

***

By the end of the week, the last heavy snow had disappeared from the valley floor.

For the first time in months, the Old Stone Road lay bare.

Its dark stones crossed the new green of Ashvale and disappeared between the trees.

Aether stood on the ridge above it.

The wind was warmer now.

Far below, someone was walking along the road.

Not the wayfarer who had stayed in Ashvale weeks before.

Another traveler.

The figure wore gray wool and carried a long staff. He did not turn toward the village or call for shelter. He simply followed the ancient stones toward the eastern pass.

Aether watched until the trees swallowed him.

Then he looked back along the road.

People came and went.

Garan had walked it.

The wayfarer had walked it.

Strangers Aether would never know walked it.

The villages beyond the vale fought, traded, starved, survived.

And through all of it, the road remained.

Aether looked down at the pine board in his hands.

Perhaps Kaelen was wrong.

Perhaps the markings were not a map at all.

But if they were—

Aether turned the board slightly.

A river.

A ridge.

A road.

Places connected to other places.

For the first time, he wondered whether the Old Stone Road was only one small part of something much larger.

Spring wind moved through the pines.

Aether tucked the board beneath his arm and started down toward Ashvale.

There was more to copy.

***

Ashvale was still dark when Harold began checking the wagon.

A damp spring wind moved through the valley. Behind the shuttered windows of the longhouses, only a few hearths showed signs of life.

Harold walked once around the wagon, inspecting the wheels, traces, and cargo.

Cured pelts.

Dried venison.

Carved wooden tools.

Several coarse sacks of rock salt for barter.

Kaelen had already checked the harness three times.

Harold noticed him reaching for it again.

“You've done that.”

Kaelen grinned. “A fourth time won't hurt.”

“It won't help either.”

Kaelen checked it anyway.

Harold shook his head.

Aether had slept little himself.

But while Kaelen had spent half the night thinking about hunters, bows, traps, and whatever might be traded at the fair, Aether had been thinking about names.

Riverbend.

Pinewood Ridge.

Windhill.

Places he had heard travelers mention around the fire.

Places that had existed, until now, only in his imagination.

In eleven winters, Aether had never gone beyond the country surrounding Ashvale.

Today he would.

When the first gray light appeared above the eastern ridge, Harold climbed onto the wagon.

“Up.”

Kaelen was already there.

Aether followed.

Harold flicked the reins.

The wagon rolled through the gate and onto the Old Stone Road.

***

Spring had changed the valley.

The great snowdrifts had collapsed into dark banks of slush. Water ran beside the road, carrying twigs and dead leaves toward the lower ground. Birds called from the timber.

Aether sat high on the wagon bench and watched everything.

Before long, they began passing other travelers.

A shepherd drove a small flock of mountain sheep along the shoulder of the road.

Two men pushed a handcart loaded with iron pots and tools.

Hunters came out of narrow tracks between the trees with bundles of fur across their backs.

All of them were going the same way.

Aether watched one group join the road from the north, then another from a wooded hollow farther east.

The road was gathering people.

Harold knew some of them.

He raised a hand to one shepherd and exchanged a few words with an older hunter. Others he greeted only with a nod.

Aether looked back.

More travelers had appeared behind them.

For most of his life, the Old Stone Road had seemed empty.

Today it was alive.

***

Near midday, the wagon climbed the last ridge.

Harold pulled the team to a slower pace.

Aether looked ahead.

Then forgot to breathe.

The land opened into a broad basin below them.

People were everywhere.

Dozens of wagons stood around the clearing. Hundreds of men, women, and children moved among them. A dark river curved along one side of the basin, and at its center stood three enormous oaks whose branches spread over much of the gathering.

Blankets and tables had been arranged beneath them.

Goods covered nearly every surface.

Iron tools.

Bundles of wool.

Salt fish.

Furs.

Clay pots.

Leather.

Baskets.

Things Aether could not name.

The noise reached them even from the ridge.

Voices.

Animals.

Laughter.

Hammering.

Children shouting.

Aether stared.

He had never seen so many people in one place.

Beside him, Kaelen leaned forward.

“Look at those bows.”

Aether barely heard him.

“The hunters by the middle tree,” Kaelen said. “Look at the limbs. Those are longer than ours.”

Aether glanced over.

Kaelen's eyes were fixed on a group of hunters.

“Southern yew, maybe.”

Aether turned back toward the crowd.

He was looking at faces.

Some people wore cloaks dyed in colors he had never seen in Ashvale. Others spoke with accents that made familiar words sound strange. A woman passed carrying a basket of silver-scaled fish. Two children ran after her, laughing.

Aether watched them disappear into the crowd.

The world was not empty beyond Ashvale.

It was crowded.

***

Once the wagon was secured, several traders called Harold away.

Before leaving, he looked at the boys.

“Stay inside the tree line.”

Kaelen nodded.

“So do I have to say the rest?”

“No.”

Harold looked at Aether.

Aether sighed.

“Don't wander off.”

“Good.”

Harold left.

For a moment, the brothers stood together.

Then Kaelen looked toward the hunters.

Aether looked toward everything else.

They separated without a word.

Aether wandered slowly through the fair.

He did not buy anything.

Mostly, he listened.

People from Riverbend had brought crates of dried fish.

Shepherds from Pinewood Ridge traded bundles of coarse gray wool.

Men from the northern slopes carried raw iron and flint.

Around the cook fires, people argued about the late frost, wolves in the western draws, and whether spring runoff would make the lower crossings impassable.

Aether listened to names.

Windhill.

Stone Bridge.

Blackrock Crossing.

Whitetail Slope.

The Vale of the Long River.

He repeated them quietly to himself.

Every name belonged somewhere.

Every somewhere had people.

Ashvale, he began to understand, was only one of them.

That realization did not make home seem smaller.

It made the world seem larger.

***

Near sunset, Kaelen appeared out of the crowd and grabbed Aether by the sleeve.

“Come.”

“What?”

“You need to see this.”

Kaelen pulled him around the far side of the great oaks.

Several older woodsmen sat in a circle on the packed earth.

Between them lay a large piece of elk hide, darkened by years of grease and handling.

Lines had been scratched across its surface.

Hundreds of them.

Aether stopped.

“Is that—”

One of the hunters looked up.

“A map.”

Kaelen was already kneeling.

Aether dropped beside him.

The hunter used a short wooden pointer to indicate a series of marks.

“Whitetail Slope.”

He moved the pointer.

“Wolf runs here.”

Another mark.

“We took a large bear near this creek two winters ago.”

He traced a line farther south.

“And don't bring horses through this canyon before summer. Spring water turns the bottom into a bog.”

Another hunter leaned over.

“That creek doesn't run there anymore.”

“It did last year.”

“Last year isn't this year.”

The first hunter grunted.

A third man took a piece of charcoal and altered the line.

“The deer moved north too.”

“Because of the wolves.”

“Because you fools hunted the southern herd too hard.”

The men began arguing.

Kaelen listened with complete attention.

Aether barely heard them.

He was staring at the hide.

Rivers.

Ridges.

Paths.

Places where animals gathered.

Places where people camped.

The map was crude. Some lines had been rubbed out and redrawn. Others were little more than scratches.

But Aether understood what he was looking at.

Someone had walked those places.

Then come back and made marks so another person could find them.

He thought of his pine boards.

The Old Stone Road.

The fallen pillars.

Garan's carving.

Kaelen's words returned to him.

It looks like a map.

Aether leaned closer to the elk hide.

If these hunters could mark their small part of the wilderness—

What had the people who carved the stones been trying to mark?

***

Kaelen talked almost the entire way home.

“That northern hunter said the stags beyond the eastern ridge are nearly twice the size of ours.”

Harold grunted.

“And did he mention how far you'd have to carry one after killing it?”

Kaelen ignored that.

“And those traps—did you see the double springs? If we made two or three, we could set them along the upper draw before winter.”

Harold glanced at him.

“We?”

Kaelen hesitated.

“You.”

“Better.”

Aether sat beside them, looking out at the passing trees.

His thoughts remained with the map.

By the time the wagon entered Ashvale, darkness had settled across the valley.

Harold began unloading.

Kaelen helped with the pelts.

Aether carried two sacks inside, then disappeared into the longhouse.

He lit a small tallow candle.

One by one, he spread his pine boards across the floor.

Then he found a clean piece of wood.

From memory, he began drawing the hunter's map.

The river first.

Then the ridge.

The crossing.

The camps.

He forgot details.

Some lines were probably wrong.

More than once he stopped, trying to remember whether a creek had turned north or south.

But he kept drawing.

When he finished, he placed the new board beside the others.

The difference was obvious.

The hunters' map was rough and practical. It showed a small piece of land they knew well.

The ancient markings were something else.

Larger.

More complicated.

Perhaps Kaelen had been right.

Perhaps they were maps.

Perhaps they were not.

Aether still could not read them.

But now he understood something he had not understood before.

A map was not the land itself.

It was what someone remembered about the land.

Aether looked at the boards spread around him.

Someone had remembered these places.

Someone had thought they were worth marking.

The candle burned lower.

Aether picked up his charcoal again.

At the top of the newest board, he carefully wrote the first name he had ever placed on a map.

Ashvale.
