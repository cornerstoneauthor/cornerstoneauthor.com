CHAPTER 5

THE WAYFARER

Winter was dying, though it surrendered slowly.

Heavy drifts still filled the gullies around Ashvale, but the wind had lost the iron bite of midwinter. Deep among the pines came the occasional drip of melting frost.

In the vale, there were few sweeter sounds.

Near dusk, the village hounds began barking.

Hunters carrying bundles of firewood stopped and turned toward the southern boundary.

A lone figure was approaching along the Old Stone Road.

The traveler wore a salt-stained cloak over faded wool, with a battered pack across his shoulders and a short sword at his hip. He walked with the steady pace of a man accustomed to long roads.

“A wayfarer,” someone said.

Harold stopped and narrowed his eyes.

Then, to Aether's surprise, a crooked grin appeared beneath his beard.

“Look at that,” Harold said. “The fool is still breathing.”

The traveler was perhaps forty, his face weathered by sun and cold, his beard touched with gray. When he saw Harold, he smiled.

“Still haven't starved, I see.”

Harold laughed and stepped forward, catching the man by the shoulder.

“Not yet.”

The two greeted each other with the rough familiarity of men who had known one another a long time.

Aether stood near the edge of the gathering crowd.

Travelers had come through Ashvale before. He remembered them vaguely—hunters, peddlers, men passing north or south before winter closed the roads.

He had never paid much attention to them.

Now he could not stop looking.

The man's boots were deeply cracked. The hem of his cloak had been repaired more than once. Strange red dust clung to the bottom of his pack.

None of it came from Ashvale.

The thought struck Aether with unexpected force.

This man had walked the road.

He had come from beyond the vale.

***

That night, the central fire burned high.

The traveler sat nearest the flames with a steaming bowl of mutton broth in his hands and a horn of dark ale beside him.

No one in Ashvale would have thought to send a traveler back into the cold without food and fire. The road was hard enough without men making it harder.

Aether and Kaelen sat near the outer edge of the gathering, pretending to work at pieces of wood while listening to every word.

Harold took a drink.

“How fares the north?”

“The same as ever.”

Harold grunted. “And what does that mean this year?”

The traveler wiped his mouth.

“Some raid for land. Some for water. Some for sheep. The rest raid whoever did the raiding.”

A few of the hunters laughed.

Harold did not.

“Men never tire of tearing at each other.”

“It isn't boredom,” the traveler said. “It's hunger.”

The laughter faded.

He stared into his bowl.

“Stone River Valley has one village left.”

Harold frowned.

“There were three.”

“There were.”

“And the others?”

“Gone.”

The traveler took another drink.

“Some died before Yule. The rest went south.”

No one spoke for a while.

Aether looked around the fire.

Ashvale had always seemed permanent to him.

The houses had been here before he was born. Harold knew every trail in the surrounding forest. Garan remembered winters from half a century ago.

Yet somewhere beyond the mountains, villages just like this one had disappeared.

Not ancient cities.

Not ruins from Garan's stories.

Villages where people had been alive only months before.

“What about the south?” one of the hunters asked.

The traveler gave a tired smile.

“Two clans spent three moons fighting over a stretch of riverland.”

Kaelen leaned forward.

“Who won?”

The traveler looked at him.

“No one.”

Kaelen frowned.

“How can no one win?”

“The river is still there, lad.”

He took another sip of ale.

“Most of the men aren't.”

Kaelen fell quiet.

Aether remembered the words.

***

As the night went on, the conversation turned away from hunger and fighting.

The traveler spoke instead of the road.

He had seen rivers so wide that mist hid the opposite bank. Settlements built among the roots of enormous trees. Stone cities in the south with walls taller than any building in Ashvale.

Aether listened to every word.

Each place made the world larger.

Finally, he could no longer keep silent.

“What is the farthest place you've ever been?”

The traveler looked toward him.

“The farthest?”

Aether nodded.

The man scratched at his beard.

“The Great Salt Sea, I suppose.”

“The sea?”

“Aye.”

Aether waited.

The traveler smiled.

“Imagine a lake.”

“I've seen a lake.”

“Now imagine one so large you can't see the other side.”

Aether frowned.

“How large?”

“Large enough that you can stand on the shore and look straight ahead and see nothing but water until it meets the sky.”

Aether tried to picture it.

He couldn't.

Beside him, Kaelen leaned forward.

“Are there rabbits there?”

The circle erupted in laughter.

Even Harold bent his head, shaking with it.

The traveler laughed loudest.

“Aye, young hunter. Thousands.”

Kaelen nodded, satisfied.

Aether stared at him.

“You don't know if that's true.”

Kaelen shrugged.

“Good enough for me.”

***

The next morning, the traveler prepared to leave.

Villagers gathered around him to barter. Cured pelts and smoked meat changed hands for sea salt, iron nails, and a handful of other things difficult to find in the vale.

Aether remained nearby.

He watched the traveler tighten the straps on his pack, inspect his horse's hooves, and secure his belongings for the road.

Yesterday the man had arrived from somewhere Aether could barely imagine.

Now he was simply going back.

The thought fascinated him.

The traveler reached for his reins.

Then stopped.

His eyes had fallen on a flat piece of pine leaning against the woodpile.

Aether had spent the last few days carving it.

Branches.

Curving lines.

Dots like stars.

The marks from the Old Stone Road.

The marks from the fallen pillars.

And now, after what Garan had shown him, the marks from the old man's wooden carving.

The traveler let go of the reins.

He crossed the yard and picked up the board.

His expression changed.

Aether noticed immediately.

The easy smile was gone.

The traveler turned the wood in his hands, studying the carvings.

“Where did you get this?”

Aether stepped closer.

“I made it.”

The traveler looked sharply at him.

“Who taught you these marks?”

“No one.”

“Then where did you see them?”

Aether pointed toward the edge of the village.

“On the Old Stone Road. And on some fallen stones east of here.”

The traveler looked toward the road.

For several seconds, he said nothing.

Then his thumb moved slowly across one of the carved lines.

“Strange.”

Aether's heart quickened.

“What's strange?”

The traveler looked at him.

“I've seen these before.”

Aether forgot the cold.

“Where?”

The man looked down at the board again.

Aether waited.

“Where?”

The traveler handed it back.

“Far from here.”

“How far?”

A faint smile returned to the man's face.

“Farther than you've walked.”

“That doesn't answer me.”

“No.”

“Was it on another road?”

The traveler took up his reins.

“Maybe.”

“On a building?”

The man put one foot into the stirrup.

“Maybe.”

Aether followed him.

“What did they mean?”

The traveler stopped.

For the first time, the amusement left his face entirely.

“I don't know.”

Aether fell silent.

The man swung into the saddle.

“But I've seen them.”

He nodded once toward the board in Aether's hands.

“So whatever you're looking for, boy, it didn't begin in this valley.”

Aether stared at him.

The traveler turned his horse toward the Old Stone Road.

Harold called a farewell. Several others raised their hands.

Then the wayfarer rode east.

Aether stood at the edge of Ashvale and watched him grow smaller against the pale winter landscape.

He looked down at the board.

Garan had spent fifty years wondering whether one night in a storm had meant anything at all.

Now a stranger from beyond the vale had recognized the same marks.

It was not an answer.

But it was something.

Aether raised his eyes toward the road.

For most of his life, it had been little more than stone beneath his feet.

Then it had become a question.

Now, for the first time, it had become a way out.
