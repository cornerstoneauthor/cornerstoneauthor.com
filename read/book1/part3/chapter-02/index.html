CHAPTER 2

Odren

← PreviousPart III ContentsNext →

They walked on along the main street and soon found an inn. It stood beside the street, unassuming but very clean, a house of stone and timber two stories high, with a wooden sign at the door that had seen many years. When they pushed the door open, the smell of baking bread and stewed meat came out to meet them. It was just past noon, and the hall was full of travelers: some had only just arrived in the city and were asking the innkeeper about rooms; some had finished their meal and sat with maps spread open, planning the next leg of a journey in low voices; and by the window several merchants sat over their ale, talking of matters whose place-names the three travelers had never heard.

The innkeeper was a man in his fifties. When he saw them come in, he set down his ledger at once. "Rooms for the three of you?"

Kael nodded. "Three rooms."

The innkeeper drew out his register with practiced hands. "How many days?"

The three of them looked at one another. "For now… we do not know," Elowen answered softly.

He did not press them. He only smiled and said, "That is Riverport for you. A great many people arrive saying they will stay one day, and in the end they stay a year." As he spoke, he set three copper keys on the counter. "Welcome to Riverport."

Seren took her key, and a strange feeling rose in her heart. After drifting for so long, she felt for the first time that she could stop — truly stop — and draw breath. What she did not know was that less than a quarter of an hour after the gate register had been completed, a record bearing three names had already been carried into Odren's study.

The corridor upstairs was quiet, though the noise of the street still came faintly through the windows. When they opened their doors, all three of them let out a long breath at nearly the same moment. They had come all the way from the forest; they had passed through wolves, and through the narrow place between life and death; and only now had they truly come to rest. Kael set his baggage down beside the table and rolled his shoulder slowly. Seren opened the window, and the afternoon wind came in off the street, and from far away she could still hear the horns of the harbor. She stood looking down at the street in silence. There was none of the forest's stillness here, but there was another kind of life, belonging wholly to the city.

Elowen sat on the edge of the bed and set down at last the cloth bundle she had carried in her arms the whole way. She was silent for a long time before she said, softly, "We are here."

The room fell quiet. All three of them understood what the words meant. All along the road they had had one purpose only: to bring her safely to Riverport. Now it was done. And yet, for some reason, none of them felt the lightness they had expected — because all of them knew that from here onward, the road they walked would no longer be the same road.

Seren turned, and was about to speak, when a soft knock came at the door.

All three of them looked up. Kael was on his feet by instinct. Outside, the innkeeper's voice came, courteous and a little uncertain: "Forgive the disturbance — someone is asking for you downstairs."

Kael opened the door. The innkeeper stood outside, his face full of puzzlement. "It is a man from the lord's household. He says he is looking for a young lady by the name of Elowen."

The three of them exchanged glances. Elowen blinked. "So soon?"

The innkeeper gave a wry smile. "I could not say. But it would seem they already know you have entered the city."

A moment later, outside the inn, a fine black carriage stood waiting in the street. Beside it stood a man of about fifty, neatly dressed and composed in bearing. When he saw the three of them come out, he stepped forward and made Elowen a formal, grave bow. "May I ask — are you Miss Elowen?"

"I am."

A gentle smile appeared on the man's face. "My name is Matthew. I am Lord Odren's servant. When my lord learned that you had arrived in Riverport, he sent me at once to receive you."

At this, he looked toward Seren and toward Kael. His expression did not change in the least; still courteous, he said, "My lord also gave me a message to deliver."

He bowed slightly.

"My lord says — the three of you are to come home, together."

Matthew drew the carriage door open for them. "Please, the three of you."

The carriage was roomier inside than it looked from without. The seats were covered in dark leather; there was no decoration beyond need, only a small copper lamp fixed in one corner and a folding wooden table. Plainly, this carriage had been made for comfort and use, and not to proclaim its owner's rank.

When the three of them were settled, the carriage moved slowly away from the inn. The streets outside the window were as busy as ever, but the carriage did not hurry, and Kael, looking out, saw that it was not taking any of the shortcuts that threaded through the markets, but a broader and tidier street instead. He did not ask. Matthew seemed to read the question in his eyes, and said with a smile, "This is my lord's wish. It is Miss Elowen's first time in Riverport. There are some places she ought to come to know first."

Elowen nodded slightly.

As the carriage went on, the shops along the street gave way, little by little, to free-standing buildings of stone, and before every door there was a constant coming and going: clerks carrying thick ledgers in their arms, couriers running on errands, merchants and common folk arriving on business of their own. Matthew raised a hand toward the window. "That is the Harbor Office. Every merchant vessel that enters Riverport is registered there — cargo, crew, course, and berthing all fall under its charge."

Elowen looked where he pointed. A stone building three stories high stood beside the river, with small carts for carrying documents drawn up at its door, and officials in long robes of dark blue passing hurriedly in and out — busy, and yet wholly orderly. The carriage did not stop. It only rolled slowly past.

Not long after, they came to a building broader still, and before its doors stretched a long line: men pushing wooden carts heavy with goods, men standing patient with ledgers in their arms. "That is the Tax Office," Matthew went on. "Every cargo that enters Riverport is given its final inspection there. Many merchants dread the queue above all things." A smile touched the corner of his mouth. "But what they dread more is leaving something off the register. For once a thing is left off, next time it will not be a simple matter of paying the tax."

Kael sat to one side and said nothing through all of this, only now and then looking out of the window. He had seen all these places before. He had simply never truly noticed them when he passed.

The carriage went on, the road widened, and ahead of them opened a broad square, and at its center stood an ancient bell tower. The bell sounded suddenly, deep and far-carrying, and almost no one on the square so much as broke stride; they had plainly long been used to that voice. Matthew looked toward the tower. "You will come here often, in time to come. Behind the bell tower stands the Order of the Sacred Lamp."

Elowen started slightly, and looked where he meant without thinking. It was a cluster of quiet buildings in white stone, standing in sharp contrast to the busy streets around them. There was no clamor there, and no market stalls — only a few brothers of the Order in robes of gray-white, walking slowly along a cloister. Matthew offered no further introduction, and the carriage did not stop; it rounded the bell tower slowly and went on toward the higher parts of the city.

The higher they climbed, the fewer the shops along the street, and in their place the residences of officials began to appear. People still came and went here, but all was noticeably quieter: now and then a clerk hurried past with documents in his arms, and now and then a courier on a fast horse passed through the street at the guards' sign and galloped on toward the heights. There was no luxury anywhere in the quarter, yet it breathed an order built up over long years of daily turning. Seren watched it all in silence, and felt suddenly that what truly upheld Riverport might not be its harbor alone, but also these people before her eyes, who did their work in quiet, day after day.

Matthew walked ahead of them, and the three followed him through a spacious entrance hall. As they went, people kept stopping in their work to bow slightly to Matthew, and then returning to what they had been doing. No one spoke loudly; no one stared about. The whole residence was quiet and ordered. Along the corridor, the doors of offices stood open on both sides: here a man bent over his desk sorting files; there, several men stood before maps on the wall, discussing something in low voices; there again, a clerk carried newly copied documents at a brisk pace toward another room. It was not like a private house at all. It was more like an office of government that woke and worked every day of the year. Seren found her steps slowing without her willing it. She was seeing for the first time that what a ruler of a city faced each day was not only councils and nobles — far more often, it was document after document waiting to be dealt with, and matter after matter waiting to be decided.

Matthew stopped before a wooden door and knocked twice, lightly. "My lord."

From within came a steady voice, almost at once. "Come in."

Matthew pushed the door open — but he did not usher the three of them in at once. He went in first himself, and drew the door closed after him. The room within fell silent. Outside the door, only the three of them remained. Elowen drew a slow breath, and found that her palms, at some point she had not noticed, had grown faintly damp. Kael stood where he was and said nothing. Seren gently took Elowen's hand. She spoke no word of comfort; she only held it, once, lightly. Elowen raised her head and smiled at her.

A moment later, the door opened again. Matthew stood in the doorway, his composure undisturbed. "The three of you — my lord asks you to come in."

They entered the study. The room was spacious. One whole wall was given to books and files, and on the other hung a great map of Riverport, marked in dense detail with the harbor, the roads, the rivers, the warehouse quarters, and the villages and towns round about. On the wide desk, documents not yet dealt with lay in neat order, and a quill rested in its stand with the ink not yet dry upon it.

Behind the desk, a man of middle years was rising to his feet. He was about fifty, dressed in a long coat of dark gray, well cut and without a single ornament beyond need. His temples had taken on some gray, but it had cost him nothing of his vigor, and the long years of office had given him a stillness, a quality of being held in reserve. He came around the desk toward them, unhurried, making no show of an official's dignity. His gaze came to rest on Elowen, and stayed there a long time, as though he were seeking in this young girl before him the memory of someone else.

At last he smiled, gently. There was nothing of the ruler's majesty in that smile — only the plain gladness of an elder who sees one of his own young people come home. "Welcome home."

Elowen's eyes reddened. She bowed her head a little. "Uncle."

Odren nodded. His gaze moved then to Seren and to Kael. He asked nothing about who they were, and he did not hurry to speak; he only nodded to them, courteously. Then he looked back to Elowen, and his voice was as calm as before. "The road has been hard on you."

A brief silence followed. Then, as though he had suddenly remembered something, he looked toward the door.

"What about the others?"

The study fell utterly quiet.
