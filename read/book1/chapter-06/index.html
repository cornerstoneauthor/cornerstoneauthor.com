CHAPTER 8

The Drift of Years

The leaves thickened over the Old Stone Road.

Summer sunlight broke through the canopy and scattered across the ancient stones. Deer moved through the brush. The river swelled with mountain runoff and then slowly fell again as harvest approached.

Life in Ashvale followed its old rhythm.

People planted.

Hunted.

Gathered.

Cut wood.

Then autumn came, and they prepared for winter.

For the adults, one year often looked much like another.

For children, a year could change everything.

Aether's corner of the sleeping loft began to fill with pine boards.

At first, he recorded little more than names.

Riverbend.

Windhill.

Pinewood Ridge.

Whitetail Slope.

Then the names gathered details.

The knots used in Riverbend fishing nets.

The number of sheep kept on the Windhill slopes.

The curve of a Pinewood Ridge longbow.

The scarred hands of an old tracker from Whitetail.

Whenever a traveler stopped at the village well, Aether found a reason to sit nearby.

He rarely interrupted.

He listened.

Then, after dark, he wrote down what he remembered.

Sometimes the same traveler returned months later and told a story differently.

Aether noticed that too.

He kept both versions.

One afternoon, Garan discovered the growing stack behind the grain jars.

The old man pulled out one board.

Then another.

He sat for some time reading what Aether had written and studying the charcoal lines.

Aether waited.

Finally, Garan returned the boards to the stack.

He said nothing.

But he smiled.

***

Autumn turned the ridges gold.

Then gray.

Then white.

Harold came inside one evening and stopped when he saw Aether sitting on the floor surrounded by blackened boards.

“Still at those?”

Aether nodded.

“Aye.”

Harold looked down.

Roads.

Names.

Fragments of conversations.

Marks copied from old stone.

Aether waited for him to say something.

Harold only removed his sword belt and hung it beside the door.

Then he stepped carefully over the boards.

“Don't leave them where someone can put a boot through them.”

“I won't.”

Harold grunted and went to wash.

Aether returned to his work.

***

The winter was harder on Garan.

For three days he did not leave his furs.

When he finally returned to the central fire, he walked slowly and leaned more heavily on his stick.

His stories had changed too.

Sometimes he stopped in the middle of one and stared into the flames.

Once, he forgot the name of a hunter he had known for forty years.

No one laughed.

Garan eventually remembered.

Or perhaps someone quietly reminded him.

Aether was not sure.

But the little wooden carving still hung beneath the old man's tunic.

And whenever the fire burned low, Garan noticed.

One night, wind shook the longhouse walls while Aether sat beside him feeding small branches into the hearth.

Garan watched the coals.

“The names,” he said.

Aether looked over.

“What about them?”

“You still keep them?”

“Yes.”

Garan nodded.

“Good.”

A long silence followed.

Then the old man spoke again.

“Keep them.”

Aether looked at him.

Garan's eyes remained on the fire.

Aether did not ask why.

“I will.”

***

Spring returned.

The second Spring Fair felt different.

Aether recognized people now.

The net-maker from Riverbend.

The old shepherd from Windhill.

The bowyer from Pinewood Ridge.

People who had once seemed to belong to stories now had names, habits, bad knees, missing teeth, loud laughs.

Some remembered Aether.

Others did not.

He remembered them anyway.

Kaelen had changed more visibly.

He was taller now, his shoulders broader. His hands had hardened from bowstrings, traps, and wet rope.

He sometimes disappeared into the timber for several days with Harold.

Then, eventually, without Harold.

When Harold gave him the watch horn for his first solitary night on the ridge, Kaelen took it without ceremony.

Aether noticed that Harold watched him climb the platform.

He kept watching long after Kaelen had reached the top.

***

One afternoon, Aether sat beneath the eaves with a fresh pine board across his knees.

Kaelen walked past carrying a bundle of cedar arrow shafts.

He stopped.

“You missed someone.”

Aether looked up.

“Who?”

“The boatbuilder from the river mouth.”

Aether frowned.

“When?”

“Last night.”

“You talked to him?”

“He talked. I ate.”

Kaelen pointed at an empty place on the board.

“He knew the southern currents. Said the river splits below Stone Bridge during the spring floods.”

Aether stared at him.

“What?”

Kaelen shifted the shafts under his arm.

“Are you going to write it down or not?”

Aether smiled.

Then he picked up the charcoal.

Kaelen continued toward the shed.

Aether bent over the board.

A moment later he called after him.

“Kaelen.”

His brother turned.

“What was his name?”

Kaelen told him.

Aether wrote that down first.

***

Another autumn came.

Another winter.

Ashvale changed slowly.

A roof collapsed beneath heavy snow and was rebuilt before spring.

A child was born in the northern longhouse.

An old woman who had lived beside the stream since before Harold was born did not wake one winter morning.

The following summer, two new families came from farther north.

They raised longhouses near the southern wall.

No one in Ashvale spoke of these things as history.

They were simply things that happened.

Aether wrote them down.

***

The third spring stripped the snow from the Old Stone Road.

Aether was twelve.

Kaelen was fifteen.

Gray had begun to spread through Harold's beard.

Garan walked more slowly now.

Two new longhouses stood near the southern wall, their fresh-cut pine still shining pale against the older timber.

And beneath the oaks beside the stream stood a new mound of earth.

Near twilight, Aether carried his boards up the slope overlooking the valley.

Below him, Ashvale went about its evening.

Kaelen emerged from the tree line with grouse hanging from his belt.

Harold worked beside the stable, the steady strike of his hammer carrying across the clearing.

Garan sat beside the central fire.

Aether lowered himself onto the grass.

He took the oldest board from the bottom of the stack.

Almost no bare wood remained.

Roads crossed it.

Names crowded its edges.

Some belonged to people who still came through Ashvale every spring.

Others belonged to people who would never come again.

Aether ran his thumb over one of them.

Then he placed the board back beneath the others.

From the top of the stack, he took a new piece of pine.

Clean.

Pale.

Empty.

He rested it across his knees.

Below, someone called for Kaelen.

A child began crying near one of the new longhouses.

Harold's hammer continued its steady rhythm.

And beside the fire, Garan bent forward to place another branch among the coals.

Aether pressed the charcoal to the wood.

Then he began again.

***

Spring runoff filled the valley stream.

Wild clover pushed through the joints of the Old Stone Road, pale against the weathered masonry.

Aether was twelve now.

His legs had lengthened. His shoulders had begun to lose the softness of childhood. In the loft, his collection of pine boards had grown into stacks filled with river bends, settlement names, livestock counts, and the names of people who had died.

Kaelen was fifteen.

His hands were marked by rope burns and bowstrings, and when the hunting horn sounded, he no longer waited to be invited.

One gray morning, Harold stood in the yard checking the straps on his pack.

He and Kaelen were preparing for three days in the northern timber.

Aether carried out two waterskins.

Harold reached into the tool crate and pulled out a flat sharpening stone.

“Take this to the mill.”

Aether looked at him.

“To Lane?”

“Aye.”

Harold placed the stone in his hand.

“Tell him the mountain melt is heavy. The timber crews are coming down early to clear the piers.”

Aether nodded.

“He is not to drop the wheel gates until they arrive.”

“Don't drop the gates. Wait for the crews.”

Harold nodded.

“Good.”

Kaelen passed on his way toward the gate and tapped the sharpening stone with the end of an arrow.

“Don't drop that either.”

Aether rolled his eyes.

Kaelen grinned and followed Harold out of Ashvale.

Aether looked down at the stone in his hands.

It was a simple errand.

Still, Harold had given it to him.

***

The river trail was loud with runoff.

Aether followed the Old Stone Road toward the mill.

Near one of the old mile markers, he noticed a fresh line cut into the base of the stone.

He stopped.

Only for a moment.

He crouched, studied the mark, then continued.

Farther down the road, an old net-maker from Riverbend was resting beside a well.

Aether knew him from the Spring Fair.

The man recognized him too.

They talked.

A shipwright had opened a new yard near the delta.

Three timber barges were trapped upstream by the high water.

The spring current had shifted near one of the southern crossings.

Aether listened.

He repeated the details to himself as he walked.

By the time he reached the mill, the water wheel was roaring.

Lane stood on the wet footing with an oak mallet in his hand.

Aether held out the sharpening stone.

“Harold sent this.”

Lane took it.

“He said the mountain melt is heavy. The timber crews are coming early.”

Lane nodded.

Aether looked toward the wheel.

“And you're to prepare to drop the gates.”

Lane tossed the stone into his toolbox.

“Understood.”

Aether headed home.

***

The next afternoon, Lane came up the path shouting.

“Harold!”

Aether looked up from beside the grain bins.

Lane's apron was soaked.

“Who told you to drop the gates? The mill race is backed into the timber stacks.”

Harold was working at the skinning bench.

He stopped.

Then looked at Aether.

Aether felt the words return.

Do not drop the gates.

Wait for the crews.

His stomach tightened.

“I did.”

Lane turned.

“What?”

“I told you.”

Lane stared at him.

Aether swallowed.

“I said the wrong thing.”

The damage was not serious.

Several logs had washed into the race, and Lane spent the next hour clearing them with iron levers.

No one was hurt.

Nothing was lost that could not be repaired.

That did not make Aether feel any better.

***

He sat beside the river until evening.

Kaelen found him there.

“You're still here?”

Aether did not look around.

“I gave Lane the wrong message.”

Kaelen sat on a rock nearby.

“So I heard.”

“I told him to drop the gates.”

“Aye.”

“Harold told me not to.”

“Aye.”

Aether waited.

“That's all you're going to say?”

“What do you want me to say?”

“It was the first thing he trusted me to do.”

Kaelen looked at him.

Then he tossed the twig into the river.

“When I was your age, Harold gave me three iron traps.”

Aether waited.

“I lost all three in Blackrock Pool.”

“You never told me that.”

“Why would I?”

Kaelen leaned back on his hands.

“The next winter I dropped three days of salt in the mud when wolves came onto our trail.”

Aether almost smiled.

“Anything else?”

“I nearly burned down the smokehouse.”

Aether looked at him.

Kaelen shrugged.

“Grease pan.”

“You nearly burned down the smokehouse?”

“Nearly.”

“And Harold still lets you hunt?”

“Eventually.”

Aether looked back toward the river.

Kaelen stood.

“You made a bad turn.”

Aether said nothing.

“So turn back.”

Kaelen picked up his bow.

“That's what you do when you lose a trail.”

Then he started toward home.

After several steps, he looked back.

“You coming?”

Aether rose.

***

That night, Harold sat sharpening a knife beside the hearth.

Aether came down from the loft.

“Lane cleared the race.”

Harold continued drawing the blade across the stone.

“I know.”

Aether stood there.

“I told him the wrong thing.”

“Aye.”

“You told me not to drop the gates.”

“Aye.”

Aether waited.

“You aren't angry?”

Harold looked up.

“Would that put the logs back?”

“No.”

“Then no.”

Aether shifted his weight.

“Are you going to stop giving me messages?”

Harold reached beside the hearth and picked up a small piece of charred bark.

He tossed it onto the table.

“Next time, write it down.”

Aether looked at the bark.

“Important words don't belong only in your head.”

Harold returned to the knife.

“Especially when your head is busy with roads, strangers, and whatever else you stare at all day.”

Aether smiled faintly.

Harold glanced at him.

“I've never met a man who carried his first load without spilling some of it.”

Aether picked up the bark.

“Did you?”

Harold's knife stopped.

He looked at Aether.

“Go to bed.”

Aether smiled.

That was answer enough.

***

Upstairs, Aether lit a small candle.

He took out a fresh pine board.

For once, he wrote no names.

No roads.

No rivers.

Instead:

Hear the words.

He paused.

Then beneath it:

Write down what matters.

Aether looked at the two lines.

After a moment, he added one more.

If you make a mistake, correct it.

He blew the charcoal dust from the board.

Then he placed it with the others.
