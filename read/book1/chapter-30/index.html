CHAPTER 30

Who Takes the Road

The next morning, the sky over Stony Creek hung low and bruised. Heavy gray clouds pressed down on the mountain peaks, thick with the promise of rain. A crowd had already gathered in the village square. It wasn't just the locals; representatives from neighboring hamlets and the hunter from Riverbend stood shoulder-to-shoulder near the cold ash of last night's fire. Yesterday’s revelation had shattered any lingering illusions. This crisis was too vast for a single village to bear alone.

Today, a decision had to be made. The village elder stepped into the center.

"We’ve scoured the woods. We’ve shared what we know. Now the question is simple: what do we do next?"

Silence fell over the square. No one rushed to speak. The Riverbend hunter broke the quiet.

"Whitestone City. If there's any place left with answers, it's there."

A few veteran trackers nodded grimly. They had weighed the option all night. The question wasn't *whether* someone should go, but *who*. Involuntarily, most eyes drifted toward Harold. He was the only man here who had walked the paved roads of the high cities, the only one who knew how the world beyond these trees operated. Rane leaned against his spear, looking at Harold.

"If you go, we have the best chance of actually getting something done."

Murmurs of agreement rippled through the crowd. But Harold shook his head.

"I can't leave."

The elder didn't object; he understood all too well. Stony Creek was no longer acting alone; it had become the anchor for half a dozen shattered hamlets. Hunters were sharing patrols, pooling rations, and coordinating watchtowers. That fragile web was held together almost entirely by Harold's authority. If he left, the defense would collapse.

"Then send Rane," someone called out. Rane let out a dry, humorless laugh.

"If I go, who leads the mountain patrols? Who keeps the boys from getting slaughtered in the brush?"

The crowd went quiet again. The other experienced trackers were either nursing old wounds, guarding young families, or holding critical posts. Every capable man was a load-bearing beam in a house ready to cave in. The debate ground to a halt. Aether had been sitting on a supply crate, listening silently. Now, he cleared his throat.

"If we go to Whitestone City... what exactly are we looking for?"

The villagers blinked at the boy. The Riverbend hunter answered, "Information. Answers."

Aether nodded slowly.

"And what if the people who have those answers refuse to talk to us?"

The question hung in the cold air. No one had considered it. They had naively assumed that reaching the city was the hard part—that truth was just sitting there, waiting to be picked up.

"What if they don't believe us?"

Aether continued, his voice steady despite the eyes on him.

"What if they think this is just some backwoods trouble involving a few dirty, paranoid farming villages?"

The silence grew heavier. It was a sharp, uncomfortable point. Whitestone City was weeks away. The high lords and wealthy merchants in their stone towers wouldn't care about a few dead sheep or missing hunters in the fringe territories. The Riverbend hunter let out a soft laugh.

"The kid thinks further ahead than the rest of us put together."

Aether shifted uncomfortably.

"I just mean... even if we find the people who know what's going on, we have to make them listen first."

Rane scratched his beard, a look of realization dawning on his face.

"He's right. Last night, when you showed up, you weren't going to tell us a damn thing about Riverbend at first."

The Riverbend hunter grinned.

"Because the boy kept asking the right questions until I did."

He gestured toward Aether.

"He didn't ask to argue, and he didn't ask to prove he was clever. He asked because he genuinely wanted to know. People open up to that."

The elder gave a slow nod.

"Plenty of men can talk. Very few know how to listen."

Harold remained silent, but his eyes rested on Aether, then shifted to his son, Kaelan. A subtle realization seemed to settle in his gaze: perhaps these boys could navigate a world where broad shoulders and sharp steel meant nothing. By noon, the council reached its verdict. A party had to be sent to Whitestone City to gather intelligence, seek allies, and find out what was poisoning the land. Then came the final question: who goes? The Riverbend hunter was the first to speak.

"Kaelan."

No one argued. Over the past weeks, the boy had proven himself. He could track, hunt, survive in the wild, and—most importantly—he knew how to keep his head when blood was spilled. The hunter then pointed a dirt-caked finger at Aether.

"And him."

Surprise rippled through the crowd.

"The scholar's boy? Why?" someone asked.

"Because he knows how to make people talk," the hunter replied simply.

"And in Whitestone City, the deadliest terrain isn't the mountains. It's the people."

No one raised an objection. The elder stepped forward, his voice carrying over the quiet square.

"By decision of Stony Creek and our allied hamlets: Kaelan and Aether will travel to Whitestone City. You go as our eyes and our voice."

The decree settled over the boys like a heavy wool cloak. Kaelan and Aether stood frozen. Though they had felt the wind blowing in this direction, having the path laid out before them brought a surge of conflicting emotions—fear, anxiety, a knot in the stomach, and beneath it all, a quiet, thrumming thrill. That night, the village settled into a tense quiet. Kaelan sat alone on a bench outside his cottage, watching the black silhouette of the forest against the night sky.

Heavy, familiar footsteps sounded behind him. Harold sat down beside his son. Neither spoke for a long time. Eventually, Harold reached into his tunic and pulled out a small object. It was an old bronze medallion. Its edges were worn smooth, its engraved sigil almost completely rubbed away by years of handling. It had been cared for, kept hidden for a very long time. Kaelan had never seen it before.

"What's this?"

Harold stared down at the metal, his voice quiet.

"A remnant from a lifetime ago."

Kaelan didn't push. He knew his father; if Harold wanted to explain, he would. If he didn't, asking was useless. Harold pressed the cool bronze into Kaelan's palm.

"When you reach Whitestone City, go to the East District. Look for a man named Edwin."

Kaelan closed his fingers around the metal, memorizing the name.

"Will he help us?"

"I don't know,"

Harold said frankly. Kaelan blinked. Harold looked out toward the dark horizon.

"The medallion might be worthless now. Edwin might be dead, or he might close his door in your face."

It was a classic Harold answer— no guarantees, no heroic promises, just the stark, unvarnished truth. Harold turned his head, his dark eyes locking onto his son's.

"But if there's anyone left in that city who will hear you out to the end... it might be him."

The night wind swept through the trees, making the timbers of the village creak. Kaelan looked down at the faded bronze in his hand, the realization finally settling deep in his chest: he was about to step onto a road his father had walked decades ago. And at the end of that road lay a world he had only ever dreamed of.
