CHAPTER 41

Whitestone City

The ground began to vibrate before the sun cleared the horizon.

Outside the gates, the camp slowly came awake. Campfires were put out, tents folded away, and oxen backed into wagon shafts. Merchants, travelers, displaced families, hired guards, and farmers all moved toward the same place, their separate roads narrowing into one long line.

Whitestone City.

Aether had hardly slept. Through the night he had kept looking toward the high wall beyond the camp. Now that morning had come and the gates were finally within reach, the weariness gave way to excitement.

The column crept forward, inches at a time, until the sheer scale of the gatehouse erased the sky.

The wall was built from enormous gray-white stones laid one above another, high enough that Aether had to tip his head back to see the battlements. Up on the high walks, heavy-armored guards paced in silent, rhythmic patrols, their gray cloaks snapping in the dawn wind.

At the threshold, the world dissolved into noise.

Merchants bickered over wheel tolls, mothers hissed at weeping toddlers, and guards shouted for the line to move. When Aether and Kaelan finally reached the iron archway, two city guards stepped into their path, their eyes flat and practiced. They dug through the boys’ linen packs with swift, unfeeling hands, checked their boots for concealed steel, and barked a brief interrogation.

After a brief inspection and a few routine questions, the lead guard waved them through.

"Next."

Aether took a step forward, his boot crossing the threshold into the dark tunnel of the gatehouse—and then out into the blinding light of Whitestone City.

For a moment he forgot to move. People streamed around him as though the gate were nothing more than a doorway they had crossed a hundred times. A woman balanced a basket against one hip while pulling a sleepy child with the other hand. Two porters argued over a handcart without ever stopping. Somewhere above them, a bell rang, and no one even looked up. To Aether, every sound seemed to belong to a different world; to everyone else, it was simply morning.

Kaelan caught his sleeve and drew him out of the flow before a wagon wheel came too close. Aether glanced back at the gate. From outside, the wall had seemed like the end of the road. From inside, it felt more like the beginning of a hundred roads at once.

It wasn't the grandeur that struck him first. It was the noise.

It slammed into him like a physical blow: the iron clang of steel hitting anvils, the thunderous grinding of wooden axles on cobble, the guttural roars of livestock, screeching gulls, street hawkers screaming their prices, and thousands of voices merging into a ceaseless roar. He froze in the middle of the street, completely overwhelmed.

The main avenue was vast—wide enough to swallow four of Stony Creek's dirt tracks side by side. Stone-built manors pressed against timber shops; three-story taverns leaned over sprawling warehouses. And everywhere, there were people. Men in greasy leather hunting coats, mercenaries carrying longbows wrapped in oiled cloth, porters hauling crates, and refugees wearing the dust of distant provinces.

Aether turned in circles, his eyes darting frantically from signboards to balconies, unable to process the scale of it all.

A heavy hand shoved his shoulder from behind. Kaelan passed him, his eyes locked straight ahead. "Keep moving."

Aether stumbled, catching up. "There are... how do this many people even live in one place?"

Kaelan didn't look back, his hand resting near his belt. "Find an inn first."

They navigated the choked avenues, asking three different street vendors before finding a low-ceilinged tavern tucked into a narrow side alley. The room they rented was tiny— containing nothing more than two narrow cots, a scarred pine table, and the stench of old straw. But after weeks of sleeping in the dirt, it was a fortress.

The moment Kaelan dropped his pack, Aether dashed to the small glassless window. Three stories below, the alley thrummed with endless foot traffic. He felt like he could stand there for days just watching the world bleed past.

Below the window, a baker carried trays across the alley while a boy swept yesterday's straw into a heap. A woman leaned from an upper window to call to someone Aether could not see. Nothing about it was remarkable, and that was what held his attention. So many people lived here closely enough to hear one another cough, quarrel, laugh, and begin work each morning. Gray Vale had taught him the shape of almost every life around him. Here, he could watch a street for an hour and still know no one's name.

The thought made the city feel larger than its walls.

"Let's go down," Aether said, his voice tinged with restless energy.

Kaelan was already unbuckling his armguards. "Eat first."

"We can eat out there."

Kaelan paused, looking at him, then let out a small breath. "Fine."

They found a soup stall near the edge of the lower market. The air was a thick, suffocating slurry of smells: fresh rye bread, roasting mutton, raw hides, horse manure, crushed herbs, and exotic spices Aether didn't have names for.

Near the stall's steaming cauldrons, a cluster of traveling merchants sat on overturned casks, slurping greasy broth.

"The northern track is dead," one trader grunted, wiping his beard with the back of his hand. "Dead and gone."

"Another raid?" his partner asked.

"Aye. Lost three outriders before we hit the garrison line. Whatever's coming out of the timber, it isn't scared of steel anymore."

A third man leaned in, lowering his voice. "The garrison sent a squad out three days ago."

"Sent 'em?" The first merchant let out a bitter laugh. "They can send a regiment for all the good it'll do. They're just holding the stone walls now."

Aether slowed his chewing, leaning closer. But before he could hear more, the noise of a nearby argument drowned them out.

"I'm telling you, it wasn't a normal pack!" a young trapper was shouting, slamming his earthenware mug onto a crate. "I saw a wolf larger than a plow-horse! It cleared a six- foot fence without touching the timber!"

"You saw a shadow and pissed yourself, boy," an older man mocked. "Wolves don't grow to the size of horses."

"I know what I saw!"

Further down the street, a group of refugees had gathered around a well. A woman was weeping over the price of flour, while an old man cursed the city authorities, claiming Whitestone had swallowed ten thousand souls since last winter and was running out of grain. Every corner held a different rumor, every voice carrying a fragment of panic, truth, or wild exaggeration.

By late afternoon, the sensory overload had exhausted Aether. They sat on a low stone step off the main thoroughfare, watching the crowd file past.

Yet beneath the rumors, ordinary life kept moving. A cobbler continued fitting a new sole while two customers argued about the northern road beside him. A mother bought onions with one hand and held a sleeping child with the other. A merchant complained about missing wagons, then stopped mid-sentence to haggle over the price of lamp oil. Aether found himself watching those small things as closely as he listened to the stories. Whatever was happening beyond the walls had not stopped life here. It had only begun to press against it.

Aether rubbed his temples, his brow furrowed. "Everyone's talking about the beast tide... but no two people tell the same story."

Kaelan stared at the cobbles between his boots. "They won't."

"Why not? Either a village was burned or it wasn't."

"Because everyone only sees the fire in their own yard," Kaelan said softly.

Aether chewed his lip. "Then how do we know what's real?"

Kaelan didn't answer. He couldn't. They were strangers here, listening to shattered pieces of news dropped by terrified people.

When they climbed the dark stairs back to their room, the city below was still blazing with light. Torches flickering along the high walls, lanterns swinging outside taverns, the dull hum of thousands of voices echoing off the stone.

Aether sat on the edge of his cot, the noise of the market still ringing in his ears. "Which story do you think is true?"

Kaelan didn't reply. Instead, he reached into his tunic, pulled out the heavy brass crest Harold had given them, and set it squarely in the center of the wooden table.

The brass caught the dim candle light.

The noise of the city seemed to recede. Aether looked at the emblem, his racing thoughts suddenly coming to a dead halt. The vastness of Whitestone, the market rumors, the towering walls—it all shrank away, leaving only the cold reality of why they had walked through weeks of mud and fear.

Kaelan stared at the brass crest, his voice dropping into a quiet, absolute register.

"Tomorrow. The East Ward."

Aether nodded, his chest tightening. "We find Edwin."

"Aye."

Outside, Whitestone carried on into the night. Inside the small room, Harold's crest rested quietly between them, reminding both boys why they had come.
