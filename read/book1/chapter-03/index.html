CHAPTER 3

BEYOND THE VALE

The world had not yet woken.

Ashvale lay dark beneath the last hours before dawn. Wind moved between the timber longhouses, slipping through cracks in the walls with a low whistle. Most of the settlement still slept beneath heavy furs.

The hunters were already awake.

Before the first gray light appeared above the eastern peaks, Kaelen had followed Harold beyond the perimeter fence and into the trees.

Their boots left two sets of tracks in the fresh snow—Harold's broad and deep, Kaelen's lighter prints following close behind.

At fourteen winters old, Kaelen carried a short bow across his back and a skinning knife at his belt. No one in Ashvale thought much of it. Children learned early what the valley required of them, and Kaelen had always taken naturally to the forest.

Harold walked ahead without speaking.

Kaelen followed the same way.

In the timber, unnecessary noise meant frightened game and an empty table.

The snow had muffled everything. Pines stood motionless beneath their white burden, and even the wind seemed distant here.

Harold stopped.

One mittened hand rose.

Kaelen froze behind him.

Several tracks crossed the snow near a thicket.

He crouched and studied them.

“Hares,” he whispered.

Harold glanced down. “How many?”

Kaelen followed the marks with one finger.

“Three.”

“Which way?”

Kaelen looked at the bent grass, then toward the thicker trees.

“East.”

Harold nodded once.

“Good.”

That was all the praise Kaelen needed.

He stood and followed his father deeper into the woods.

There was nothing remarkable about any of it to him. He knew the difference between a wolf's print and a stag's. He knew when snow had fallen over a track and when a track had been made after the snow. He knew where rabbits sheltered from the wind and which hollows were best avoided after dark.

He knew because Harold had taught him.

And because, in Ashvale, knowing such things mattered.

***

Aether woke considerably later.

By the time he pushed through the longhouse door, pale sunlight had begun to spill across the valley.

Kaelen was already back.

He stood beside the woodpile with three winter hares hanging from one shoulder, chewing on a piece of flatbread.

Aether stared at him.

“You've already been hunting?”

Kaelen tore off another bite. “Since before dawn.”

“With Harold?”

Kaelen nodded.

He reached inside his coat, pulled out a second piece of bread, and tossed it over.

Aether caught it.

“Thanks.”

“My da wants me to check the deadfalls along the eastern ridge,” Kaelen said. “You coming?”

Aether stopped chewing.

“How far east?”

Kaelen gave him a look.

Aether's eyes narrowed.

“Past the Old Stone Road?”

“Along it.”

That was enough.

“I’m coming.”

Kaelen smiled faintly.

“I thought you might.”

***

They left Ashvale behind and followed the ancient road east.

Their boots struck the exposed stones wherever the wind had swept the snow away. Elsewhere, they walked through a thin white crust that cracked beneath each step.

The farther they went, the quieter the valley became.

The familiar grazing fields disappeared first. Then the last woodcutters' paths vanished beneath untouched snow. Before long, Aether could look back and see nothing of Ashvale at all.

Only trees.

He turned several times anyway.

Kaelen noticed.

“You worried?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Aether looked back once more.

“I've just never been this far.”

“I know.”

They walked on.

After a while Aether said, “Do you come out here often?”

“When I need to.”

“Alone?”

“Sometimes.”

Aether looked at him.

Kaelen glanced over. “What?”

“Nothing.”

“You're doing that thing again.”

“What thing?”

“Thinking too loudly.”

Aether laughed.

Kaelen shook his head and kept walking.

For several minutes the only sound was the crunch of their boots.

Then Aether asked, “Do you ever wonder what's past these woods?”

Kaelen sighed.

Aether grinned. “You knew I was going to ask.”

“I did.”

“And?”

Kaelen stepped over a fallen branch.

“I figure we'll find out if we ever need to go there.”

“That doesn't make you curious?”

“Not enough to walk there for no reason.”

Aether shook his head.

Kaelen glanced sideways at him.

“That is why I brought you.”

Aether frowned. “What do you mean?”

“Because if I hadn't, you'd eventually come out here by yourself.”

“I would not.”

Kaelen looked at him.

Aether hesitated.

“Probably not.”

Kaelen snorted.

“And if you got lost, Harold would come looking for you. Then he'd blame me for knowing you were foolish enough to try.”

“He wouldn't blame you.”

“He absolutely would.”

Aether laughed.

Kaelen did too.

The sound carried briefly through the trees before the forest swallowed it.

***

By midday, the character of the woods had changed.

The pines grew older and broader here, their trunks thick enough that two boys could have hidden behind one without difficulty. Snow lay untouched between them.

And still the Old Stone Road continued.

Straight through the forest.

Aether had begun to wonder whether anything could make it turn when something beside the road caught his eye.

He stopped.

“Kaelen.”

Kaelen turned.

“What?”

Aether pointed through the trees.

Several large stones lay half-buried beneath snow and dead vines.

Not natural stones.

Pillars.

Aether left the road at once.

“Wait—”

But he was already struggling through the drift.

He reached the nearest fallen pillar and swept snow from its surface with both hands.

Lines appeared beneath the frost.

His breath caught.

“Kaelen.”

His brother came up behind him.

Aether brushed harder.

There they were.

Branches.

Rivers.

Stars.

And the same strange angular markings he had found on the Old Stone Road.

“They're the same.”

Kaelen crouched beside him.

Aether traced one of the carvings.

“The people who built the road made these too.”

“Looks that way.”

Aether stared at the broken pillars scattered among the trees.

Some were buried almost completely. Others had cracked cleanly in two. One still leaned at an angle from the frozen ground, as if it had once marked the entrance to something that no longer existed.

“What was this place?”

Kaelen looked around.

“No idea.”

“There could have been a building here.”

“Could have.”

“Or a gate.”

“Could have.”

“Maybe even—”

Kaelen's hand closed suddenly around Aether's shoulder.

“Quiet.”

Something in his voice changed.

Aether stopped.

Kaelen was no longer looking at the ruins.

He was looking beyond them.

At the snow.

Fresh tracks crossed the far side of the clearing.

Aether's excitement disappeared.

The prints were much larger than a hare's.

He looked toward the trees.

“Wolf?”

Kaelen nodded.

“Winter wolf.”

Aether swallowed.

Somewhere inside the thicket, a branch scraped against another.

Kaelen slowly moved between Aether and the sound.

“Stay behind me.”

Aether obeyed.

Kaelen slipped the bow from his shoulder and took an arrow from the small quiver at his back.

“Is it close?”

“Yes.”

“How close?”

Kaelen did not answer.

His eyes moved across the clearing, reading things Aether could not—the tracks, the wind, the dark spaces beneath the trees.

Then the brush shifted.

A gray-white wolf stepped into view.

It was larger than any Aether had seen near the village.

Its coat blended almost completely with the winter ground. Only its eyes stood out, pale and fixed on the boys.

Aether's heart began pounding.

The wolf lowered its head.

Kaelen raised the bow.

The arrow looked very small.

“Kaelen…”

“Don't run.”

Aether went still.

The wolf took one step forward.

Kaelen did not loose the arrow.

Instead, he backed toward one of the fallen pillars, keeping himself between Aether and the animal.

“Pick up that branch.”

Aether reached down without looking and found a length of dead pine.

“Hold it out. Make yourself bigger.”

Aether lifted it.

His hands shook.

The wolf stopped.

For several long seconds, nothing moved.

Then Kaelen stamped hard against the stone and shouted.

“Go!”

Aether shouted too, swinging the branch above his head.

The wolf flinched.

Kaelen shouted again.

The animal hesitated, ears flattening.

Then, as suddenly as it had appeared, it turned and disappeared between the trees.

Neither boy moved.

Aether listened until even the faint sound of paws in snow was gone.

Only then did Kaelen lower the bow.

Aether released a breath.

“You knew it would leave?”

“No.”

Aether stared at him.

Kaelen put the arrow back into his quiver.

“But it was alone.”

“That matters?”

“Usually.”

“Usually?”

Kaelen looked toward the trees.

“If there had been three of them, we'd be having a different conversation.”

Aether tightened his grip on the branch.

Kaelen glanced at him and smiled.

“Come on.”

“You're smiling.”

“We're not dead.”

“That is a very low standard.”

“It has worked well so far.”

Aether laughed weakly.

Kaelen started back toward the road.

This time, Aether stayed close behind him.

***

They reached the eastern ridge near sunset.

The climb was steeper than Aether had expected. By the time he pulled himself over the last rise, his legs ached and his boots were wet at the edges.

Kaelen walked several paces farther, then stopped.

Aether almost ran into him.

“What?”

Kaelen pointed.

Far beyond the ridge, past folds of forest and a line of distant hills, something rose into the evening sky.

Smoke.

A single thin column, barely visible against the darkening blue.

Aether stared.

It was too far away to come from Ashvale.

For a moment he said nothing.

Then, quietly:

“That's a fire.”

“Looks like one.”

“Someone made it.”

“Probably.”

Aether stepped forward until he stood at the edge of the ridge.

Beyond them lay miles of forest he had never walked, hills he did not know the names of, and somewhere among them, people.

Real people.

Not people from one of Garan's old stories.

Not names from a forgotten tale.

People who were alive now.

Cooking.

Working.

Arguing.

Sleeping beneath roofs he had never seen.

“Kaelen.”

“Hm?”

“There really are people out there.”

Kaelen looked at the distant smoke.

Then at Aether.

“Of course there are.”

Aether almost laughed.

For Kaelen, the discovery seemed no more surprising than finding tracks in the snow.

But Aether could not stop looking.

Until that moment, everything beyond Ashvale had been an idea.

Now it had smoke rising from it.

The sun slipped lower behind them.

Kaelen adjusted the hares on his shoulder.

“We should go.”

Aether remained where he was for another moment.

Then he turned and followed.

The distant smoke stayed with him all the way home.
