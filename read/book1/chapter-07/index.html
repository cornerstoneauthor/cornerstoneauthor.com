CHAPTER 10

The Quarrel

← PreviousPart I ContentsNext →

The spring rain brought down more than water.

High in the northern draws, wind had torn several large trees from the riverbank. The melt carried them south until five trunks jammed against the mud near the place where the village stream crossed the Old Stone Road.

In Ashvale, five trees mattered.

They were roof beams.

Fence posts.

Winter fuel.

A larger sheep pen.

By the time Aether and Kaelen reached the riverbank, half the village seemed to be there.

Tom the trapper stood ankle-deep in mud.

Across from him was Lane the miller.

Both were shouting.

“My boys put the first hooks in them!” Tom yelled.

Three iron hooks were buried in the bark of the largest trunk.

Lane pointed toward two of his apprentices standing in the shallows with a thick hemp rope.

“And my boys pulled them out of the main current. Without that rope, your hooks would be halfway to the delta.”

“We saw them first.”

“And we saved them.”

Tom stepped closer.

“So they're ours.”

Lane did the same.

“Try taking them.”

Aether stood beside Kaelen at the edge of the road.

He knew both men.

Tom had eaten at their hearth.

Lane had helped repair Harold's roof after an ice storm.

Neither looked like a thief.

Kaelen folded his arms.

“Tom's right.”

Aether looked over.

“Why?”

“First iron in the timber.”

Kaelen pointed at the hooks.

“That's how it's done.”

One of Lane's sons heard him.

He turned and pointed toward their longhouse farther up the slope.

A corner of the roof had been stripped open. Bare rafters showed beneath the gray sky.

“We need them.”

Kaelen looked at the roof.

Then at the logs.

His certainty faded.

“It's only five trees,” he muttered.

An old tracker beside them turned.

“Five trees is a warm bed in January.”

Kaelen said nothing after that.

The argument grew louder.

Finally someone called:

“Get Harold.”

***

Harold came down from the ridge without a weapon.

He did not shout for silence.

He did not step between Tom and Lane.

He simply stood on a flat stone several paces away.

Then he listened.

When both men had finished, Harold looked at Tom.

“When did you see the timber?”

“Second hour after dawn.”

“Who reached it first?”

“My eldest.”

“Where?”

“Above the bend.”

Harold turned to Lane.

“Where did you set the rope?”

“At the shallows.”

“Before or after the rocks?”

“Before.”

“Who went into the water?”

“My two boys.”

“How deep?”

Lane pointed to his thigh.

Harold kept asking.

Who had seen what.

Who had done what.

Where.

When.

By the time he finished, no one was shouting anymore.

Harold looked at the five trunks.

“Tom takes two.”

Tom opened his mouth.

Harold continued.

“Lane takes two.”

Lane frowned.

“The fifth goes to the timber crew.”

“For what?” Tom asked.

“The footbridge.”

Harold pointed upstream.

“If the May flood takes it, none of you will care who owned these trees.”

Silence.

Tom looked unhappy.

Lane looked unhappy.

Aether noticed that Harold did not seem bothered by either fact.

Tom spat into the mud and waved his sons toward the two trunks.

Lane called his apprentices back into the water.

The crowd began to break apart.

No one cheered.

No one thanked Harold.

The work simply continued.

***

That evening, Aether found Garan carving a piece of cedar beside the fire.

He sat down nearby.

For a while, he watched the old man's knife peel thin curls from the wood.

“They aren't bad men,” Aether said.

Garan did not look up.

“Who?”

“Tom and Lane.”

“No.”

Aether waited.

Garan continued carving.

“Then why were they ready to fight?”

The old man turned the cedar in his hands.

“Tom has known winters when his children slept cold.”

The knife moved again.

“Lane has known winters when rain came through his roof.”

Aether looked into the fire.

“So who was right?”

Garan smiled faintly.

“You were there.”

“I know.”

“Then you tell me.”

Aether frowned.

“Tom found the trees.”

“Aye.”

“Lane's sons pulled them out.”

“Aye.”

“Lane needs the roof.”

“Aye.”

“Tom needs the wood too.”

“Aye.”

Aether became frustrated.

“You're not answering.”

“I know.”

Garan shaved another curl from the cedar.

After a while, he said:

“People usually see most clearly what is closest to their own hearth.”

Aether looked at him.

Garan nodded toward the fire.

“A cold man sees firewood.”

Another stroke of the knife.

“A hungry man sees food.”

He shrugged.

“That doesn't make either of them wicked.”

Aether thought about Harold standing in the mud.

“He gave neither of them what they wanted.”

“No.”

“They were both angry.”

“Aye.”

“Then how did he know it was fair?”

This time Garan stopped carving.

He considered Aether for a moment.

“Who said it was fair?”

Aether stared at him.

Garan returned to the cedar.

“Sometimes you choose what lets people wake tomorrow and keep living beside each other.”

The fire cracked.

Aether said nothing.

***

Later, in the sleeping loft, Aether took out one of his pine boards.

He almost began to write.

Instead, he stopped.

What would he write?

Tom was right.

That was not true.

Lane was right.

That was not true either.

He turned the charcoal between his fingers.

Then he drew a line down the middle of the board.

On one side, he wrote:

Tom — found the timber. First hooks. Needs winter wood.

On the other:

Lane — saved the timber from the current. Needs roof beams.

Below both, after a long pause:

Bridge — everyone needs it.

Aether sat back.

The board did not tell him who was right.

It only showed him why none of them thought they were wrong.

For now, that was enough.

***

The autumn air turned sharp before the leaves changed. Down by the stream, the morning mist pooled thick around the fence posts, hugging the earth before burning off into the pale sky.

Aether was coming back from the river trail with a bundle of dry scrub pine under his arm when he ran into Kaelen near the entry lane. Kaelen had a stack of heavy oak cordwood slung over his shoulder, three times the size of Aether’s haul.

"Where’s that bound?" Aether asked.

Kaelen jerked his chin toward the eastern rise. "Mother Mela."

Aether shifted his bundle. Mela’s small turf-roofed cottage sat alone against the ridge. Her husband had been under the cairn stone twelve winters back, and her knees had turned stiff as dry elm when the cold damp settled in.

"I’ll carry half," Aether said.

"Near enough," Kaelen grunted, not slowing his stride.

They dropped the wood outside her door. The leather hinges creaked before Kaelen could straighten his back. Mela stood in the frame, bent-backed and thin, her white hair tied tight in a scrap of wool. She looked at the fresh oak logs, her brows drawing together until her eyes vanished into deep folds of skin.

"Take it back," she said.

Kaelen blinked. "What?"

"Take it back," she repeated, her voice thin as a dry husk. "I’ve enough to see me through."

Kaelen looked down at the dark, lichen-crusted bark, his hands hanging limp at his sides.

"The frost is coming early this year," Aether blurted out. "It’s better to have it stacked."

"Take it back," she said again. Her fingers gripped the doorframe, her knuckles knobby and white.

She pulled the heavy latch down from inside.

They hauled the bundle back to the main track. Aether kept turning his head, looking back at the small, shut cottage until the brush hid it from view. "Why turn good timber away?"

Kaelen kicked a loose cobble down the path. "Guess she didn't want it."

"It’s oak," Aether said. "Everyone needs oak."

Kaelen shrugged, hitching the strap over his shoulder. "Don't know."

***

By the next week, the whole village had felt the same wall.

When a boy carried a bucket of clean well-water to her porch, she left it on the grass until it went cold, then hauled her own wooden pail down the slope two inches at a time. When the weavers sent their daughters to patch her wicker fence after the gale, she sat on her bench and watched them with a tight, pale mouth until they laid their tools down and left.

In the longhouse, the women spoke over their spindle-wheels.

"She’s turned sour," one muttered.

"Cold as January frost," said another, pulling her thread tight. "Never used to be that way."

Aether listened from the corner where he was oiling harness leather. He rubbed his thumb raw against the stiff cowhide. It didn't make sense. The timber was split; the water was clear; the fence was broken. You fix what is broken.

The storm came later that week, rattling the roof-slates and driving rain through the timber seams all night.

When the sun broke through the grey clouds the next morning, Aether saw that the lean-to behind Mela’s cottage had given way. The thatch was soaked, collapsed over a heap of dark, dripping pine.

He ran back to the yard, grabbed his mallet and a handful of iron nails, and pulled Kaelen along by the sleeve.

They slogged through the mud to the cottage. Aether banged his fist against the oak door. "Mother Mela! The lean-to’s down! We brought cedar shingles!"

The cottage remained silent. Then came the thin, dry rasp from behind the timber.

"No need."

Aether’s hands froze on the mallet handle. "The rain’s coming back by dusk—"

"No need."

The latch didn't move.

On the river path, Aether smashed his heel into a mound of wet clay. "Why? It’s just shingles! We’re standing right there with the tools!"

Kaelen carried his adze over his shoulder, his boots making heavy shluck-shluck sounds in the mire. He stared down at the gray river water. "Don't ask me."

That afternoon, Harold was at the wood-splitting log near the timber gate. His heavy axe went thwack-crack, sending clean pine wedges flying into the turf.

Aether sat on a stump, his chin resting in his hands, and told him about the lean-to.

Harold didn't stop. He set another log upright, drove the bit deep into the heartwood, and split it with a single, heavy grunt.

"Help me load the handcart tonight," Harold said, wiping his brow with the back of his forearm.

"For Mela?" Aether asked.

Harold nodded.

"She won't take it."

Harold set another pine log on the block. "That’s her business."

***

The valley was dark when they pushed the two-wheeled cart up the ridge. The wind blew cold through the dead bracken, smelling of wet earth and river mud.

They stopped five paces from Mela’s porch. They didn't knock. They didn't call out.

Harold unslung the logs two by two, stacking them in neat, even courses beneath the overhang of her front eave, clear of the roof-drip. When the cart was empty, he wiped his gloves on his thighs and turned back down the track.

Aether hurried to catch up, his breath coming in white puffs. "Why stack it if she’s going to tell us to take it away?"

Harold walked with long, slow strides, his heavy cloak flapping against his calves. He didn't answer until they reached the ford.

"She’s not turning down the wood," Harold said, his voice low against the rush of the water.

Aether looked up at his father’s gray profile. "Then what?"

"She’s turning down the debt."

Harold kept walking into the dark. "A person can freeze. But they don't want to live on another man's ledger."

***

Three days later, the sun was bright and brittle.

Aether was carrying a bundle of dry rushes past the cottage when Mela called to him. She sat on a low wooden bench by her door, an old woolen cloak spread across her knees. Her hands were dark and knotted like oak roots as her bone needle pulled a gray thread through the frayed wool.

"The oak under the eave," she said, her voice dry as dead leaves. "Tell Harold it was well-dried."

Aether stopped, shifting the rushes against his hip. He looked at her small, trembling thumb pushing the bone needle through the thick cloth.

"Mother Mela," he said.

"Aye?"

"Why do you keep telling everyone 'no need'?"

The needle stayed buried in the wool. Mela looked out across the valley toward the timbered ridges, where yellow larch had begun to break the dark green of the pines.

"Because I’m old, lad," she whispered.

Aether didn't move.

She turned her hands over, staring at the thick, scarred palms that had washed linen, held sick children, and hauled sheep out of snowdrifts for sixty winters.

"When I was young, I carried the water," she said, her chest giving a small, brittle shake. "My husband helped build the stone bridge at the lower crossing. We gave what was ours to give."

She pulled the needle through, her knuckles cracking in the cold air.

"Now my legs are gone. And every time a boy brings a bucket or a man drops a log, they look at me like I’m a broken fence post." She pulled the wool tight around her shoulders. "I know it’s kindness. But I sit in that dark house, and I get terrified."

Aether’s voice was very small. "Terrified of what?"

Mela looked down at the frayed hem of her skirt. "Of the day I’m nothing more than a mouth taking from the pile."

***

The fire pit in Garan’s hut was down to red coals when Aether walked in.

The old tracker was crouched on his low stool, threading a piece of oiled gut through a split net-frame.

Aether sat on the dirt floor, watching the smoke curl toward the roof-hole. "She just doesn't want to be a burden."

Garan didn't look up from his knot. "Young men spend their whole lives terrified that nobody needs them."

He pulled the gut line taut with his yellowed teeth, biting it off clean.

"Old ones," Garan rasped, setting the net aside, "are terrified that the only thing left for them to do... is need."

The wind rattled the birch door. Outside, the valley lay dark and wide under the autumn stars, cold stone and deep timber stretching away into the silent night.
