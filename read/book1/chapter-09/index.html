CHAPTER 9

THE TRUST

Spring runoff filled the valley stream.

Wild clover pushed through the joints of the Old Stone Road, pale against the weathered masonry.

Aether was twelve now.

His legs had lengthened. His shoulders had begun to lose the softness of childhood. In the loft, his collection of pine boards had grown into stacks filled with river bends, settlement names, livestock counts, and the names of people who had died.

Kaelen was fifteen.

His hands were marked by rope burns and bowstrings, and when the hunting horn sounded, he no longer waited to be invited.

One gray morning, Harold stood in the yard checking the straps on his pack.

He and Kaelen were preparing for three days in the northern timber.

Aether carried out two waterskins.

Harold reached into the tool crate and pulled out a flat sharpening stone.

“Take this to the mill.”

Aether looked at him.

“To Lane?”

“Aye.”

Harold placed the stone in his hand.

“Tell him the mountain melt is heavy. The timber crews are coming down early to clear the piers.”

Aether nodded.

“He is not to drop the wheel gates until they arrive.”

“Don't drop the gates. Wait for the crews.”

Harold nodded.

“Good.”

Kaelen passed on his way toward the gate and tapped the sharpening stone with the end of an arrow.

“Don't drop that either.”

Aether rolled his eyes.

Kaelen grinned and followed Harold out of Ashvale.

Aether looked down at the stone in his hands.

It was a simple errand.

Still, Harold had given it to him.

***

The river trail was loud with runoff.

Aether followed the Old Stone Road toward the mill.

Near one of the old mile markers, he noticed a fresh line cut into the base of the stone.

He stopped.

Only for a moment.

He crouched, studied the mark, then continued.

Farther down the road, an old net-maker from Riverbend was resting beside a well.

Aether knew him from the Spring Fair.

The man recognized him too.

They talked.

A shipwright had opened a new yard near the delta.

Three timber barges were trapped upstream by the high water.

The spring current had shifted near one of the southern crossings.

Aether listened.

He repeated the details to himself as he walked.

By the time he reached the mill, the water wheel was roaring.

Lane stood on the wet footing with an oak mallet in his hand.

Aether held out the sharpening stone.

“Harold sent this.”

Lane took it.

“He said the mountain melt is heavy. The timber crews are coming early.”

Lane nodded.

Aether looked toward the wheel.

“And you're to prepare to drop the gates.”

Lane tossed the stone into his toolbox.

“Understood.”

Aether headed home.

***

The next afternoon, Lane came up the path shouting.

“Harold!”

Aether looked up from beside the grain bins.

Lane's apron was soaked.

“Who told you to drop the gates? The mill race is backed into the timber stacks.”

Harold was working at the skinning bench.

He stopped.

Then looked at Aether.

Aether felt the words return.

Do not drop the gates.

Wait for the crews.

His stomach tightened.

“I did.”

Lane turned.

“What?”

“I told you.”

Lane stared at him.

Aether swallowed.

“I said the wrong thing.”

The damage was not serious.

Several logs had washed into the race, and Lane spent the next hour clearing them with iron levers.

No one was hurt.

Nothing was lost that could not be repaired.

That did not make Aether feel any better.

***

He sat beside the river until evening.

Kaelen found him there.

“You're still here?”

Aether did not look around.

“I gave Lane the wrong message.”

Kaelen sat on a rock nearby.

“So I heard.”

“I told him to drop the gates.”

“Aye.”

“Harold told me not to.”

“Aye.”

Aether waited.

“That's all you're going to say?”

“What do you want me to say?”

“It was the first thing he trusted me to do.”

Kaelen looked at him.

Then he tossed the twig into the river.

“When I was your age, Harold gave me three iron traps.”

Aether waited.

“I lost all three in Blackrock Pool.”

“You never told me that.”

“Why would I?”

Kaelen leaned back on his hands.

“The next winter I dropped three days of salt in the mud when wolves came onto our trail.”

Aether almost smiled.

“Anything else?”

“I nearly burned down the smokehouse.”

Aether looked at him.

Kaelen shrugged.

“Grease pan.”

“You nearly burned down the smokehouse?”

“Nearly.”

“And Harold still lets you hunt?”

“Eventually.”

Aether looked back toward the river.

Kaelen stood.

“You made a bad turn.”

Aether said nothing.

“So turn back.”

Kaelen picked up his bow.

“That's what you do when you lose a trail.”

Then he started toward home.

After several steps, he looked back.

“You coming?”

Aether rose.

***

That night, Harold sat sharpening a knife beside the hearth.

Aether came down from the loft.

“Lane cleared the race.”

Harold continued drawing the blade across the stone.

“I know.”

Aether stood there.

“I told him the wrong thing.”

“Aye.”

“You told me not to drop the gates.”

“Aye.”

Aether waited.

“You aren't angry?”

Harold looked up.

“Would that put the logs back?”

“No.”

“Then no.”

Aether shifted his weight.

“Are you going to stop giving me messages?”

Harold reached beside the hearth and picked up a small piece of charred bark.

He tossed it onto the table.

“Next time, write it down.”

Aether looked at the bark.

“Important words don't belong only in your head.”

Harold returned to the knife.

“Especially when your head is busy with roads, strangers, and whatever else you stare at all day.”

Aether smiled faintly.

Harold glanced at him.

“I've never met a man who carried his first load without spilling some of it.”

Aether picked up the bark.

“Did you?”

Harold's knife stopped.

He looked at Aether.

“Go to bed.”

Aether smiled.

That was answer enough.

***

Upstairs, Aether lit a small candle.

He took out a fresh pine board.

For once, he wrote no names.

No roads.

No rivers.

Instead:

Hear the words.

He paused.

Then beneath it:

Write down what matters.

Aether looked at the two lines.

After a moment, he added one more.

If you make a mistake, correct it.

He blew the charcoal dust from the board.

Then he placed it with the others.
