CHAPTER 13

The Unopened Door

Late autumn settled over the vale, turning the slopes a dry, rustling gold that rippled under the wind like sunlit water. The grain was in, the threshing was done, and the winter frosts had not yet hard-set the roads. It was a brief breathing space for the longhouses, or at least it seemed so from the lane.

Aether was hoisting forty-pound sacks of barley inside the mill when the shouting started outside.

It was not a roar, but a sharp, grating sound, like two flat river stones grinding against each other in the mud.

"I told you it wasn't on purpose!" a man called out.

"Not on purpose doesn't mend the timber, does it?" came the answer, tight and bitter.

Aether set his flour sack down and stepped into the doorway. A small circle had already formed near the water-gate.

Old Matthew stood in the center, his face dark as cold iron. At his feet lay a heavy handcart with its left axle sheared clean at the hub. Opposite him stood a younger wood-cutter, holding a pair of hemp ropes and looking half-guilty, half-sullen.

"I only borrowed it for the ridge haul," the cutter said, gesturing toward the broken axle.

"Borrowed?" Matthew let out a dry, barking laugh. "You borrow it, you break it, and you drag it back for me to mend. Next season you'll be borrowing my longhouse floor."

The crowd shifted uncomfortably. The wood-cutter’s knuckles tightened on his ropes. "I said I'd haul a fresh ash branch to replace the spindle!"

"Replace it?" Matthew’s voice went up an octave, hard enough to slice through the cold air. "If you knew how to shape a spindle, the cart wouldn't be sitting in the mud with its gut torn out!"

The air around them went dead silent. The miller finally shoved through the circle, taking the cutter by the elbow and steering him down the river track, leaving Matthew alone with the wreckage.

Aether watched from the mill step as Matthew gripped the tongue of the ruined cart and dragged it up the incline himself. Every step he took dug his boots three inches into the earth, his broad back rigid as an oak post.

An old weaver standing nearby shook his head. "There he goes again."

"Never used to be that way," another grumbled, turning back toward his loom.

Aether took two steps forward. "Used to be?"

The weaver paused, glanced at Aether’s charcoal-stained tunic, and gave a vague flick of his wrist. "Long before your time, lad." He walked off without another word.

Aether remained by the door. His mind drifted back to Rowan sitting on his oak bench. Before, he would have let the weaver’s comment drift past like river foam. But now, the phrase lingered—a piece of broken slate lying loose in the grass.

Over the next three days, he picked up other fragments scattered around the longhouses.

Matthew’s temper’s gotten sour as spoiled milk. Been that way five winters now. Used to be the first man to lend a haul-line.

Everyone had a piece of the story, but none of them held the full grain.

By the end of the week, Aether was working with Kaelen down by the river bend, driving split pine stakes to mend a section of wattle fence scoured out by the last high water.

Aether held the stake straight while Kaelen brought the heavy wooden beetle down— thwack, thwack.

"Why do you think Matthew’s always burning inside?" Aether asked between blows.

Kaelen wiped sweat from his brow with his sleeve. "Because he’s got a foul mind."

"People say he didn't used to."

Kaelen lifted the mallet again. "Then he used to have a good one."

Aether glared at him. Kaelen let out a short, quiet grunt that might have been a grin. "You've taken to measuring everyone's chest lately."

"I'm not measuring," Aether said, shifting his grip on the pine post. "I just want to know why."

Kaelen set the mallet head down in the mud, leaning his weight on the long ash handle. "And what happens when you know?"

Aether opened his mouth, but the words caught in his throat. He looked at the gray river water curling around the wattles before speaking. "Maybe... maybe a man can help."

Kaelen stared at the current. "Maybe," he muttered, though he sounded like he was talking to the timber.

Over the next week, Aether kept his eyes on Matthew’s yard.

The old man never sat still. He patched his thatch; he split cordwood; he hauled gravel to the lane; he reshod his own sled runners. He worked with a dull, ceaseless momentum, yet his face carried a gray, heavy shadow—the look of a man who hadn't slept straight through a night in years.

Once, late in the afternoon, Aether saw him sitting alone on a log pile behind his barn. He wasn't working. He wasn't looking at the mountains. He just sat, his hands dropped between his knees, his broad shoulders hunched so low he looked smaller than the timber around him.

For a moment, Aether wondered if what he was seeing wasn't anger at all, but exhaustion—a weight carried so long it had hardened under the ribs.

Maybe he needs a hand with the heavy load, Aether thought.

Before dawn the next morning, while the grass was still stiff with frost, Aether ran to Matthew’s side yard. A massive pile of uncut ash logs lay heaped beside the shed—hard, knotty wood that took three blows of a wedge to split.

Aether picked up the heavy splitting maul resting against the log block. He swung. The steel bit deep.

By the time the sun cleared the eastern crag, a neat quarter-cord of split ash was stacked straight against the barn wall. Sweat was dripping off Aether’s chin, sticking his linen shirt to his shoulder blades.

The wooden gate creaked. Matthew walked into the yard carrying an iron pot of tallow.

He stopped dead in his tracks. He looked at the freshly stacked wood, then at the maul in Aether’s hands, then at Aether’s red, flushed face.

He didn't speak for a long time.

Aether wiped his forehead with his wrist, offering a small, tired grin. "Thought I’d give you a head start on the ash."

The silence in the yard grew thick and heavy, cold as the frost on the grass.

Matthew’s brow pulled down until his eyes went pitch dark. "Who told you to touch my wood?"

Aether’s grin faded. "I... nobody. I just—"

"Who told you to touch it?" Matthew took a heavy step forward, his boot crushing a wood chip with a loud snap.

Aether backed up an inch, his hands still gripping the maul handle. "I saw you were tired lately. I wanted to help."

"I don't need your help, lad!" Matthew shouted, his voice cracking like a dry branch. "Mind your own hearth!"

Aether stood frozen. "I was only trying to save you the labor—"

"Mind your own hearth!" Matthew barked again. He snatched the heavy maul out of Aether’s hands, slammed it head-first into the chopping block, and turned his back.

He walked into his longhouse, his heavy timber door slamming shut behind him with a crash that rattled the iron hinges.

Aether stood alone in the frost-covered mud, his palms still burning from the ash handle.

***

That evening, Harold was sitting under the timber lean-to, replacing an iron rim on an old cart wheel. He drove the cold rivets home with steady, measured strokes of his hammer.

Aether sat on a stool in the dark corner, his knees pulled up, his thumb picking at a callus on his palm. He told Harold about the ash logs, the maul, and the slammed door.

"I was just trying to help him," Aether said, his voice dropping low.

Harold didn't stop hammering. Clang. Clang. Clang.

He set the hammer down on the anvil block, picked up an oiled rag, and wiped the black scale from the iron rim.

"He knows," Harold said flatly.

Aether raised his head. "If he knows, why did he get mad? Why throw it back in my face?"

Harold stared out toward the dark valley, where the first longhouse hearth-smokes were rising into the cold twilight.

"Because he knows," Harold repeated softly.

Aether stared at his father's broad, quiet face, completely lost.

***

Two nights later, Aether walked up the ridge path to Garan’s small turf hut.

The old tracker sat outside, his hands moving over an old net-frame, knotting dried sinew with a small bone needle. The orange light of the sunset caught the silver hair on his forearms.

Aether sat down on the dry moss nearby. He didn't know how to start, but eventually, the whole story came out again—the cart wheel, the split ash, the slammed oak door, and Harold’s words that made no sense.

"I just wanted to lift some of the weight," Aether finished, staring at the dirt.

Garan didn't look up from his bone needle. He pulled a sinew knot tight with a sharp tink.

"You ever knock on a longhouse door late at night, lad?" Garan asked, his voice rasping.

Aether blinked. "Aye."

"Sometimes a man's inside," Garan said, his milky eyes watching his needle move. "Sometimes he's asleep. And sometimes..."

He paused, resting his withered hands on his knees.

"...sometimes he's sitting right by the hearth, watching the latch move. And he stays right there, quiet as a stone, because he isn't ready to open it."

The wind sighed through the larch trees behind the hut, scattering a handful of dry needles across the turf.

"Some doors aren't locked because you lack the strength to push them," Garan whispered. "They're locked because the man inside isn't ready to let the cold air in."

Garan tied off his final knot and laid the bone needle aside.

Aether sat on the moss as twilight swallowed the lower river path. Down in Ashvale, doors were closing one by one against the night.

***

Ask anyone in Ashvale about Thomas’s chief affliction, and they’d give you a small, dry smile before telling you the same thing: "Ask him tomorrow."

Mend the birch fence? Tomorrow. Haul the winter turf? Tomorrow. Buy salt at the lower ford? Tomorrow.

It was an old joke in the vale. Men said that if the high crag ever split and came sliding down into the longhouses, Thomas would stand in his doorway, look at the sky, and say the mountain could wait until morning.

Yet Thomas was no sluggard. When his boots were in the mud, he pulled as hard as any man. He planted his wheat, he hauled his share of timber, and his iron hook was always ready when a neighbor’s ox stuck in the swamp. But he lived under the quiet delusion that time was a well without a bottom.

Until the first winter rain hit the upper ridge.

It began one afternoon. Aether was looking for Kaelen near the timber yard, but found the shed empty.

"Up on the high slope," a weaver told him. "Working the drainage ditch."

Aether took the upper goat path. Halfway up the rise, he found Kaelen crouched in a deep mud trench, his trousers soaked to the knees, driving a flat spade into the rocky clay. Thomas stood on the turf bank above him, holding a bundle of hazel stakes, looking vaguely apologetic.

"You took your time getting here," Kaelen grunted, not looking up as his spade hit stone.

Thomas scratched his bearded chin. "Had the sheep to fold down at the lower pen. Took longer than I thought."

Kaelen didn't answer. He drove the steel down again—shluck.

Aether stepped over the clay heap. "What happened here?"

"Ditch is choked with mountain slate and dead brush," Kaelen said, wiping mud off his cheek with his shoulder. "If the rain catches it like this, the overflow runs straight down into the lower barley terraces and the winter wood-heaps."

"Then let's clear it," Aether said, reaching for a spare wooden shovel.

Kaelen stopped his blade in the dirt. "I told him three months back this ditch needed clearing before the autumn storm."

Thomas shifted his hazel stakes from one arm to the other. "I was going to get to it right after the grain haul. Then the mill axle broke, and then..." He gave a brief, easy shrug. "Well, there was time enough."

"Aye," Kaelen muttered, bending back over his spade. "There's always tomorrow."

The tone in Kaelen's voice wasn't loud, but it hung in the cold air like frozen mist.

They worked until dusk, clearing the lower turn and dragging brush from the channel, but the upper bend was still packed with slate when the light failed. Thomas promised he would finish it at first light.

Morning came, and so did a lame ewe, a broken pen rail, and a dozen smaller things that seemed urgent enough. Thomas never made it back to the upper bend.

The storm hit two nights later.

It was a black, driving rain that rattled the roof-slates and turned the longhouse lanes into roaring gutters. Aether woke twice to the sound of timber creaking against the gale.

By dawn, the shouting started on the upper rise.

The choked ditch had given way under the weight of the mountain run-off. A wall of gray, gravel-laden water had burst the bank, carving a two-foot trench straight through the lower field and flooding the main timber yard. Half a winter's worth of dry cordwood was sitting in three feet of freezing silt, soaked through to the core.

When Aether reached the slope, half the village was already working in the rain. Men were hauling logs out of the muck, women were clearing the stone debris, and boys were digging a new channel with wooden spades.

Kaelen was there, caked in black mud from his thighs to his hair.

Thomas was beside him, his face pale, frantically tossing wet logs onto the dry bank. "I'm sorry," Thomas kept saying to no one in particular. "I was going to clear the upper turn yesterday evening—"

Kaelen dropped his spade. The wooden handle hit the mud with a dull thwack.

"You said that three months ago," Kaelen said.

The rain had stopped, leaving only the sound of water rushing down the stone channel. The men on the bank went quiet.

Thomas stood with a wet log in his arms, his mouth opening, then closing.

Aether had never heard Kaelen speak like that. Kaelen rarely lost his temper; he was as steady and dull as a slate paving stone. But these words were heavier than a shout.

"I know," Thomas said softly, looking down at his boots.

"No, you don't," Kaelen said, his voice flat as river ice. "If you knew, the ditch would have been dug before the leaves turned."

Kaelen picked up his spade, turned his back, and shoved the blade back into the clay. He didn't look at Thomas again for the rest of the day.

***

By dusk, the water had been turned back into the main stream, and the flooded wood was stacked to dry in the wind. The villagers drifted back to their hearths, leaving Kaelen alone on the slope, scraping the thick, dried clay off his shovel with a flat stone.

Aether walked up the rise and stood beside him. The valley below was turning dark, the first hearth-fires showing bright against the gray turf roofs.

"You were hard on him today," Aether said quietly.

Kaelen didn't stop scraping. Scratch. Scratch. "Aye."

"Was it worth making an enemy over a choked ditch?"

Kaelen set the stone down and looked out over the valley, his gray eyes cold in the twilight. "I'm not angry at him."

Aether blinked. "Then what was that?"

Kaelen wiped his hands on his trousers, his shoulders sloping slightly as he looked at the freshly dug trench.

"I just thought..." Kaelen whispered, his voice almost lost in the evening wind, "...I thought he was a man you could count on when he gave his word."

Aether stayed quiet. Below them, Thomas was still hauling wet cordwood from the mud, one log at a time.

Kaelen picked up his tool cart and started down the track toward the longhouses, his heavy boots making slow, rhythmic crunching sounds in the gravel.

Aether looked back at the dark channel on the hillside.
