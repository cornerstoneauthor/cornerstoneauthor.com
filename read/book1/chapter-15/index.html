CHAPTER 15

THE ROAD BEYOND THE VALE

Years passed after the flooding of Thomas's wood-yard. Winters came down from the crags and loosened their grip again; by the time the latest cold finally receded, Ashvale had shifted its skin more than once.

Snow had buried the vale and melted away; spring floods had turned the lanes to mud; harvests had risen gold and vanished beneath the sickle. The changes came so slowly that Aether rarely noticed them until something familiar suddenly felt smaller than he remembered.

The great oak by the river—the one sheared in two by the midwinter ice-gale—was gone now, leaving only a low, mossy stump near the ford. Green shoots were pushing through the rotten bark at its base. Unless a man knew where to look, he’d never guess a fifty-foot crown had once shaded that bank.

Thomas spent the early spring mending his boundary fence. He worked every day from dawn till the sun dropped behind the crags. When passersby called out from the lane, asking if he'd finish by tomorrow, Thomas would just offer a brief, quiet nod and keep driving his wooden pegs. He didn't laugh anymore, but the fence stood straight and true.

Matthew still carried his sour face, but twice that month he stopped his handcart to help a boy pull a stuck calf from the drainage ditch before walking off without a word.

Rowan spent the sunny afternoons on the mill bench, spinning grand yarns about winter blizzards that had buried longhouses to the chimney-tops. Everyone knew the snow had barely reached the eave-line, but they sat on the turf wall and listened anyway, because his loud, rolling voice made the lingering chill feel a little lighter.

Harold’s beard had turned gray at the temples over the dark months, though his arm stayed heavy on the anvil.

One afternoon, Harold called Aether and Kaelen over to the timber frame behind the forge to help him carry an old oak ridge-beam. Aether bent his knees, braced his core, and prepared to heave with every ounce of strength he possessed.

The beam lifted cleanly off the grass.

Aether blinked, looking down at his hands. "Did the wood dry out?"

Kaelen let out a short grunt from the other end. "Wood's the same weight it was last autumn."

Aether looked at his fingers, broad and rough now, stained with timber grease and charcoal. He smiled. He wasn't a boy reaching for his father's belt anymore.

Kaelen still carried the heavier build of the older boy, but Aether no longer looked like a child beside him. At fifteen, his shoulders had broadened, his hands had hardened, and the sleeves of last winter's tunic had crept halfway up his forearms.

Garan had watched them haul water up from the ford one evening, squinting through his milky eyes before shaking his head. "Grass grows fast when the rain turns warm," the old tracker had muttered.

***

Then came the news about Ean.

No one remembered who brought it first, but by the next morning, the word had run through every longhouse, every field, and every corner of the mill.

Ean was leaving.

He was nineteen, four years older than Aether, with broad wrists and a quiet way of working. He’d helped mend half the roofs in Ashvale, hauled turf for the widows, and cleared timber after every storm. Nobody disliked him, and nobody had ever imagined him anywhere else.

When Rowan heard the news, he hailed Aether from the mill bench. "Have I ever told you?" Rowan shouted, waving a crust of bread. "I left Ashvale once when I was young!"

"Six days!" Aether called back over his shoulder without slowing down.

The men by the well broke into a roar of laughter. Rowan waved his fist in mock outrage. "Seven! It was seven days, you sharp-tongued terrier!"

The laughter died away, but the talk remained.

"Why go?" Aether asked Kaelen as they stacked slate near the quarry wall. "What's wrong with the vale?"

Nobody had a clear answer. Some said Ean was bound for one of the southern cities; others said he was joining a merchant train to the coast.

That evening, the answer came from Ean himself. He sat on the low stone wall outside the mill, surrounded by half the village. Rowan didn't tell stories; Thomas didn't talk about tomorrow. Everyone just watched the young man scrape the mud off his boot heel with a twig.

"There's no grand reason," Ean said, looking up at the high, jagged crags that ringed the northern sky. "I just want to see what the world looks like on the other side of those ridge-pines."

The yard went still.

The spring wind rustled through the larch needles overhead. Aether felt something stir in his chest—a strange, quiet ache he had never felt before. He had looked at those northern crags his entire life, but he had never permitted himself to ask what lay beyond them.

Now, someone had spoken the question out loud.

A pace back from the crowd, Harold stood leaning against the wood-shed wall, an old leather traveling pack resting on his knee. He wasn't talking. He was pulling a heavy waxed thread through a split seam in the leather, his bone awl going punch-punch through the thick hide.

Aether looked at the pack. It wasn't Harold’s size; it was smaller, built for a younger man's shoulders.

***

The night before Ean was set to ride out with a passing wool-wagon, Aether woke past midnight.

A thin line of yellow lamp-light spilled from the timber shed into the dark yard. Aether pulled his woolen cloak over his shoulders and stepped softly across the cold gravel.

Harold sat on a low stool inside, an old iron dagger lying across his knee. The blade was worn thin from thirty years of stone-grinding, its horn hilt polished smooth by grease and sweat.

Shhhk... shhhk...

The blade moved across the wet oil-stone in slow, circular sweeps.

Aether sat down on an empty turf-crate. "For Ean?"

Harold nodded, not breaking his rhythm.

"You brought that knife back from the southern marches, didn't you?" Aether asked.

"Aye."

Aether watched the dull reflection of the oil lamp on the wet steel. "Do you want him to go?"

The oil-stone stopped. The shed went dead quiet, save for the wind picking at the thatch eaves.

Harold looked down at the thin steel blade in his calloused palm. "No," he said softly.

Aether stared. It was rare for Harold to state his mind so plainly. "Then why spend three nights stitching his pack and grinding your best iron for him?"

Harold set the stone down, his broad thumb running over the freshly honed edge.

"Because it’s his road," Harold said, his voice deep and gravelly. "A man can't walk another man's trail for him. Even if you know he's going to hit the gravel and bleed... he still has to walk it."

He lifted the dagger, slid it into a worn leather sheath, and pulled the strap tight.

***

The sun had not yet cleared the eastern ridges when the village gathered at the stone arch where the river track met the Old Stone Road.

Nobody had called a meeting, but they were all there. Rowan stood by the well-coping; Thomas leaned on his wooden spade; Matthew stood at the back, his arms crossed over his chest; even the old miller had left his wheel idling in the race.

Ean stood beside the wool-wagon, the oiled leather pack strapped tight across his chest, the horn-handled dagger resting against his hip. He looked small against the vast, open valley, his face a mixture of terror and bright, fierce light.

Rowan stepped forward, clapping a broad hand onto Ean’s shoulder. "Have I ever told you about the market town at the south ford?"

"You have," Ean said, a small grin breaking across his pale face. "Four times."

"Good," Rowan barked, stepping back with a hearty grin. "Don't go forgetting it."

The villagers offered quiet nods, their hands tucked into their woolen sleeves.

Ean climbed onto the wagon box, taking the wooden reins from the teamster. He looked back at the turf roofs, the timber stacks, the stone bridge, and the faces he had known since his first steps in the mud.

"I’ll be back," Ean said, his voice cracking slightly before he straightened his posture. "I’ll bring back stories grander than Rowan's."

Thomas offered a firm nod. "Aye. We'll be here."

Matthew let out a gruff snort from the back. "Try not to freeze in a ditch, lad."

Ean laughed, his eyes finally settling on Harold.

Harold didn't make a speech. He stepped up to the wheel, reached out his massive, scarred hand, and gave Ean’s shoulder a single, firm squeeze. Ean nodded, his jaw tightening as he gripped the reins.

The teamster cracked his whip over the horses' ears. The heavy iron wheels turned, grinding into the wet gravel with a dry, heavy crunch.

The wagon moved down the Old Stone Road, shrinking with every heartbeat—past the willow bend, past the lower ford, past the boundary marker—until it was nothing more than a dark speck against the vast, pale horizon where the forest met the sky.

Then it vanished into the timber.

The crowd stood silent for a long moment before slowly turning back toward their longhouses, their boots crunching on the gravel as the daily work called them home.

Soon, only Aether and Harold remained standing at the stone arch, the morning wind blowing cold through their cloaks.

Aether stared down the empty road, watching the dust settle back into the ruts.

"Do you think he'll ever come back?" Aether asked quietly.

Harold didn't answer right away. His gray eyes stayed fixed on the distant notch in the mountain ridge where the road vanished into the wild beyond.

"I hope he does," Harold said softly.

Aether looked up at his father's weathered face, then back toward the notch in the ridge.

The Old Stone Road lay empty again, cutting through the timber toward the world beyond the vale. Aether kept looking long after Ean was gone.
