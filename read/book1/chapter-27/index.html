CHAPTER 27

The Old Camp

The next morning brought a rare moment of peace to Stony Creek. No alarms. No wolf howls. No one jolting awake from a nightmare. Yet the village remained tense. Men still patrolled the perimeter fence, spears leaned against doorways, and even the children playing in the dirt kept glancing nervously toward the treeline. Everyone was waiting for something. No one said it aloud. Kaelan stood at the village entrance, staring into the treeline.

His mind was still tangled in yesterday's trek—the chaotic pawprints, the unexplainable signs, and Harold's final words. *We haven't seen enough yet.* He didn't know what it meant, but he couldn't stop thinking about it. Footsteps sounded behind him. Rane approached carrying a wooden bucket. Seeing Kaelan standing there, he offered a faint smile.

"Sleep well?"

Kaelan shook his head.

"I slept. But I couldn't stop thinking about those tracks."

Rane's smile faded. Neither could he. They stood in silence for a moment before Kaelan spoke up.

"What was the old hunting camp like... before?"

Rane blinked, caught off guard. He set the bucket down.

"Want to go see it again?"

Kaelan looked up.

"Now?"

"Now,"

Rane said.

"Nothing else pressing today anyway."

Kaelan nodded, and the two walked out of the village together. On the other side of the village, Aether was helping the locals repair the palisade. There had been no attack last night, but the damage from previous days was severe. Timber needed hauling, ropes needed replacing, and several shattered posts had to be dug out and replanted. Aether had never done manual labor like this before. Back in Gray Valley, he spent his days thinking, asking questions—not wrestling with timber and mud.

But here in Stony Creek, the work seemed endless. Solve one problem, and another took its place. Nobody stopped just because they were tired. Stopping didn't make the problems go away. While he was hauling a log, the village elder walked over, cradling a stack of wooden tally tablets. Aether cast a curious glance. They were covered in finely carved numbers.

"What are those?"

"Loss records," the elder replied.

"How many sheep we lost. How many fence posts broke. How much timber we've burned through."

Aether paused.

"You track all of that?"

The elder smiled faintly.

"Of course. Otherwise, by next month, we won't know what we have left."

He walked on with his tablets. Watching his retreating back, Aether suddenly understood something. Responsibility wasn't some grand, heroic notion. Most of the time, it was just work you had to do today—and do again tomorrow. Meanwhile, Rane and Kaelan pushed deep into the woods. This time, they weren't rushing to track prints. They simply walked slowly along the old hunting trail. Sunlight filtered down through the canopy, and the woods were quiet enough that only their footsteps broke the silence.

After walking for a long time, Kaelan noticed markings carved into many of the tree trunks. Most were faded, nearly swallowed by bark.

"What are those?" he asked. Rane reached out, running a thumb over an old notch.

"Trail markers. There used to be one every few hundred paces."

Kaelan looked down the path. Sure enough, there was another. And another beyond that, stretching deep into the gloom.

"Why doesn't anyone maintain them now?"

Rane was quiet for a beat.

"Because nobody walks this way anymore."

"Why?"

"I don't know," he said softly. Kaelan realized he had been hearing that phrase a lot lately. He used to think adults knew everything. Now, even Rane and Harold were constantly saying: *I don't know.* They reached the camp by noon. In the harsh light of day, the old camp looked even more dilapidated. The collapsed shelter, the moss-grown fire pit, the trail swallowed by weeds—everything screamed that human feet hadn't trodden here in a very long time.

Rane stood in the middle of the clearing, silent. Kaelan didn't rush him. After a long pause, Rane chuckled softly.

"First time I came here, I was about your age."

Kaelan looked at him.

"With your father?"

"Yeah."

Rane nodded.

"There used to be fires here at night. You'd see a dozen hunters gathered around. Telling stories, mending arrows, bragging about who brought down the biggest game."

His eyes drifted back to the collapsed roof.

"Back then, I thought it would always be like that."

The wind gusted through the clearing. Neither spoke.

"What happened?"

Kaelan asked.

"What happened?"

Rane smiled bitterly.

"Fewer people came. At first it was just a couple missing. Then half. Then nobody."

"Why?"

Kaelan pressed. Rane remained silent for a long time before shaking his head.

"I truly don't know."

He really didn't. It wasn't secrecy; it was ignorance. Kaelan looked around, a strange feeling creeping over him. These ruined remnants hadn't appeared overnight. They had vanished piece by piece, until one day, everyone simply got used to their absence. They spent the afternoon surveying the surrounding area, uncovering old stakes, rusted traps, and overgrown foundations whose original purpose was lost to time. This place had once been important. Now, nobody could say why. On the walk back, Kaelan was unusually quiet.

He thought of Gray Valley, of the everyday things he had taken for granted growing up. For the first time, he realized that years from now, Gray Valley might change too. It might fade just like this camp. The thought made him deeply uncomfortable. Back in Stony Creek, Aether worked until dusk before finally sitting down. His arms throbbed so severely he could barely lift them. The village elder passed by carrying his tablets. Seeing Aether collapsed on a crate, he chuckled.

"Tired?"

Aether nodded honestly.

"Exhausted."

The elder sat down beside him, looking out at the half-repaired fence.

"It's only been one day."

Aether let out a dry laugh.

"How have you managed to keep this up for three months?"

The elder didn't answer right away. The evening breeze stirred the air. Finally, he said, "Because somebody has to."

It was a simple sentence, yet it left Aether speechless. Responsibility didn't vanish just because it was hard. Most of the time, it simply fell on you because there was no one else to take it. The setting sun painted Stony Creek in shades of gold. In the distance, Kaelan and Rane returned. They looked worn out, but the heavy gloom from yesterday was gone. Today they hadn't been hunting for impossible answers; they had simply looked at the past—at things that had already disappeared.

And those things felt heavier than mere pawprints. Just as everyone prepared to retreat indoors for dinner, a sudden commotion broke out at the village entrance. A rider thundered into the settlement. A stranger. His clothes were caked in mud, his face drawn with bone-deep fatigue, as if he had ridden from the ends of the earth. The villagers froze. Rane frowned, stepping forward quickly.

The hunter swung down from his saddle, stumbling, barely able to catch his breath before raspy words burst from his throat: "Have you... have you been attacked here too?"

The air went dead silent. Rane stared at him, then gave a slow nod. The stranger's face drained of what little color it had left.

"God help us," he breathed.

"It's not just you."
