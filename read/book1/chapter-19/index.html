CHAPTER 34

The Blacksmith and the Merchants

← PreviousPart I ContentsNext →

The early autumn wind picked up a faint bite of cold.

In the days after leaving the waystation, Aether and Kaelan pushed further down the Great Road. The highway grew steadily busier. They passed lone wanderers, sprawling merchant caravans hauling heavy freight, and specialists the boys had never laid eyes on before—wheelwrights, horse trainers, herbalists, and cloth merchants.

Every person on the road seemed to know precisely what they were doing and where they were going. To Aether, it felt entirely new.

By dusk on the fifth day, they finally reached a town.

It was called Stonebridge, and its scale dwarfed Stony Creek. While it paled in comparison to the legendary Whitestone City, it was still vast enough to leave both boys speechless.

Stone-paved main streets. Two-story timber buildings. Smoke billowing from busy blacksmith shops. A chaotic, bustling marketplace. Freight wagons constantly pulling in and out, flanked by peddlers shouting their wares from the roadside.

Aether stood at the town entrance for a long time, mesmerized, as if beholding a whole new world for the very first time.

"There are ten times more people here than back in Stony Creek," he whispered.

Kaelan took in the crowd with a sharp glance. "More than that."

***

They stepped into town and quickly discovered that the heart of Stonebridge wasn't the taverns, but the marketplace.

A massive crowd of locals had gathered in a ring to watch a heated scene, arguments echoing over the noise of the square.

"You're stealing my bloody livelihood!" a heavy, gruff voice roared.

"It's just fair trade!" another voice fired back.

Aether and Kaelan squeezed through the pressing bodies to get a clear look.

A burly, broad-shouldered blacksmith stood in front of his stall, his face flushed red with rage. Across from him was a merchant crew that had just rolled into town.

The merchants' tables were piled high with iron goods—pots, nails, farm tools, and hunting knives—priced far lower than anything made in town.

"It takes me three days to forge a single scythe!" the blacksmith shouted, veins bulging on his neck. "You're selling yours cheaper than the cost of raw iron!"

The merchant leader sneered, unimpressed. "Because we haul them in bulk from the North! A larger supply means a cheaper price. Are you saying we shouldn't do business?"

The shouting match grew more vicious by the second. The crowd split, onlookers throwing in their own opinions—some backing the local blacksmith, others taking the side of the merchants. Neither side was willing to yield an inch.

Aether found himself instinctively leaning toward the blacksmith.

The smith looked far more tragic. His forge stood right beside the marketplace—its timber walls old and blackened, the hearth fires burning continuously. He had clearly poured decades of sweat into this town. The merchant caravan, by contrast, was merely passing through, ready to pack up and move on once their cartloads were cleared.

***

That evening, Aether made a point to visit the forge.

The smith's name was Bren. He had a wife, three young children, and a tired apprentice learning the trade. As the hearth fire flickered across Bren's face, he looked bone-weary.

"If they keep undercutting me like this," Bren muttered, resting his heavy arm on the anvil, "the townsfolk will never buy from me again."

"What will you do then?" Aether asked softly.

Bren fell silent for a long moment, staring into the embers. "I don't know. But I have a family to feed."

Aether had no answer for him. On the walk back to their inn, Bren's heavy words weighed on his mind.

***

The next day brought an unexpected turn when they ran into a young guard from the merchant caravan, who invited them to share a meal.

Over bread and broth, Aether learned the truth behind the cheap iron.

The caravan had been on the road for four agonizing months. They had braved bandit-infested mountain passes, navigated regions ravaged by feral beast tides, and pressed on through stretches of road washed out by torrential rain. To cut costs, the crew slept in the dirt beside their freight wagons. Some hadn't survived the trek at all.

Aether grew quiet. These merchants weren't the greedy, comfortable predators he had pictured.

"Why sell the goods so cheap then?" Aether asked, looking at the guard.

The young guard let out a bitter laugh. "Because we aren't the only caravan on the road. If we mark the prices up, nobody buys. If nobody buys, we rot out here and go broke."

Aether sat back, at a total loss for words.

Yesterday, he had been certain the blacksmith was the sole victim. Today, he realized the merchants were risking their lives just to stay afloat.

***

By dusk, another shout broke out in the marketplace, fiercer than the last.

Bren accused the merchants of intentionally driving him out of business; the merchants accused Bren of inciting a riot among the locals. Words turned into shoving, and the crowd surged forward, tension thick enough to ignite.

Just as fists were about to fly, the town mayor arrived. A squad of local guards pushed between the men, forcing the confrontation to a halt.

Yet nothing was settled.

The merchants kept selling. The blacksmith remained furious. Everyone walked away bitterly dissatisfied.

***

Night fell over Stonebridge.

Aether sat on the wooden steps outside their inn, gazing toward the quieted market square in silence. Beside him, Kaelan was methodically checking their travel packs.

"Who do you think is right?" Aether asked suddenly.

Kaelan stopped his work. "I don't know."

"You don't know?" Aether looked up, surprised. Kaelan usually had a firm grasp on things.

Kaelan considered it for a beat. "I think they're both afraid."

"Afraid?"

"The smith is afraid of losing his home," Kaelan said softly. "The merchants are afraid of losing their coin. They're both just trying to protect what's theirs."

Aether went quiet.

Those words pulled a string of memories to the surface. The villagers in Stony Creek were afraid of the beasts; Vic was afraid of losing what little he had left. Now, Bren was afraid of losing his craft, and the merchants were afraid of going hungry.

It seemed everyone out here was terrified of something.

A night breeze swept down the stone street, carrying the faint, rhythmic *clang* of an anvil from the distant forge. One strike after another.

Aether stared into the dark, watching the faint glow of hearth fire in the distance, lost in thought.

He had assumed the world was simple—that the blacksmith was wronged, the merchants were heartless, and one side had to be the villain. But reality refused to fit into neat boxes.

Bren wasn't wrong. The merchants weren't wrong either.

*So why were they at each other's throats?*

*Why did two decent men, both fighting tooth and nail to feed their children, end up as enemies?*

The question spun endlessly through his mind, offering no easy answers.

As the hour grew late, Kaelan headed inside to sleep. Aether remained on the steps, staring out toward the Great Road. In the distance, torches from passing caravans moved slowly through the gloom like a river of fire crawling through the dark.

It struck him then: the further he traveled from Stony Creek, the wider the world became—and the heavier its problems grew.

Vic had shown him that some people get trapped in the past. But Bren and the merchants were showing him something far more troubling: even when no one is evil, conflict still tears people apart.

Aether rose slowly to his feet, a dark, unsettling question taking root in his chest for the very first time.

*If both sides are right... why does everything still go wrong?*

No answer came back from the dark.

Only the distant torchlight on the Great Road, slowly crawling eastward into the night.

***

The wind grew colder as early autumn deepened.

After leaving Stonebridge, Aether and Kaelan pushed further east along the Great Road. Broad farmlands began to unfurl on either side of the highway. Unlike the sparse, scattered plots of Stony Creek, the fields here seemed to stretch into infinity, golden waves of wheat rolling beneath the wind all the way to the horizon.

Aether stood atop a grassy ridge, staring out at the sight for a long time.

"Look at all that grain," he whispered softly. "How many mouths could that feed?"

Kaelan took in the vast expanse with a quiet glance. "A great many."

For the first time, Aether truly understood that Whitestone City existed not merely because of its massive stone walls, but because of these lands, these scattered villages, and the farmers who worked the dirt.

***

At dusk, they arrived at a settlement called Riverdale.

Before they even reached the edge of the village, a heated shouting match echoed from the central square.

A dozen angry farmers had crowded into the open space, their faces dark with fury. Opposite them, a handful of armed guards flanked a heavy wooden transport wagon, standing beside a middle-aged tax collector clad in a simple gray robe.

The two sides were locked in a tense standoff.

"Our yield this year is half of what we brought in last autumn!" an elderly farmer shouted, shaking a gnarled fist. "We can barely keep our own children fed! Why are you still demanding the full tax?"

Angry mutters cascaded through the gathered villagers.

The tax collector looked exhausted, dark rings carved deep beneath his eyes, yet he stood his ground. "The tax quota is set by Whitestone City. I have no authority to alter it."

"Then let the lords in Whitestone City come down here and look at our fields themselves!" someone roared from the back.

The tension in the air climbed toward a boiling point.

Aether frowned, his hands clenching into fists. His sympathies aligned with the farmers almost instantly. Their distress was plain to see—threadbare clothes, hollow cheeks, and the genuine, unfeigned terror of a harsh winter ahead. That kind of desperation couldn't be faked.

For the rest of the day, Aether walked through the village, talking with the locals.

One farmer spoke of feral beasts slaughtering their livestock; another talked of sick children and medical debts; another showed him crops rotted through by sudden torrential rains. Every family bore a heavy burden; every house had a story of hardship.

The more Aether listened, the angrier he became. *Why insist on collecting taxes now? Why couldn't the city grant them a single year's grace? Why couldn't they give these people room to breathe?*

That evening, back at their campfire, Aether blurted out to Kaelan, "It isn't fair. What they're doing is wrong."

Kaelan didn't reply. His attention was focused on something else entirely.

***

The following morning, Kaelan pointed out a curious detail.

The tax collector and his guards were eating the exact same sparse rations as the travelers on the road—stale black bread, lukewarm water, and tiny strips of dried meat. Their tunics were frayed at the cuffs and patched at the elbows. They looked nothing like the pampered, greedy officials Aether had envisioned.

"They don't look wealthy either," Kaelan observed quietly.

Aether blinked, caught off guard. It was a thought that had never crossed his mind.

Later that evening, Aether approached the tax collector himself.

The man’s name was Matthews. He was around fifty years old, his face weathered and lined by years of travel. He looked more like a tired merchant than a man wielding authority.

"Do you know how hard they have it?" Aether asked directly.

Matthews fell silent for a long moment, staring out over the darkening fields. "I do."

"Then why still take their food?"

Matthews sighed softly, his gaze lingering on the horizon. "Because I also know that the garrison needs grain to eat. The waystations need coin for repairs. The highways must be maintained, and the bridges need guards to keep the roads safe from bandits."

Aether went quiet.

Matthews continued in a low, measured voice. "If there were only one village in this land, things would be simple. But it isn't just Riverdale. There are dozens of villages, hundreds of miles of trade routes, garrisons, and entire regions depending on the order we keep."

Aether had no answer. He had never looked at the world from such a scale before.

***

That night, everything shattered.

While inspecting a communal granary, one of the soldiers discovered a hidden cache of unrecorded grain.

Word spread through the village like wildfire, and within minutes, an enraged mob of farmers swarmed the storehouse.

"That's our winter survival stash!" a farmer screamed. "That isn't tax evasion!"

"The declared amount doesn't match the storage," the soldier insisted, holding his ground. "It violates the realm's law."

The argument flared violently. Accusations turned to shouting, and shouting turned to physical jostling.

In the chaos, a young farmer surged forward to grab the grain bag. Instinctively, a young guard shoved him back.

The farmer lost his footing, stumbled backward, and struck his head against a sharp stone boundary marker.

Blood welled up instantly, staining the dirt red.

The entire square went dead silent.

A heartbeat later, absolute chaos erupted. Women screamed, elders cursed, and the young guards nervously drew their weapons, forming a tight defensive ring. The air tightened like a snapped bowstring.

Aether stood frozen in the surging crowd, his heart hammering against his ribs.

The nature of the dispute had changed in a single second. It was no longer about grain or taxes—someone had bled.

Matthews’ face turned deathly pale. He shoved his way through his men, barked orders for them to sheath their steel, and demanded someone fetch the local healer immediately.

Stepping into the center of the crowd, he announced loudly, "The collection is suspended. We stop here today. Everything recorded will be sent up to Whitestone City for review."

Neither side walked away satisfied, but the violence receded.

***

Night fell over Riverdale.

Aether sat by the flickering campfire, staring into the dark in long, unbroken silence.

Every scene from the day played endlessly in his mind.

The farmers spoke the truth—they were genuinely struggling to survive. The tax collector spoke the truth as well—the roads and garrisons required grain to function. Even the young guard who shoved the farmer hadn't intended to hurt anyone.

*So why did someone still end up bleeding in the dirt?*

What confused Aether even more was a detail he learned later that night: the young farmer who was injured happened to be one of the men hiding the largest hoard of undeclared grain. He was truly desperate, yet he was also truly breaking the law.

Thoughts tangled violently in Aether's mind.

Everyone was telling the truth, yet each person only held the piece of truth that belonged to them.

The farmers saw their own misery. The tax collector saw his duty. The soldier saw his orders.

*Yet no one seemed able to see the whole picture.*

***

The firelight flickered softly against the dark. Beside him, Kaelan sat silently, whittling a sturdy branch with his hunting knife.

After a long time, Aether spoke, his voice barely above a whisper. "Kaelan?"

"Yeah?"

"If everyone can only see what's right in front of them... who is supposed to look out for the whole world?"

Kaelan stopped his whittling. He stared into the night, silent for a very long time, before slowly shaking his head.

"I don't know."

It was a rare admission from him. Aether didn't push further—he didn't have the answer either.

Far off in the dark, the faint torchlight of travelers on the Great Road moved slowly onward, flowing like an endless, unyielding river.

And that unanswered question flowed right along with it, sinking deep into the boy's heart.
