CHAPTER 25

More Than Meets the Eye

By the time the sun fully cleared the northern ridges, Stony Creek was already at work. The signs of the midnight attack were scattered across the southern quadrant: a splintered section of cedar rail, dried brown blood on the frost-hardened earth, and the skinned carcass of the dead ewe hanging in the butcher’s shed. To the villagers, it was just another morning of clearing damage. Ren did not rest. After a brief meal of cold salt pork, he gathered his tracking kit and headed back toward the southern gap.

Harold and Kaelen followed close behind. In the daytime, the forest surrendered secrets that the night hid under its cloak. They picked up the wolves' exit trail instantly. Clear, deep paw-prints cut through the muddy bank of the runoff ditch, heading straight into the tall pine timber.

"Here,"

Kaelen noted, pointing his bow-tip at a clear pad-mark in the clay. Ren nodded, sweeping his eyes ahead.

"Aye. Three of them fled this way."

They pushed deeper into the woods, following the broken ferns and fresh mud turns. But as they crossed the first ridge, Ren's pace slowed. He dropped to one knee, his face tightening.

"Five,"

Ren muttered. Kaelen halted beside him.

"What?"

"There weren't three wolves out here last night,"

Ren said, pointing to a cluster of overlapping prints near an uprooted spruce.

"There were at least five."

Kaelen crouched down. New prints—some smaller, some signi ficantly larger—merged with the escape trail from different angles.

"Why didn't the other two attack?"

Kaelen asked, a chill running down his neck.

"They were waiting,"

Harold's deep voice answered from behind. They moved further north, following the heavy trail. The further they went, the more chaotic the forest floor became. The prints multiplied—not just wolves, but deer, mountain goats, and small game—all crisscrossing in frantic, aimless patterns. Then, Harold stopped dead in his tracks.

"Hold."

Ren and Kaelen froze instantly. Harold was kneeling by a small patch of dark, wet silt near the creek bed. His broad, calloused fingers hovered an inch above the ground, his eyes narrowed into tight slits. Kaelen stepped closer and looked down. It was a depression in the earth. Shallow, dragged, and strange. It was roughly the width of an ox's hoof, but there were no split-claws, no pad-marks, and no drag-grooves left by nails.

It looked as though something heavy had pressed into the soil, smoothing the mud flat before moving on without leaving a single print. Ren leaned down, his brow pulled into deep furrows.

"What in the hells is that? A bear?"

"No bear leaves a trail like that,"

Harold said softly.

"A dragged log?"

Kaelen suggested.

"Look at the edges,"

Harold replied, gesturing to the displaced silt.

"The earth was pushed outward, not dragged along the line. Something stood here. Something heavy."

Ren looked at Harold, his mouth set in a hard, grim line.

"Have you ever seen a mark like this in the high ranges?"

Harold remained silent for a long time. He reached out and touched the dirt, feeling its texture. Finally, he shook his head.

"Never."

A subtle, suffocating quiet descended over the woods. The wind whistled faintly through the high canopy, but down on the forest floor, not a single bird sang. They found two more of the strange marks further up the ridge, each spaced strangely far apart, before the rocky terrain swallowed all remaining sign. They had no answers. Only a growing, cold certainty that whatever was moving through these mountains was far larger—and far stranger—than a pack of starving wolves. By mid-afternoon, the three men trudged back through Stony Creek’s northern gate.

The villagers gathered around them instantly, their faces pale and eager.

"Did you find their den?"

"Will they come back tonight?"

"Did you kill the pack leader?"

Ren remained silent, staring at the dirt. Kaelen looked away, unable to meet the desperate eyes of the farm wives. Harold stepped forward, his voice steady and calm.

"No den found yet. We strengthen the eastern fence tonight."

The crowd quieted down. The spark of hope in their eyes flickered out, replaced once again by that familiar, dull endurance. They didn't yell. They didn't curse Harold. They simply dispersed back to their chores—back to the hammer, the scythe, and the hearth. Aether stood near the granary, watching the scene unfold. A terrible realization washed over him. The most terrifying thing about Stony Creek wasn't the wolves or the unknown beast in the forest. It was that these people were starting to accept it.

They were becoming accustomed to living like cattle in a pen, waiting for the butcher's knife every time the sun went down. Later that evening, as the sun dipped behind the peaks, casting long, bloody shadows across the valley, Harold walked out of the village alone. He didn't take Kaelen. He didn't take his iron shield. He walked back to the edge of the northern treeline, where the first strange marks had been discovered. The wind combed through the tall grass at his knees.

Harold crouched in the dimming light, his hand resting on the smooth, unnatural depression in the dirt. He stared deep into the swallowing blackness of the timber, where the shadows were creeping down the mountain like a rising tide. He stood up slowly, his gray eyes reflecting the last cold light of the dying sun.

"This isn't all of it,"

Harold whispered to the empty woods. The forest gave no reply. Only the cold mountain wind blew through the pines, carrying with it the icy promise of a long, dark night.
