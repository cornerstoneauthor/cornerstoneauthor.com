CHAPTER 23

Hands in the Darkness

The old man left the church later than usual that night. At the doorway he glanced back along the rows of empty benches. The candles had been extinguished, and silence had returned to the vaulted hall. He closed the wooden door and turned toward his room. In the courtyard Seren had also stopped walking. She looked up at the night sky. Tomorrow she was supposed to leave. She had just drawn a breath and turned away when a heavy knock sounded at the outer gate.

There was a pause, then another blow. It did not sound like a visitor announcing himself. It sounded like someone spending the last of his strength to remain standing. The old man stopped at once. Lamps appeared in the courtyard as several young people hurried out wearing their coats. He reached the gate and pulled it open. Before it was halfway ajar, a tall figure pitched forward. The old man caught him, and the others rushed to help.

In the lamplight they saw a man scarcely past his twenties, though his weathered face suggested a much longer life. His cloak was soaked with blood. A terrible wound ran from his left shoulder across his back, and the torn fabric bore marks like those left by an animal's claws. Older scars covered his arms, chest, and waist: pale lines from wounds long healed and narrower traces of blades, each seeming to belong to a different place and a different struggle. The old man's expression darkened. “Quickly. Bring him inside.”

Someone tried to remove the long sword from the stranger's back, only to discover that his right hand still gripped the hilt even in unconsciousness. His fingers had turned white with the effort. A young man tried to loosen them, but they would not move. The old man shook his head. “Leave it. Save the man first.”

Together they carried him into a room behind the church. Blood fell drop by drop onto the stone path, and its metallic smell soon mingled with the night air. Seren came at once. Without waiting to be asked, she set water to heat, brought clean linen, and gathered the medicinal herbs that had become familiar to her during her stay.

The old man cut away the cloak where it had stuck to the wound. For a moment everyone fell silent. The injury in his shoulder went almost to the bone, but it was the older scars that held their attention: a tangled map of suffering across a life none of them knew. As he worked to stem the blood, the old man spoke quietly. “We'll know by morning whether he can survive.”

No one answered. Seren wrung out a cloth and wiped the cold sweat from the stranger's brow. More lamps were lit; fresh water was heated; someone fetched the herbs drying in the rear courtyard. The old man remained kneeling beside the bed, his movements steady. He examined the man's chest and back after treating the shoulder. The old injuries were frightening, but they had healed years ago. The fresh wound was the danger. Something with enormous claws had torn the flesh apart.

“Not a blade,” the old man murmured.

“And not a wolf, either,” one of the younger helpers said.

The old man offered no reply. He continued stitching the wound while the room grew quiet enough for them to hear the thread being drawn through flesh. On the other side of the bed Seren worked without pause. Bloodied cloth after bloodied cloth went into the basin until the water turned dark red. As she cleaned the man's face, she finally saw him clearly. He was young, yet there was an age in his features that did not belong to his years. A pale scar marked his brow; a thin knife wound traced his chin. Even unconscious, he frowned as though sleep had never offered him safety.

At last the old man set down his needle. “The bleeding has stopped for now. What happens next depends on tonight.” Almost immediately the man's body shuddered. His breathing quickened, and sweat beaded across his forehead. Seren touched his skin and drew back her fingers. “He's burning up.”

The old man nodded, unsurprised. “The fever has begun. With a wound like this, it may be the hardest part.” He went to the cabinet, selected several herbs, and began grinding them. “Someone must remain with him all night. Keep wiping him down and changing the cloths. If he can endure the fever...” He looked toward the bed. “Then perhaps he has a chance.”

They took turns watching him as the hours passed. Someone prepared medicine, someone fetched water, and someone replaced the cloths soaked through with sweat. Seren stayed, seated on a small stool beside the bed. Outside, the wind passed around the bell tower, but the bell did not ring. The church grew quiet except for the occasional crackle of the fire. The wounded man seemed to be entering a darkness no one else could follow him into. None of them knew that this night would alter the journey Seren had expected to continue alone.

The lamps burned on. When the old man returned with a bowl of freshly prepared medicine, its bitter fragrance mingled with the scents of timber and smoke. He touched the stranger's forehead and frowned. The fever remained high.

“How is he?” a young helper whispered.

“I don't know,” the old man replied. “It may depend on how fiercely he wants to live.”

No one pressed him. They had done what they could, and sometimes the rest could only be endured. The old man laid another damp cloth on the patient's forehead; it warmed almost immediately. Seren took it, dipped it back into the basin, and replaced it with a fresh one. Her hands moved with the quiet assurance she had acquired caring for the church's other guests.

No one noticed that when Seren gently supported the man's shoulder and turned him to change the dressing on his back, the tight lines of his face eased a little. His breathing became more even. The old man glanced down but said nothing, continuing to secure the bandages. Through the remaining hours the fever rose and fell. Sometimes it seemed to subside, only to return more fiercely. The man's body trembled, and cold sweat broke out on his forehead. Seren wiped it away each time, without complaint, as she might have tended an older brother she had never met—or any of the other people who came to the church needing help.

There was little conversation. Now and then the old man reminded someone not to disturb the wound or to give the patient a little more water. Exhaustion settled over the watchers. A woman from the kitchen brought hot soup and urged the old man to rest. He drank two mouthfuls before returning to the bedside. Seren saw the redness around his eyes. “Let me take a turn watching him,” she offered.

The old man studied her for a moment, then nodded. “Very well. Call me if anything changes.” He went out, closing the door softly. With the others gone, only the fire disturbed the silence. Seren wrung out another cloth and laid it against the stranger's forehead. At the touch of her fingers, his breathing began to slow, and the frown he had worn for so long gradually eased.

Somewhere in the darkness beyond his reach, the hands that had been supporting him seemed to lift him once more. There was no voice, no light, no face—only those hands, steadfast and near. When he was cold, they were there. When fever consumed him, they were there. When thirst tormented him, they remained. Even as he sank toward a darkness without end, they would not leave.

They demanded nothing and gave no explanation. Again and again they held him up, as though unwilling to let him fall any farther, as though there were an unseen path beneath him that he had not yet learned to trust. He did not know whose hands they were or why they remained, but one thing became steadily clearer: they did not want him to die.

There was no command and no force, only a gentle persistence he could not resist. It was like the care of someone who had waited a very long time for an exhausted traveler and now steadied him, telling him without words that the road continued and that he need not stop here. Little by little, the cold and fear and loneliness receded. In their place came something he had never known.

Someone cared whether he lived. Someone was willing to wait for him to wake. Someone wanted him to go on. No voice spoke those truths, yet they felt more real than any words he had heard. The knowledge entered a heart made numb by years of hardship, like the first spring warmth loosening a river locked in winter ice, or rain settling into earth that had long been dry. Nothing about it was grand or overwhelming. Yet for the first time in years, he felt warmth, hope, and love reaching a place within him he had thought no longer capable of feeling.

He still did not know where he lay or to whom those hands belonged. He knew only that whenever he seemed about to slip into death, they lifted him again. Not yet, they seemed to say. It is not time to leave.

In the room, the wood in the hearth gave a soft crack. Seren sat beside the bed, changing the cloth whenever it grew warm and raising his head carefully to feed him a few drops of medicine. Some ran down his chin. She wiped them away and tried again, patiently, until he swallowed a little. Only then did she release the breath she had been holding. He did not wake. Sometimes his body struggled, as though fighting something she could not see. She supported his shoulder and held the fresh dressing in place to keep the wound from tearing open. Gradually he settled, and his breathing steadied once more.

* * *

In the darkness the hands were still there. He could no longer tell how much time had passed or why he was able to continue. Whenever his strength failed, they supported him without offering reasons or explanations. They accompanied him step by step, as one might walk beside a child learning to stand. If he fell, they helped him rise. If he could go no farther, they waited. They never hurried him, and they never gave up. A place within him that had lain barren for years began to soften. The feeling was so unfamiliar that he almost wanted to weep, though it had been a very long time since he had shed a tear.

* * *

The first birdsong broke through the long night. A pale light appeared at the edge of the sky. The old man opened the door and came quietly inside. Seren was still seated beside the bed, weary from a sleepless night, one hand supporting the young man's arm as though she feared that releasing him might send him back into the fever. The old man touched his forehead and waited a moment. His expression eased. “The fever... it's fallen a little.”

Seren checked for herself. He was right: the man's skin was no longer as hot as it had been. The old man let out a breath, and a faint smile crossed his face. “At least he survived the night.”

Morning light entered the room. The bells sounded at their usual hour, and the life of the church continued. The ovens were lit, children carried firewood, and visitors came to the gate for their daily food. The old man climbed the tower and went through the courtyard as he always did, but he returned to the sickroom more often than usual.

* * *

The fever had eased, yet the stranger did not wake. At times his brow tightened or his lips moved without sound. The old man inspected the wound and nodded. “It hasn't grown worse. That's something.” But the danger had not passed, and he did not pretend otherwise.

* * *

Seren's packed bag remained by the door of her room. She neither unpacked it nor moved it away, as though it were waiting for a decision. Each day she continued helping in the kitchen and caring for visitors. In the afternoons she returned to the sickroom to change the dressings, give water, and tend the wound. No one told her to stay. No one asked when she intended to leave. After a while, she stopped asking herself.

* * *

One evening the old man sat beside the bed and felt the young man's pulse. After a long silence, he said, “That he's lived this long is already a grace.” Seren nodded. The old man smiled faintly and tucked the blanket around his patient. “The rest will depend on him. Some people return in body while their hearts are still a long way off.”

Seren did not fully understand. She watched the young man, whose breathing was steadier than the night before but whose face remained tense, as though he were still struggling with an invisible adversary.

* * *

Night fell again. Seren entered the dim room carrying warm medicine. Only a single lamp burned beside the bed. She sat down and supported the young man's shoulder, preparing to feed him as she had the previous night. Just as the wooden bowl touched his lips, his fingers moved. The motion was so slight that she almost missed it. She looked down, but the scarred hand had gone still again. Perhaps, she thought, she had imagined it.
