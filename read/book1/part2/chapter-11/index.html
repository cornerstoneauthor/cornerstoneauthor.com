CHAPTER 11

Seeing

← PreviousPart II ContentsNext →

Old Wayne came back carrying the box of iron nails against his chest.

“Found them.” He set the box down with a soft thump and patted the lid, smiling. “I thought I’d put them in the wrong place. Turns out they were buried under the boards at the back.”

The woman who dried fish in the yard laughed. “If you hadn’t found them, no one would be finishing a roof today.”

“That wouldn’t do.” Wayne waved the thought away. “If you give someone your word, you finish the work.” He lifted the box again and looked toward Seren. “Come along, girl. There’s another house waiting for us.”

Seren nodded and followed him out of the yard.

They took the narrow path east along the shore. The sea wind came damp and salt-laden across the village, stirring the hems of coats and carrying the smell of drying nets. People greeted Wayne wherever they went.

“Uncle Wayne, helping someone again?”

He only raised a hand and smiled. “Might as well do what I can while these old arms still work.”

Before long they reached another house. Its yard was larger than the first, and half the roof had already been stripped away. Several men stood on ladders, prying loose boards darkened by rot, while fresh timber lay stacked below. A shipwright steadied one ladder. An old net-mender sat nearby shaving wooden wedges, and two younger men carried planks back and forth across the yard.

The moment Wayne entered, one of the men on the roof leaned over the edge.

“Uncle Wayne! At last. You found the nails.”

Wayne lifted the box over his head. “Without these, none of you would be going home before dark.”

Laughter moved through the yard, and then everyone returned to work. One measured boards while another passed up tools; someone held the ladder steady as another drove nails into the new timber. Seren stood aside at first, uncertain where she belonged, until the shipwright handed her a coil of rope.

“Just help us pass the boards up, girl. That’ll be enough.”

“All right.”

She took the rope and joined them. One board, then another, then a third. Little by little she found the rhythm of the work. What struck her most was how little anyone needed to say. A glance toward an empty hand, a tilt of the head toward a tool, a shift in someone’s stance—each small gesture seemed enough. They had done such things together for years, perhaps for most of their lives, and the knowledge had settled into their bodies.

Wood shavings drifted through shafts of sunlight. Hammers struck the roof in a steady cadence, solid and patient beneath the sound of the sea.

After a time, the young man above leaned out again. “Uncle Wayne! We’re short on timber.”

Wayne looked up, then glanced at the few boards left in the corner. “Seems I counted badly after all.” He brushed sawdust from his hands. “There’s more in the back shed.”

“I’ll fetch it,” the young man offered.

The shipwright shook his head. “Take your time, Wayne. We can fit this side while you’re gone.”

Wayne nodded. “Good.”

He shouldered the wooden carrying frame and disappeared down the village path. The yard grew quieter after he left, though the hammers continued. The fish-drying woman came in with a bucket and poured cool water for everyone.

Seren accepted a bowl and watched Wayne’s figure recede between the houses.

“Grandfather Wayne is a good man,” she said.

The shipwright smiled. “That he is. If someone in White Bay is in trouble, he’s usually the first one through the door.” He steadied a board against the wall. “When we were younger, we used to say he meddled too much. Took us a while to understand. It isn’t that he likes other people’s business. He simply can’t bear to see someone struggling when he can help.”

His smile lingered, but his eyes lowered. “Other people’s troubles—he carries them as though they were his own.”

He said no more and took up his hammer again.

The old net-mender had not looked up. After a while, he ran a hand over the fresh board beside his foot and spoke quietly.

“Wayne has repaired a great many roofs in this village. If someone else’s roof leaks, he always finds a way to mend it.” The old man paused, and the hammering seemed suddenly louder. “His own house, though... that is one thing he has never managed to repair.”

Seren remained still. She knew he was not speaking about the roof over Wayne’s head.

A middle-aged man carrying timber lowered the planks from his shoulder and wiped the sweat from his brow. He did not look at anyone when he spoke.

“Wayne will bow his head for anyone. Always has. But once he steps through his own door...” He shook his head. “No one knows why he and his son can’t bring themselves to speak.”

Then he bent to lift the next board.

No one answered him. No one offered a judgment or decided who had been right and who had been wrong. The hammers resumed their place in the silence.

Seren looked from one face to another. Each person had spoken differently, yet all of them had stopped at the same place, as though there were a crack running through Wayne’s household that everyone in White Bay could see but no one knew how to cross.

Then Wayne’s bright voice came from beyond the yard.

“Got the timber!”

Everyone turned. He came in under the weight of the carrying frame, walking steadily, the same easy smile on his face.

“Just in time,” the shipwright called.

The yard returned to its former rhythm. No one behaved as though anything difficult had been said. They joked, passed boards, steadied ladders, and worked the final planks into place. Watching Wayne move among them, Seren understood that the villagers could admire him, love him, and ache for him all at once. None of those things contradicted the others.

At last the final board was nailed down.

“Done!” the young man on the roof cried.

Laughter broke out below. The woman who owned the house stood near the gate, bowing again and again.

“Thank you. Truly, I don’t know how to repay any of you.”

Wayne put his hammer back into the box. “We live in the same village. What use is there in talking about repayment?”

Her eyes had gone red. “If you hadn’t come, we’d be setting bowls under the leaks again when the rain starts tonight.”

“Then next time someone else needs a hand, you go help them.” Wayne patted her shoulder. “That’s how a village lives.”

The others nodded as if the matter required no further explanation.

They began putting away ladders and stacking the leftover timber. Children ran beneath the eaves and stared up at the new roof as though it were some grand work of craft. Sunlight warmed the fresh boards to a pale gold.

Seren stood in the yard and watched. Here, helping one another seemed as ordinary as breathing. No one counted what had been given; no one waited for a reward.

The shipwright leaned the ladder against the wall and dusted his hands. “There. For once we may finish early.”

The old net-mender rose with a groan and stretched his back. “If my waist still belongs to me by supper, I’ll count the day a success.”

More laughter followed.

Then a young voice called from outside the yard. “Word from the harbor! The wind held fair. The fishing boats are coming in early.”

The shipwright glanced toward Wayne. “Then your boy should be home soon.”

Wayne was lowering his hammer into the box. His hand stopped for the briefest moment. Then he closed the lid and smiled as usual.

“That’s good. We may still have a hot meal waiting when we get back.”

Nothing in his voice had changed, yet the yard fell quiet for a heartbeat. The net-mender looked down at his tools. The shipwright cleared his throat and lifted the ladder. The fish-drying woman filled the silence with a cheerful tone.

“Early is good. Sea was calm today, so the catch should be decent. Nice to have the house full at supper.”

Wayne nodded. “Aye.”

That was all.

They finished packing away the tools and went their separate ways. The woman whose roof they had repaired followed them to the gate, waving.

“Come eat with us sometime.”

Wayne waved back. “When I can.”

On the walk home he seemed exactly as he had that morning. He stopped to greet people, lifted a child who had fallen in the road, and carried an old man’s bundle for part of the way. The same reassuring smile met everyone.

Seren followed a few steps behind, unable to forget what she had heard. Other people’s roofs, Wayne could mend. His own had remained broken for years.

The wind tugged at his coat as he walked. His back was still straight, his pace neither hurried nor slow. No stranger seeing him would have guessed that the man who carried so many burdens for others had one wound in his own home that neither skill nor kindness had managed to close.

As they neared the house, a long horn sounded from the harbor. Cheers rose almost at once and came rolling through the village on the sea wind.

“The boats are back!”

“They’re back!”

The harbor sprang to life. People turned toward the water; some waved, while others hurried down to the quay. Wayne stopped as well. Far out, fishing boats were gliding toward shore, their sails turned gold by the setting sun.

He watched them for a while. His smile remained gentle, but something quieter had entered it.

“Come,” he said at last.

He continued toward home.

The gate stood half open when they arrived, and the smell of supper reached them before they entered the yard. Wayne’s wife was gathering the fish that had been drying in the sun. She set down the bamboo tray when she saw them.

“Back already?”

“Aye.”

Wayne lowered the tool box beside the wall and rolled one aching shoulder. “Roof’s finished.”

“Good.” Her face brightened. “The fish soup is nearly ready, and I steamed some shellfish. We can eat while everything’s hot.”

She glanced toward the harbor, where voices still carried through the evening air. “They came back early today. Supper should still be warm.”

Wayne answered with a quiet sound and went to wash at the well.

Seren said nothing. She did not need to ask whom the old woman meant.

Footsteps sounded outside the gate, steady and unhurried. The wooden door opened, and a young man entered with a wet fishing net over one shoulder. Seawater darkened the legs of his trousers, and fine white sand clung to his boots. He set the net in the corner and brushed droplets from his clothes before lifting his head.

“I’m home.”

Wayne’s wife smiled at once. “Go wash your hands. You’ve timed it well—the food’s just ready.”

The young man nodded. “All right.”

Only then did he notice Seren. Surprise crossed his face, brief and restrained.

“This is Seren,” his mother explained. “She passed through the village yesterday. She’s been staying with us.”

He inclined his head. “Hello.”

“Hello.”

The exchange was courteous and easy. Then his gaze shifted toward his father.

Wayne had finished washing and was setting the bucket back beside the well. Their eyes met for a moment.

“How was the catch?” Wayne asked.

“Not bad.”

“Wind stayed down?”

“Aye.”

“Good.”

There was no quarrel in the words, no coldness sharp enough to name, not even impatience. Yet Seren felt the distance between them at once. It was not a great distance. That was what made it strange. Either man could have crossed it with a step, and neither did.

Wayne’s wife clapped her hands lightly, as though she had lived beside this silence long enough to know how to move around it.

“Enough standing about. The food will go cold.”

They went inside.

The oil lamp cast a warm glow across the wooden table. A pot of fish soup steamed at the center beside a plate of shellfish and a few simple dishes. It was not a rich meal, but after the day’s work the room felt warm and full.

Wayne was already seated when his son came in from washing. He glanced at the young man’s wet trousers.

“Sea water’s cold. Change those before long.”

His son nodded. “Aye.”

Silence settled again.

Wayne’s wife ladled soup into wooden bowls. “You’ve all worked today. Eat.” She placed a bowl before her husband, another before her son, then handed one to Seren. “And you, girl—don’t be shy.”

“Thank you.”

For a while the only sound was the soft touch of wooden spoons against bowls.

Wayne reached for a piece of fish. When he looked up, he saw that the plate of shellfish nearest his son was nearly empty. His hand moved toward the second plate, as if to push it across the table. Before his fingers reached it, the young man stood and moved the plate himself.

Wayne’s hand remained in the air for a fraction of a second. Then he withdrew it and lowered his eyes to his soup.

No one spoke.

Seren saw it.

A little later Wayne cleared his throat. His son looked up. For a moment the young man’s lips parted as though words had reached the edge of speech. Then he lowered his gaze and continued eating.

Wayne’s wife saw that too. She added another spoonful of soup to Wayne’s bowl.

“You’ve been working all day. Have more.”

Then she placed a piece of fish in her son’s bowl. “You too.”

“All right.”

The room remained peaceful. No accusation was spoken, no old grievance dragged into the light. Yet to Seren the table seemed divided by an invisible river. Father and son stood on opposite banks, each looking toward the other, neither willing—or perhaps neither knowing how—to enter the water.

After supper the young man carried the bowls to the kitchen. Wayne went outside and cleaned his tools one by one before returning them to the box. Both were busy. Neither was idle. Still, they found no words for one another.

Seren helped wash the last bowl. Wayne’s wife watched the two men in the yard and sighed.

“Those two...”

She set the bowl on the shelf. “They care for each other. They do. And it isn’t as though they’ve never wanted to make peace.”

A tired smile touched her face. “They just can’t let go.”

She dried her hands and looked toward the worn wooden tool box outside.

“I’ve talked to them for years. One, then the other. They both agree with me while I’m speaking. By the next morning, everything is as it was.”

There was no blame in her eyes, only the weariness of someone who had watched the same sorrow too long.

“They’re both waiting,” she said. “Each one is waiting for the other to speak first.”

Her smile turned helpless. “And after all these years, neither has heard the words he was waiting for.”

Seren did not ask what had happened between them. The old woman did not volunteer the story.

Outside, the sea washed against the shore in its endless rhythm. Wayne closed the lid of his tool box. His son gathered the fishing net. They lived beneath the same roof, ate at the same table, crossed the same yard each day—and somehow a road remained between them that neither had managed to walk.

That night Seren lay awake for a long time.

She thought of the roof they had repaired beneath the afternoon sun. She remembered what the villagers had said, the silence at supper, and the old woman’s tired sigh.

One question returned again and again: why could two people who plainly cared for each other fail to reach one another?

No dream came to answer her. No voice stirred in the darkness.

Only the sea remained, breathing against the shore of White Bay, wave after wave.
