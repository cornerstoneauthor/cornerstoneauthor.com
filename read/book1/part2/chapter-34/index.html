CHAPTER 34

The Return of Stillness

← PreviousPart II ContentsNext →

The sun was already moving west when they emerged from the wood. Kael led his companions a good distance along the road before easing his pace. None of them was ready to speak immediately about the fight. The moments had passed too quickly to understand while they were happening, and each needed time to settle them into memory. Seren looked back more than once, though the hills had already hidden the trees. She could still hear the brief sound the wolf had made as it fell. She had expected fear to leave her helpless, yet in the midst of danger she had thought only of her staff and the positions of her companions. Now, with the threat behind them, her hands began to ache.

Seeing that she was falling behind, Kael stopped at a sheltered slope and shared out their food. He checked the bandage on his shoulder to make sure it had not begun bleeding again. Elowen ate slowly. “I didn't even know whether I could hit it,” she admitted. “I saw it coming for you, and I threw what I had.” Kael told her that saving someone did not always allow time for a perfect plan. She had recognized danger and acted within her ability; that mattered. But he also cautioned her that, if there was a next time, she should first look for a place of safety. They could not count on every thrown stone finding its mark.

Seren asked whether he truly had no other choice when the wolves attacked. Kael did not try to make the danger sound less serious. The pack had worked together better than he had expected, he said, and without the women holding their positions he might have been overwhelmed. He admitted that he had initially believed a sword in his hand would be enough to protect two companions who had never trained for combat. When the wolves closed in from three directions, he had realized how arrogant—and how unrealistic—that assumption had been. Seren was surprised to hear him speak so plainly. Kael was dependable, but he did not pretend that dependability meant never making a mistake.

They continued through hills where the wind moved across tall grass in rippling waves. In the distance farmers were gathering tools at the edge of a field, and a small cart was making its slow way toward a village. The ordinariness of the scene was almost difficult for Seren to accept. Only a short while before, wolves had been close enough to reach her with their claws. If the animals carried some unknown sickness, did the villages nearby know that danger had reached this far? Kael said they should tell the road wardens at the next inhabited place what they had seen, especially the dark mark on the wolf. They must, however, distinguish what they had witnessed from what they merely suspected. An unfounded panic could travel faster than a useful warning.

Elowen agreed. At the Order, she explained, elders recording illnesses insisted that symptoms, the time of their appearance, and the course of treatment be written down separately. Belief in one explanation was never an excuse to ignore other possibilities. She recalled the mark behind the wolf's neck: skin darkening from gray to black, edges that looked eaten away, and fur that had not entirely fallen out. Kael added the daytime hunt and the leader's coordinated withdrawal. Seren remembered that the wolves had not fought one another over prey. As they compared their observations, each discovered details the others had missed.

The discussion made Seren think about the person she had been when they first set out. She had imagined the road ahead as a line only she could perceive, and feared that without her inward guidance everyone traveling with her would be lost. Yet Kael had found water by reading the birds, Elowen had recognized an unfamiliar wound through knowledge learned at the Order, and Seren herself had managed to deflect an attacking wolf. Perhaps she had never been meant to carry every decision alone. Traveling together could mean allowing another person to lead, as well as being ready to help when that person needed her.

Crossing a low stretch of farmland, she stopped to look at wildflowers bending beside the road. She remembered Elowen's words at the crossroads: clouds could hide the sun without taking it away. At the time she had tried hard to believe them. After the wolves, she was less willing to speak of faith as a feeling of unbroken calm. Fear had been real. So had doubt. Remembering a wise saying had not made either disappear. Yet she had continued forward, held her staff, and trusted the people beside her. Perhaps growing stronger did not mean becoming incapable of fear. Perhaps it meant making a choice while fear was still present.

Kael waited for her to catch up and asked whether she was still thinking about the wolf. Seren admitted that she did not know how to regard it. It had nearly hurt them; they had been forced to kill it. Yet she still felt sorrow for it. After a silence Kael said that he, too, had felt that way the first time he killed an animal. Over years of travel he had learned to hide such thoughts. Compassion did not make a person weak, he told her. The danger lay in hesitating so long that one failed to protect the living, or in deciding that because killing had been necessary, the life taken was no longer worth mourning. His answer did not untangle all her feelings, but it helped her understand that grief was not a mistake.

Near dusk they passed a little stone shelter beside the road. Weather had worn the inscription carved into it, and a broken clay vessel lay near the entrance. Elowen studied the remaining words and said they seemed to urge travelers to remember the way home. She spoke again of the elderly sister at the Order who stood by the gate whenever someone departed, watching until the traveler disappeared from sight. Seren asked whether such concern had ever felt burdensome. Elowen smiled. When she was younger, it had. Later she had learned that being remembered was not always a restraint. Sometimes it gave a person the courage to go farther.

Seren thought of the many times she had believed she needed a clear answer before she deserved to continue toward Riverport. Their destination was still distant and many questions remained unresolved, yet for the first time she did not feel compelled to ask why the guidance had fallen silent. Wind moved across the hills, carrying the scent of river water and damp soil. She lifted her head as several white birds rose from the riverbank and turned east beneath the evening sky. At that moment, the stillness she had missed for so long returned to her.

It did not overwhelm her or show her a new road. It did not explain the strange mark on the wolves. It was more like the quiet return of a familiar companion who neither reproached her uncertainty nor demanded that she defend the fear she had felt. Seren slowed, tears gathering unexpectedly in her eyes. She said nothing. She looked toward the open sky and smiled faintly. The stillness had not erased what had happened in the wood, but within it she knew she had not been abandoned there.

Kael noticed first that the footsteps behind him had regained their steady rhythm. When he looked back and saw Seren's face, he did not ask what had changed. Their journey had taught him that protection did not always mean asking questions. Sometimes it meant allowing another person the privacy of silence. Elowen noticed too. She remembered the accounts of spiritual encounters preserved in the Order's manuscripts and felt a quiet expectation stirring, though she would not presume to interpret Seren's experience for her. They walked on as the setting sun lengthened their shadows, closer to one another than they had been when the journey began.

When the light softened, Kael stopped on a rise to judge their direction. Beyond the hills, several rivers caught the dusk like strands of silver. The air had taken on the damp scent of a great harbor still hidden beyond the horizon. If the road remained open, he told them, Riverport lay only a day or two ahead. Seren looked into the distance without demanding reassurance that they had chosen correctly. The peace within her remained, and tomorrow, whatever lay ahead, they would face the road together.
