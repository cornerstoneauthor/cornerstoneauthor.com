CHAPTER 31

The Road Beneath the Clouds

← PreviousPart II ContentsNext →

Kael was already awake when the first light of morning reached the wooden window of the wayhouse. He sat on the edge of his bed for a moment, listening to an occasional crackle from the embers in the hearth below. Then he put on his cloak, tightened his sword belt, and left the room without a sound. At the bend in the stairs, he paused to listen to the yard: a horse shifting its hooves in the dirt, a wooden rail creaking in the breeze, and no unfamiliar footsteps. The suspicious marks he had noticed near the stable the previous night still troubled him. Only after finding no fresh tracks or wagon ruts did he allow himself to relax a little.

Dew clung to the grass in the courtyard. The horse that had been so weary the night before was standing again, grazing beside the fence. It raised its head to sniff Kael's hand when he approached, then returned to its breakfast as though nothing in the world concerned it. The innkeeper emerged from the kitchen carrying a basket of bread and smiled at the sight of him. “I knew you'd be the first one up. People who travel far never like wasting a morning in bed.” He offered Kael a warm loaf. Kael thanked him and reached for his money, but the man shook his head. It was a gift, he said, for the help he had given the previous night. He had evidently heard about the stable, though he asked no questions. They ate beside the fence, watching sunlight spread across the stone road to Riverport, and spoke of the waystations ahead and the unrest troubling the north that year.

“Fine weather brings more travelers onto the road,” the innkeeper observed. “But a crowded road isn't always a safe one. When merchants hear there's trouble somewhere, they all change their routes together. An empty road can be more worrying than a busy one.” Kael remembered the warning. He had never made a habit of believing every rumor carried by travelers, but when similar reports came from different directions, they deserved attention. Before returning to the kitchen, the innkeeper told him to let his companions sleep a little longer. Kael agreed, then stayed outside to refill the waterskins and examine the straps of their packs and his sword sheath. For him, looking after others often began with such ordinary, easily overlooked tasks.

By the time Seren and Elowen came downstairs, several unfamiliar travelers had taken seats in the dining room. The merchants who had spoken of the northern calamity the night before were gone; only faint rings left by their cups remained on the table. The innkeeper's wife brought hot soup, eggs, and smoked meat. Looking at the vacant table, Seren remarked that the room had seemed so lively yesterday, yet now it was as if those people had never been there. The woman smiled. A wayhouse, she said, was a place where people stopped only for a while. Everyone arrived carrying a story of their own and departed with fragments of someone else's. Elowen glanced toward the road beyond the window, perhaps remembering the letters that had reached the Order from distant places. She said nothing of her thoughts, merely urged Seren to eat while the soup was hot.

After leaving the wayhouse, the three followed the road through rolling hills. Kael walked ahead, occasionally glancing back to see whether Elowen's injured arm was making her pack difficult to carry. Seren watched the markers along the roadside, trying to match the place-names she had heard the night before with the branches of the road before them. Morning had always been the time when she most readily felt the quiet guidance that had accompanied her journey. Today, however, it seemed to have withdrawn beyond her reach. At first she blamed weariness and tried to steady her breathing as she waited for it to return. At a second turning she stopped again, and again there was no familiar answer. She told neither companion, but her unease grew with every mile.

Near midday the road divided at the edge of a wood. One branch ran east along the river valley, its surface smooth and marked by clear wagon tracks; the other crossed a sparsely wooded slope, where an old signpost stood beside a path that showed little evidence of recent use. Seren looked from one to the other. Both appeared capable of leading toward their destination, yet she could not choose. At last she admitted, “I don't know which way we should go.”

Kael did not press her. He examined the signpost, walked a short distance along each road, and studied the tracks in the earth. When he returned, Seren confessed that at other crossings she had always felt some sense of direction, but today she could not perceive even the faintest response. She wondered whether she had done something wrong, or whether what she had trusted had been nothing more than chance.

Elowen made no attempt to supply an immediate answer. She spoke instead of the first time she had left the Order of the Sacred Lamp. An elderly sister had accompanied her to the gate beneath a sky heavy with clouds. Pointing toward the hidden sun, the woman had said, “One day you may feel that it has vanished. Don't be too quick to believe your eyes. The clouds conceal the sun from your sight; they do not make it cease to exist.” At the time, Elowen had taken the words for ordinary comfort. Only later, when difficulties found her far from the Order, had she understood how easily people mistook what they could not see for what was no longer there. She spoke gently, without presenting her own experience as proof that she could make Seren's choice for her.

Kael listened, then turned toward the road through the river valley. “Yesterday we didn't know what was ahead of us either,” he said. “But we found the wayhouse, and we met Elowen. None of that becomes untrue because you're uncertain today.”

“Then you still believe I can lead us?” Seren asked.

Kael shook his head. “I believe you've always taken our journey seriously. As for today, it's my turn to read the road.” He pointed to the wagon ruts on the left. Heavy carts had passed that way recently, he explained, and a route following the river offered better chances of finding water and settlements. It was no guarantee of safety, but it was a choice they could explain and take responsibility for.

They chose the valley road. The familiar peace did not return to Seren. Indeed, she felt almost ashamed of the hollow uncertainty that accompanied her first steps. Yet Kael did not treat her silence as weakness, and Elowen did not insist that she recover her confidence at once. After a while Seren began to see that she did not need to prove the source of every step she took. Roads still had to be observed, food and water prepared, and tired companions cared for. Such ordinary responsibilities did not disappear because she was troubled by questions she could not answer. She adjusted her pack on her shoulders and followed.

That afternoon Kael noticed several white waterbirds flying low across a hillside. He led them toward a narrow trail almost hidden in the grass, and half an hour later they came upon a clear stream at the edge of the trees. They washed the dust from their hands, refilled their waterskins, and let Elowen rest. Watching the water curl around the stones, Seren asked Kael how he could so often find answers in the smallest signs. He drew a whetstone along his blade and said he did not always find the right answer. Years on the road had merely taught him to begin with what he could actually see before deciding what to do next.

Elowen recalled a stream behind the Order's buildings. As a child she had thought it unremarkable, but after spending time away she had come to miss the sound of its water and the bells at dusk. Seren asked whether she wanted to return. Elowen admitted that she did, though she did not regret leaving. Each time she traveled and came back, familiar things seemed to carry meanings she had never noticed before. Kael slid his sword into its sheath. “Perhaps the place hasn't changed,” he said. “But the person who returns has.” Seren was quiet for a long while. She had once understood a journey chiefly as the act of reaching a destination. Now she was beginning to suspect that the road was changing her as well.

Before they left, Seren looked once more toward the stream. Several times she had wanted to ask Elowen whether never hearing that guidance again would mean the entire journey had been founded on a misunderstanding. But Elowen had her own faith and her own doubts; she could not answer every question on Seren’s behalf. They were able to travel together precisely because neither needed to pretend to understand everything. Seren kept the question to herself for now, until she had something she could speak of honestly with her companions.

When they set out again, Seren did not linger beside the stream waiting for a sign. She glanced back at the water glinting in the sun, then followed her companions. The stream ran between the stones without stopping for anyone, while somewhere beyond the country they could see, a harder stretch of road awaited them.
