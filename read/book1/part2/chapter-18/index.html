CHAPTER 18

The Carpenter's Table

“Please, wait a moment.” The voice came from across the street. It was neither loud nor commanding, yet the young husband stopped with his hands already resting on the edge of the oak table.

Everyone turned toward the doorway.

Two men were crossing the street. The older of them appeared to be in his fifties, dressed in a dark brown coat with neatly arranged cuffs. A wooden badge was fastened to his chest, bearing the carving of an oak tree above a crossed carpenter's rule and mallet. The younger man walking behind him was perhaps thirty years of age and wore the same badge.

Neither man seemed in any particular hurry. They approached without raising their voices or carrying themselves as though they had come to make trouble.

The older man stopped at the entrance and inclined his head toward the young couple before greeting the carpenter. “Good afternoon.”

“Good afternoon,” the carpenter replied.

The older man stepped inside. He did not immediately explain why he had come. Instead, he approached the oak table and laid a hand upon its surface. His fingers followed the grain of the wood, lingering briefly where two boards had been joined. Then he bent to examine the legs and gave one corner a firm shake.

The table did not move. Straightening, he nodded with unmistakable approval. “Fine work. The joints are tight, and you've chosen your timber well.” He walked around the table once more, studying it from every side, before turning toward the young carpenter. “Did you make this yourself?”

“Yes, sir.”

The older man nodded again. For a moment he seemed to be considering something. Then he said, “Your work has improved since last year.”

The carpenter offered a small smile but said nothing.

The older man glanced down at the table legs once more. “Where is the guild mark?” The question changed the atmosphere of the workshop.

The carpenter did not answer.

The older man seemed to have expected his silence. He sighed, examined the table once again, and finally straightened. “This table,” he began, “has the sale already been completed?”

The young husband stepped forward. “I've paid for it.”

The older man turned toward him. There was no hostility in his expression. “I'm afraid, sir, that you cannot take it away just yet.”

The husband stared at him. “Why not?”

Rather than answer immediately, the older man reached inside his coat and produced a small, leather-bound book. Its cover was worn from years of handling, and he opened it with the ease of someone who knew precisely where to find the passage he wanted.

He held the page out for the husband to see. “Article Thirty-Seven of the Rivergate Carpenters' Guild Regulations,” he read. “Any item of furniture valued at more than twenty silver coins must be made by a registered guild member and bear the guild's official mark before it may be offered for public sale.” He closed the book. His voice remained courteous. “This rule is not directed against any particular person. It applies to every carpenter in Rivergate.”

The young husband frowned. “But the table is already finished.”

“Yes,” the older man agreed.

“And I've already paid for it.”

“Yes.”

“I want to buy it.”

“Yes.”

The husband looked at him, increasingly bewildered. “Then why can't he sell it to me?”

The older man was silent for a moment. He placed his hand upon the polished tabletop and gave it a gentle pat. “Because it has no guild mark.”

A hush fell over the workshop.

Outside, the wind passed beneath the eaves, stirring the little wooden bird hanging near the doorway. Its wings moved faintly before settling again.

The young carpenter stood beside his workbench without speaking. He made no attempt to interrupt or defend himself, as though he had known from the beginning that this moment would eventually come.

The husband looked from the older man to the table and back again. For an instant, he seemed almost uncertain that he had understood correctly. “But this is the table I bought,” he said.

“I know.”

“And the money came from my own purse.”

“I know.”

“And he's willing to sell it to me.”

“I know.”

Each answer was delivered with the same calm assurance. The older man did not argue, nor did he raise his voice. He acknowledged every fact the husband presented, and yet the conclusion remained unchanged.

That, more than anything, seemed to trouble the younger man. “If we both agree to the sale,” he asked, “why should anyone stop us?”

The guild elder glanced toward the sign hanging outside the workshop across the street. It bore the same oak tree and crossed tools as the badge upon his coat. “Because this concerns more than the two of you.”

He turned back to the husband. “Every piece of furniture sold in Rivergate reflects upon the reputation of our carpenters. If we permit furniture without a guild mark to be sold openly today, tomorrow someone may offer work that is not nearly so good. The day after that, another man may begin cutting corners. Before long, people will be buying poorly made furniture without knowing who should be held responsible when something goes wrong.” He spoke with complete conviction.

Seren, who had remained near the doorway, could hear no dishonesty in his words. The older man truly believed that the guild existed to protect the families who purchased furniture in the city.

The husband lowered his eyes to the oak table. After a moment, he gave a short, disbelieving laugh. “But this isn't badly made furniture.”

“No,” the elder replied. He nodded toward the table. “In fact, it's better than many tables that do bear the guild mark.”

A murmur passed through the people who had begun gathering outside.

The husband stared at him. “Then why?”

The elder drew a slow breath. This time, his eyes settled upon the young carpenter. “Because he is not a member of the guild.”

Everyone looked toward the young man.

He stood quietly by the doorway, neither avoiding their attention nor attempting to explain himself. He merely brushed a little sawdust from his hands, as though the matter being discussed concerned someone else.

The husband turned to him. “Why haven't you joined?”

The carpenter hesitated, then gave a faint smile. “I've applied.”

“When?”

“Three years ago.”

“And what happened?”

“I applied again the following year. Then once more at the beginning of this year.”

The husband frowned. “Three times?”

“Aye.”

“And each time?”

“I submitted my work. I passed the examinations.” He spoke without bitterness, in the same matter-of-fact tone he had used earlier when explaining the little wooden bird to Seren.

The husband seemed more confused than ever. He turned back to the guild elder. “If he passed the examinations, why wasn't he admitted?”

For the first time, the older man did not answer at once. He looked down and straightened the little book in his hands, carefully aligning its worn cover with the pages beneath it.

A long silence passed before he spoke.

“Because there are no vacancies this year.”

The street grew quiet. The husband blinked. “No vacancies?”

The elder nodded. “Each master carpenter is permitted to train only a fixed number of apprentices, and the number of full guild members has long been kept within established limits. These regulations were made many years ago to ensure that every carpenter admitted to the guild has properly learned his craft.”

The husband listened. Then he slowly turned toward the carpenter, glanced at the finished oak table, and looked back at the elder. At last, he asked the question that seemed to have been waiting beneath everything else. “Then how much longer must he wait?”

The carpenter looked down at the grain of the wood. A small smile crossed his face. “I don't know.” He spoke the three words quietly.

Yet for a moment, no one in the street seemed able to say anything in reply.

The young husband remained silent for a long while. He looked at the table, then at the carpenter standing near the workshop door, before finally turning back to the guild elder. “So his skill is good enough.”

The elder nodded. “It is.”

“And the table he's made is good enough.”

“Yes.”

“And I'm willing to buy it.”

“Yes.”

The husband took a deep breath. The confusion that had troubled him earlier was slowly giving way to anger, though he was plainly struggling to keep it under control. “Then who,” he asked, “is unwilling?”

No one answered.

The elder stood motionless, the worn book still in his hands. The carpenter said nothing either.

The husband gave a brief laugh, but there was no amusement in it. “Yesterday, I told my wife something.” He placed his hand upon the table. “I told her we were finally going to have a table of our own. Our child would eat his meals here. He'd learn to write here. And when he was grown, perhaps he'd sit at this very table and carve little wooden toys for his own children.” His voice grew quieter as he spoke. He looked down at the polished oak, then gently patted its surface. “I thought I'd already bought it.”

When he raised his head again, his eyes were fixed upon the elder. “Now you're telling me that the table isn't badly made. The carpenter knows his trade, and I've paid the price he asked. None of those things is in dispute.”

He paused, searching for words that might make sense of what he had heard. “And yet I can't take it home, simply because…” His gaze moved toward the wooden badge on the elder's coat. “Because he doesn't have a mark?”

The elder did not deny it. He merely nodded.

For a long moment, the husband studied him. Then he asked another question.

“When the guild was first established, what was it meant to do?”

The elder seemed taken aback. He considered the question before answering. “To ensure that every family could purchase reliable furniture, and that every carpenter would be held responsible for the work of his own hands.”

The husband nodded slowly. “I believe that.”

There was no mockery in his voice. “I think that's a good thing, too.”

He lifted the leather purse from the table and held it in one hand. With the other, he gestured toward the oak table before them. “But today, I want to buy a reliable table. This man wants to earn his living through the work of his hands. And you've already admitted that what he's made is good.” He looked directly at the elder. When he spoke again, his voice was quieter, yet every word could be heard by those gathered in the street. “So tell me—who is being protected today?”

No one answered.

A breeze moved along the street, stirring the wooden bird hanging outside the workshop. Its little wings opened and closed before coming to rest once more.

The guild elder lowered his head. He held the worn book of regulations in both hands. For a long while, he said nothing. When he finally spoke, there was a weariness in his voice that had not been there before. “My boy, it is the rules I am bound to uphold.”

The husband looked at him and slowly shook his head. “But weren't those rules made to protect us in the first place?”

The elder's lips moved, yet no answer came.

The whole street had fallen so quiet that the only sound was the wind passing over the scattered wood shavings. For a time, no one moved.

Then the young carpenter stepped forward.

He picked up the heavy purse from the table and held it out to the husband with both hands. “I'm sorry.”

The husband did not immediately take it.

The carpenter offered it a little farther, his expression still composed. There was neither resentment nor anger upon his face. He looked like a man carrying out something difficult because he knew it had to be done.

At last, the husband reached for the purse. The leather settled heavily into his palm.

He looked down at it and gave a bitter little smile. “Seems we'll have to go across the street after all.” He turned toward the guild shop opposite the workshop.

Its frontage was broad, with large windows displaying tables much like the one before them. The prices, however, were considerably higher. His wife took his hand. The child in her arms was still looking at the oak table, unaware of what had passed between the adults. The husband stood there for another moment before giving a reluctant nod. “Come on.” The three of them began walking across the street.

As they passed the young carpenter, the husband stopped and placed a hand upon his shoulder. “It's a fine table.”

The carpenter smiled. “Thank you.”

It was the faintest smile he had offered all afternoon.

The couple entered the guild shop, and the little brass bell above its door gave a cheerful ring. Almost immediately, someone came forward to greet them. The young carpenter remained where he was until the door closed behind the family. Then he turned and walked back into his workshop. He did not return to his carving knife. Instead, he took hold of the oak table and carefully moved it back against the wall. He worked slowly, as though he were returning something that had briefly found a place in the world, only to be left without one again. When he had finished, the table stood exactly where it had stood that morning. Sunlight still fell across its polished surface, bringing out the warmth of the grain. Nothing about the wood had changed. The workshop, however, had grown quiet once more.

Outside, the gathering crowd gradually dispersed. Some shook their heads. Others sighed, while a few continued on their way as though nothing unusual had happened.

The guild elder remained for a little while. At last, he gave the young carpenter a small nod.

He said nothing before turning away. As he walked down the street, his shoulders seemed a little more stooped than when he had arrived.

Seren had remained near the corner throughout the exchange. Even after the street returned to its ordinary bustle, she did not immediately move. She lowered her eyes to the little wooden bird in her hands. She had been holding it all this time without quite realizing it. With one finger, she moved its wing. The two pieces of wood slowly opened, then folded together again. The simple motion reminded her unexpectedly of home, and of the people with whom she had grown up.

There had been quarrels there, too, and misunderstandings. People did not always agree, and sometimes harsh words were spoken. Yet more often than not, if they were willing to sit down together, they could eventually find a way to speak openly.

Here, things seemed different.

Rivergate had its rules, its institutions, and its marks of approval. Each person appeared to be carrying out his own duty with complete seriousness. The carpenter had made a fine table. The couple had agreed to buy it. The elder had upheld the regulations entrusted to him. And in the end, each of them had walked away with something lost.

Seren could not decide who was truly at fault.

The carpenter had done nothing wrong. Neither had the young couple. And the elder, for all the pain his decision had caused, seemed genuinely convinced that he was doing what was right. Yet the thought left a weight upon her heart, as though the unsold table had somehow become a burden she was carrying herself.

She lifted her head.

The wind came along the street, passing beneath the eaves and stirring the sawdust before brushing against the loose strands of her hair. Then, without warning, the quiet presence that had accompanied her on the road returned. It offered no words and no explanation. There was no answer to the questions that troubled her, nor any judgment upon the people she had just encountered.

Yet the feeling was unmistakable. It was almost as though someone had gently placed a hand upon her shoulder, asking nothing of her except that she continue.

Seren let out a slow breath.

She closed her fingers around the wooden bird and looked back toward the little workshop. The carpenter had returned to his place beside the window. His head was bent over the workbench, and once again he was carving a piece of wood. Whatever had happened that afternoon, it had not taken from him the reason to continue his work.

Seren did not go back inside. She did not call out a farewell. Instead, she placed the little bird carefully in her traveling bag, turned away from the workshop, and continued along the street of artisans.

The afternoon sunlight lay across the paving stones ahead of her. And Rivergate still held many stories she had yet to encounter.
