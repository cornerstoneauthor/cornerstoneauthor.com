CHAPTER 15

That Man

The officer said nothing.

He rose and slipped the cracked copper seal inside his cloak. His movements were unhurried, almost careful, as though he were still making certain of what he had found. Then he leaned toward the two soldiers behind him and spoke in a voice too low for Seren to hear.

One soldier nodded and hurried toward the end of the street.

The officer stayed.

His gaze passed slowly over everyone nearby: the merchants outside the tavern, the old woman selling cheese, the cooper gathering his tools. Finally it settled on Seren—or, more precisely, on the vegetable basket in her hands.

“Yours?” he asked.

Seren shook her head. “No.”

She returned the basket to the old woman beside her.

“It’s mine,” the woman said quickly.

The officer nodded. “The man who ran through here knocked you down?”

“Yes.”

“Did he say anything?”

“Nothing.”

The officer was quiet for a moment. Then he looked at Seren.

“And you? What did you see?”

Seren thought back. “He kept hold of a cloth bundle the whole time.”

The officer gave a small nod and wrote the answer on his parchment. When he finished, he blew lightly across the wet ink and rolled the sheet closed.

“If you remember anything else, go to the South Bridge guard post.”

He inclined his head to the old woman and left with the remaining soldier.

People watched them until they disappeared around the corner. Only then did conversation begin again inside the tavern, as though the whole street had been holding a breath it did not wish to admit taking.

The old woman sighed.

“Girl, thank you.”

Seren smiled. “I’ll walk with you for a while. Your ankle still doesn’t look right.”

They went slowly, keeping close to the edge of the street. The farther they moved from Gate Street, the quieter Rivergate became. Taverns and caravan yards gave way to ordinary homes. Clay pots stood on windowsills. Split firewood was stacked beside doors. An old man sat outside repairing a fishing net, while from a nearby courtyard came the steady wooden rhythm of a loom.

After a time the woman glanced at Seren.

“You’re not from here.”

“No.”

“First time in Rivergate?”

“Yes.”

The old woman smiled. “I was younger than you when I first came. There weren’t half so many houses on this street then.”

She paused to catch her breath and looked toward the tall bell tower in the distance.

“More people every year. More boats. More trade. Business has never been better.” Her voice trailed off. “But some things...”

She shook her head and left the sentence unfinished.

They continued.

At a small stone bridge, an elderly man sold warm cider from a wooden barrel. A porter stopped, drank from a cup, and left two copper coins. The seller did not count them before dropping them into a box.

A little girl in worn clothes came to the stall. She said nothing. She only looked at the barrel.

The old seller looked at her once, asked no questions, and filled half a cup with warm cider. Then he broke off a piece of dark rye bread and put both into her hands.

“Thank you,” the girl whispered.

She ran away clutching the bread.

The woman beside Seren smiled. “Ed always does that. He keeps whatever bread he doesn’t sell. By evening, there’s always someone who needs it.”

Seren watched the cider seller lower his head and begin wiping a cup, as though nothing at all had happened.

Wind came off the river and moved the willow branches beside the bridge.

Rivergate went on with its life.

Less than a quarter hour earlier, a young man had fled through these streets with guards behind him. A cracked copper seal had fallen between the stones. No one here knew what it meant, or how many lives might eventually be drawn together because of it.

When the guard went inside, the wooden door closed softly behind him, leaving Seren standing outside.

Beyond it, the hall was busy with movement. People crossed carrying rolled parchments. Others stopped at long tables to speak quietly with clerks. A porter set two heavy wooden crates against a wall, wiped sweat from his brow, and hurried away again.

No one in the hall seemed idle. Each person moved like a part of some great mechanism whose purpose Seren could not yet see.

Her attention drifted to the far side of the room.

A stone stair descended into shadow. An iron gate stood partly closed at its foot. Beside it sat an old jailer with white hair. He wore no armor, only a faded dark-blue coat. A heavy ring of keys rested across his knees, and he was cleaning them one by one.

Metal touched metal with small, clear sounds.

The old man noticed Seren watching. He raised his head, followed her gaze toward the iron gate, and smiled.

“First time?”

Seren nodded.

He set the keys back across his knees. “Most people don’t like looking that way. You’re different, girl.”

“Is that... the prison?” Seren asked.

The old man did not answer immediately. He looked toward the gate for a while.

“In a manner of speaking. Most of the time it’s simply a place where people wait. Some wait for judgment. Some wait for family to bring money. And some...” He paused. “Some wait for a chance no one knows will ever come.”

He shook his head with a faint smile, as though he had said more than he intended.

Footsteps sounded beyond the iron gate.

They were neither hurried nor hesitant.

The jailer rose at once and hung the keys from his belt.

The gate opened.

A man came slowly up the steps carrying an empty wooden bucket. His clothes were as plain as they could be, gray homespun with the sleeves rolled to his elbows. Several used rags lay in the bucket.

As he passed the doorway, he stopped.

He set the bucket down, bent, and pressed a loose paving stone back into place beside the threshold. The movement was practiced, as though he had done the same thing many times before.

The old jailer smiled. “Loose again?”

The man nodded. “Rain yesterday. Water got into the foundation.”

He lifted the bucket and turned to go.

Then his eyes met Seren’s.

There was no surprise in his face, no question and no suspicion. They simply looked at one another for a quiet moment.

Seren could not have explained why, but she felt at once that he was unlike many of the people she had met on the road. It was not his clothing, nor anything remarkable in his expression. It was the way he looked at another person. There was no weighing, no defense, no attempt to decide what she might want from him.

His gaze reminded her of deep water—still enough to reflect the person standing before it.

The man gave the smallest nod in greeting.

Then he carried the bucket back down the steps.

The iron gate closed. A key turned with a single hard click.

The noise of the hall swallowed the moment.

The jailer sat again and looked toward the closed gate. He sighed.

“Pity.”

The word was so quiet that it seemed meant only for himself.

Seren could not help asking, “What did he do?”

The old man watched the clerks and messengers moving through the hall. At last he shook his head.

“Girl, live in this city a few years and you’ll learn something. Some people come through that gate because they did wrong.” His eyes returned to the stair. “And some come through because they did what was right.”

Before Seren could ask more, the officer returned from the inner rooms carrying an open parchment.

“Forgive the wait.”

Seren shook her head.

He glanced down at the record. “There have been developments in yesterday’s matter. We’ve confirmed who lost the copper seal, but there are still several people we need to question separately.”

His tone held neither accusation nor suspicion. He sounded like a man dealing with one piece of ordinary civic business among many.

“Under city procedure, until the matter is clarified, we need to keep you here overnight.” He paused. “It is not imprisonment. Only procedure. It should be finished by morning.”

Seren was silent for a moment, then nodded. “All right.”

The officer seemed relieved. “Thank you for cooperating.”

He turned toward the far side of the hall.

“Evan.”

The white-haired jailer looked up.

“Take the girl to the holding room.”

Evan rose and fastened the keys at his belt. “Come with me.”

They crossed the hall.

Nothing in the room changed because Seren was being led away. Clerks bent over ledgers. A tax officer checked the quantity of wool on a cart. Several merchants stood around a map arguing over passage along the river. Seren was only one matter among hundreds, another line to be written, checked, and set aside.

Evan did not take her through the main doorway. He led her around the rear of the hall and into a narrow stone corridor lit by oil lamps. The walls had been worn smooth in places by age. The air grew cooler as they went deeper inside.

“This used to be an old storehouse,” he said. “Later they turned it into holding rooms. Most people who come here don’t stay long. Caravan disputes. Identities that need checking. Young fools who drink too much and start fighting in the street.”

He smiled. “Sleep easy, girl. Most wake the next morning and go home.”

Seren smiled faintly in return.

The corridor continued until a heavy iron door appeared ahead. Two lamps burned beside it, their light shining weakly on the metal ring.

Evan stopped and took a key from his belt, but he did not use it.

“Now that,” he said calmly, “is the real prison. Your room is beside it.”

He opened a plain wooden door instead.

Inside stood a narrow bed, a small table, and one high window scarcely larger than a book. Through it Seren could see only a strip of gray-white sky.

Evan returned the key to his belt.

“If you need anything, knock. Supper will come later.”

He had just turned away when a sound came from beyond the neighboring iron door.

No shouting. No quarrel.

Only the soft thump of a bucket being set down.

Evan looked back. His expression held equal parts resignation and fondness.

“Can’t keep still,” he muttered.

Seren followed his gaze.

The iron door was not fully closed. Through the narrow opening she saw a figure bending to place a bucket of clean water against the wall. Then the man picked up a worn broom and began sweeping rainwater from the stone steps.

He worked slowly, carefully, as though this were not a prison at all but simply the place where he happened to live and therefore a place worth keeping in order.

Evan sighed again.

“Girl, you asked me what that man did.”

He watched the figure beyond the door for a long time.

“If I hadn’t seen him walk into this place with my own eyes, I still wouldn’t believe it.”

Seren said nothing.

Neither did Evan for several moments. The corridor had grown very quiet. From somewhere above came the muffled movement of the hall: a chair scraping, a voice calling for a clerk, the dull weight of a crate being set down. Down here, each sound arrived softened by stone. The man beyond the gate seemed untouched by any of it. He drew the broom through the shallow water, gathered it toward the drain, then returned for what remained. Nothing in his manner suggested a man trying to be noticed. He worked because the water was there and someone had to clear it.

Seren thought of the first time their eyes had met. She had expected a prisoner to look angry, ashamed, frightened, or at least watchful. Instead she had found a stillness she could not name. The old jailer’s pity only deepened the question.

The broom continued to move over stone.

“At one time,” the old jailer said at last, “he didn’t spend his days walking this corridor.”

He raised a hand and pointed toward the far end of the passage, where the stairs led back to the hall above.

“Two years ago, that was where he walked. Upstairs.”

Evan’s eyes remained on the man with the broom.

“Everyone in the city hall knew him.”
