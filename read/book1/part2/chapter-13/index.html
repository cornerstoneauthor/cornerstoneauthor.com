CHAPTER 13

The Gate

← PreviousPart II ContentsNext →

The morning mist had not yet burned away, but the road was already crowded.

Seren moved with the stream of travelers toward the city. Somewhere during the last few miles, the narrow country path had widened into a broad road of packed earth and stone. Smaller tracks fed into it from both sides, bringing more people with them: an old man carrying firewood, farmers leading ox carts, a woman balancing baskets of eggs, young merchants on short-legged horses urging their mounts carefully through the press.

“Make way.”

“Coming through.”

“We need to reach the morning market.”

No one seemed offended. People shifted aside, closed the gap after the riders passed, and kept walking.

Then Seren looked up.

A pale gray shape was emerging through the mist ahead. At first she mistook it for a cliff. As the road drew nearer, however, the line became too straight, too deliberate, and far too high.

It was not a mountain.

It was a wall.

The city wall stretched away in both directions until the haze swallowed it. Beneath it, the crowd gathered like a slow river narrowing toward a single opening.

Seren stopped without meaning to.

She had never seen a wall so high. Massive gray stones had been fitted one upon another, moss growing in some of the seams. The surface carried marks of age and violence: long scars that might have come from blades or tools, patches blackened as though by fire, and stones split through yet still locked firmly into the greater weight of the wall.

Dark banners moved above the battlements. At intervals guards stood with bows across their backs and spears in hand, looking down upon the road.

No one below spoke loudly, yet the approach to the gate was full of noise. Wagon wheels groaned over stone. Oxen lowed. Children complained. Merchants argued over prices before they had even entered the market. Iron tools struck one another in a smith’s bundle with every step he took. The smells were just as crowded: wet earth, animals, smoke, charcoal, sweat.

All of it flowed toward the gate.

The closer Seren came, the slower the line moved. At last she could see more than a dozen guards stationed around the entrance. Everyone who entered was stopped. Some were questioned, some searched, some waved through after only a few words.

No one simply walked in.

Ahead of Seren, an elderly man waited beside a donkey cart carrying four wooden barrels. When his turn came, two guards approached. One struck a barrel with the butt of his spear.

A dull thump answered.

The guard frowned and struck it again. “Open it.”

The old man nodded quickly. His hands shook as he worked the stopper free. The smell of wine spread into the air.

The guard did not bother to smell it. He thrust his arm inside, feeling as far down as he could, then repeated the search with the next barrel and the next. The old man stood beside the cart, speaking under his breath as if explaining himself, but neither guard answered.

After the fourth barrel, the old man visibly relaxed.

Then the second guard bent and pulled aside a rag beneath the cart.

A small wooden box had been hidden there.

The color drained from the old man’s face.

The guard opened it. Inside were only several pieces of dried meat and a few worn garments. He looked through them, closed the box, and replaced the rag.

“Go on.”

“Thank you. Thank you.”

The old man pulled the donkey forward. After two steps he stopped, glanced to either side, and took a faded scrap of cloth from inside his coat. He unfolded it carefully. Three copper coins lay within.

He did not hand them to the guard. He placed them on the rim of one barrel, lowered his eyes, and moved on.

The guard never looked at him. Only after the cart had passed did he sweep the three coins into a small leather pouch at his belt.

No one in line reacted.

A young merchant nearby even yawned and returned to the figures in his account book, as though the exchange were as ordinary as opening a gate at sunrise.

The line moved forward.

A young mother stood in front of Seren with a child of three or four in her arms. The boy had grown restless and kept twisting to be put down. His mother patted his back.

“Soon. We’re almost there.”

The child stared up at the wall. “Mother, why is it so high?”

She followed his pointing finger and smiled after a moment. “So bad people can’t get in.”

The boy accepted this solemnly and settled against her shoulder.

Seren looked at the wall again.

Before she could decide what she thought of the answer, hoofbeats sounded behind them.

“Move! Clear the road!”

The cry came closer at speed.

Everyone turned. A troop of mounted men was charging toward the gate. They did not slow for the waiting crowd. People scattered instinctively to both sides. A basket fell. Apples rolled across the road. An old man bent to retrieve them, and someone seized his shoulder and pulled him back just before several horses thundered past close enough to stir his coat.

Dust rose around him.

The riders reached the gate. The guards straightened at once. Two soldiers dragged the wooden barrier aside, and the entire troop rode through without stopping.

Only after the hoofbeats faded inside the city did the crowd close again.

People picked up the old man’s apples and returned them to his basket. He murmured thanks and resumed his place in line. No one shouted after the riders. No one demanded an apology. A man carrying firewood brushed dust from his clothes, settled the pole across his shoulders, and faced forward.

It was as though the horses had been a gust of wind—something one made room for because one had learned long ago that there was no point standing against it.

Seren kept watching the gate.

What stayed with her was not the speed of the riders but the speed with which everyone had moved aside. It had not looked like sudden fear. It looked like habit.

The line advanced again.

A dispute broke out near the other side of the entrance.

“Why wasn’t his carriage searched?”

A foreign merchant pointed toward an ornate coach that had just passed through. His face was flushed with anger. “They turned my wagon over three times. No one even lifted his curtain.”

A guard looked at him without expression.

“Next.”

The merchant did not move. Someone behind him tugged at his sleeve.

“Leave it.”

He clenched his jaw. His eyes dropped to the sword at the guard’s waist. Whatever else he meant to say remained unsaid.

He pushed his cart forward.

When he passed Seren, he exhaled sharply. His hands gripped the handles harder than before.

Seren said nothing.

She looked from the high wall to the single gate and then to the endless movement of people beneath it. The road was crowded with hundreds of lives, yet all of them had to pass through the same narrow opening.

The sun climbed higher. More caravans appeared along the road. Men pushed barrows; mule trains joined the queue; young apprentices carried heavy crates between them, shifting the weight from shoulder to shoulder.

The guards continued at the same pace: inspect, record, release; inspect, record, release. No one hurried them. No one expected them to hurry. The order of the place seemed older than anyone standing in line.

Seren moved forward by small degrees until she could finally see the gate itself.

The doors were thicker than she had imagined, built from massive horizontal timbers studded with black iron. Rust stained some of the nails. Deep cracks ran through parts of the wood, and iron plates reinforced the lower corners. Countless wheels had polished the threshold smooth.

A man passing beside her slapped one of the doors with his palm. The sound was deep and hollow, like striking the trunk of an ancient tree.

Beyond the doors lay a long stone passage through the wall. Only a narrow strip of sky could be seen above. The air inside was cooler, and the wind made a low sound as it moved between the stone faces.

At the center stood the guards’ station: a long table, a thick ledger bound between wooden covers, boxes filled with small wooden tokens, and a spear bearing the city banner. Everything had its place.

A child suddenly began to cry.

It was the boy who had been in front of Seren. He had slipped from his mother’s hand. A little wooden windmill had rolled into the gate passage, and he crouched to run after it.

A guard lowered his spear across the way—not threateningly, but with finality.

“Don’t go in.”

The boy froze, eyes fixed on his toy.

His mother hurried forward, caught him, and bowed several times in apology.

The guard did not scold her. He bent, picked up the wooden windmill himself, brushed dust from it, and handed it back to the child.

“Thank you,” the boy whispered.

The guard returned to his place without answering.

Seren watched him more closely. He was perhaps thirty, with short stubble along his jaw and a faint old scar through his right brow. He stood straight, almost rigidly so, yet whenever children passed him he lifted the point of his spear a little higher without seeming to notice he was doing it.

Laughter sounded from the city side of the gate.

Several young men in fine robes rode out at an easy pace, talking among themselves and flicking their riding crops idly. As they passed the guards, one tossed a silver coin through the air.

The scarred guard caught it.

The riders laughed and continued down the road.

The guard looked at the coin, but instead of slipping it into his purse, he dropped it into the wooden box on the table and returned to the ledger.

Seren looked at the box, then remembered the three copper coins from the old man’s wine cart.

The line moved again.

Now Seren stood almost beneath the arch. Mud from the long road still clung to the edges of her boots. One step ahead, the ground changed to hard, level stone.

Sounds from inside the city reached her more clearly: a blacksmith’s hammer, distant bells, street vendors calling, people arguing, the warm smell of bread drifting from somewhere she could not see.

Only two people remained before her.

The first was an old farmer carrying two baskets of vegetables. A guard lifted the damp cloth covering them. Radishes, cabbages, and freshly cut greens filled the baskets. He moved them aside, pressed the bottom to check for a false compartment, then replaced the cloth.

“The same as usual today?”

The farmer grinned. “Same as usual.”

The guard made a mark in the ledger. “Go on.”

The farmer shouldered his pole and walked through with the ease of someone who had done so for years.

Next came a middle-aged man carrying a medicine chest. The guard opened it and found rows of small wooden boxes and packets of dried herbs. He lifted one packet and smelled it.

A bitter scent spread around the table.

Another guard laughed. “Still tastes as bad as it smells, I suppose.”

The medicine seller smiled. “If it weren’t bitter, no one would believe it worked.”

The guards laughed with him. The chest was closed, and the man entered the city.

Then Seren was alone at the front of the line.

She stepped forward.

The guard looked up and studied her briefly. Her pack bore the dust of the road. The edge of her cloak was worn pale, and dried mud remained on her boots. She did not look like a merchant, a farmer, or a laborer arriving in search of work.

He turned to a fresh page in the ledger and picked up a stick of charcoal.

“Name?”

“Seren.”

He wrote it down. “Where from?”

“White Bay.”

The charcoal paused.

A second guard leaned closer. “White Bay? The village on the coast?”

“Aye.”

He gave a small sound of recognition. “That’s a fair distance from here.”

Seren did not answer.

The first guard finished writing, blew the loose charcoal dust from the page, and looked up. It was probably a question he had asked a hundred times already that morning.

“What brings you to the city?”

For the first time since reaching the gate, Seren hesitated.

The blacksmith’s hammer sounded somewhere beyond the wall: one strike, then another. The guard waited. Behind her, a new line was already forming. Someone settled a cart into place; another traveler lowered a burden from his shoulder.

Seren looked at the man in front of her.

“I don’t know.”

The charcoal stopped in midair.

The guard looked at her again, this time more carefully.

The younger man beside him laughed before he could stop himself. “You don’t know? You don’t know why you came here?”

Several merchants behind Seren had heard. One murmured, amused, “That’s a new one.”

“Probably left home to see the world,” another said.

Someone else only shook his head.

Seren did not turn around. She did not invent an explanation or search for a more acceptable answer. She had told the truth.

The guard glanced at the ledger, then at the pack on her shoulders. After a moment he closed the book.

“First time here?”

“Aye.”

“Know the city rules?”

Seren shook her head.

He considered her for another moment, then reached into one of the boxes and took out a small wooden token. He placed it in her hand.

“Don’t lose this. Return it when you leave the city.”

Seren looked down.

The token was small and smooth at the edges from passing through countless hands. Two characters had been cut into its face: Rivergate. Beneath them was the city’s mark—a bridge and an open gate—and a shallow identifying number.

She closed her fingers around it.

“Thank you.”

The guard nodded and stepped half a pace aside.

“Go on.”

Seren lifted her foot and entered the long passage beneath the wall.

Her steps sounded sharper on stone than they had on the road. Sunlight still lay behind her outside the gate.

The light ahead came from another world.
