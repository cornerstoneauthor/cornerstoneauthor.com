CHAPTER 24

Traces of Peace

Seren set down the wooden bowl and pulled the blanket over him again. Rather than dwell on whether she had imagined the movement, she reached for his wrist to see if the fever was returning. At the touch of her fingers, his hand stirred once more. It was not a struggle, but something closer to an instinctive attempt to grasp what was near. There was no strength behind it, and the fingers soon relaxed. Seren looked at the scarred hand in surprise. After a moment she withdrew her own, wrung out another cloth, and placed it on his forehead. The lamp flickered, and silence settled over the room.

* * *

In the darkness, the hands had not left him. He could not tell how long he had been walking or why he could still move, but gradually the emptiness beneath his feet began to change. He could see nothing, yet he felt there was ground on which to stand—only a little, but something real. The hands did not drag him forward. They supported him, slowly, one step at a time.

It had been so long since he had walked without fear. He did not have to look over his shoulder or guard against a blade descending in the next instant. He did not have to wonder whether the ground ahead would hold another body that would never rise again. The hands remained with him, patient as though waiting for him to learn how to walk anew.

* * *

Rain began to fall outside, a light early-summer shower pattering softly against the eaves. Seren went to the window and closed one shutter halfway to keep out the night wind. When she returned, the young man's breathing was calmer. She touched his forehead; the fever had fallen again. The old man's footsteps sounded in the passage. He entered and looked first at the patient's face rather than his bandages. After a moment his expression relaxed. “He's beginning to come back,” he said quietly.

Seren looked up, puzzled. The old man studied the easing lines of the young man's brow. “Yesterday he was fighting death. Today he's beginning to fight himself.” He did not explain further. Instead he straightened the blanket and touched Seren's shoulder. “You should get some rest tonight. I'll watch him.”

She was about to stand when the patient frowned and began breathing rapidly again. Both turned toward him. The old man remained still, watching. After a tense interval, the breaths slowed and the frown disappeared. He drew the blanket back into place and motioned for Seren to go and rest.

* * *

The night passed quietly at last. In the morning sunlight lay across the bed. The young man had not awakened, but his face was less pale and his breathing more even. Occasionally his eyelids fluttered, as though he were struggling to return from a distant dream. The old man changed the medicine on his shoulder and inspected the wound. “New flesh is beginning to form,” he said.

A younger helper smiled in relief. “Then he really may survive.”

“Don't celebrate too soon,” the old man cautioned. “A wound can heal, but it won't help him if he never wakes.” He patted the patient's hand. There was no response, and he quietly withdrew his own.

* * *

Outside, life continued as usual. Someone delivered freshly picked vegetables; another person arrived with a cart bearing a sack of newly milled flour. Children chased one another around the old tree in the courtyard. The bells sounded over Guanhe as they always had. Few people passing the church could have guessed that, in a small room behind it, a man was slowly finding his way back from the edge of death.

* * *

At midday Seren brought a bowl of freshly prepared medicine. She lifted the young man's shoulder, more practiced now than she had been two days earlier, and raised a wooden spoon to his lips. At first most of the liquid escaped down his chin. She wiped it away and tried again, then again. On the fourth spoonful, his throat moved. He swallowed. Seren paused, watching him, then continued without speaking. He took more of the medicine than he had the previous day.

As she offered the last spoonful, his hand lifted slightly from the bed. It caught nothing, but the motion seemed to be searching for something, some support he could not name. He swallowed the final drops. Seren set the bowl aside and was about to rise when she saw his eyelids tremble. She waited. A moment later his lashes moved again, like those of a traveler approaching the end of a very long road.

Sunlight rested on his face. Slowly, he opened his eyes. His gaze wandered without focus over the wooden beams above him. For a long while he did not move. He seemed not to know where he was, or why he was still alive. The room was quiet except for the laughter of children outside and the distant bell sounding its measured notes. He listened, and gradually his eyes cleared. Then he turned his head.

A girl he did not know stood beside the bed, near the wooden bowl. Their eyes met. Neither spoke at first. Seren gave him a small smile. “You're awake.”

He looked at her without answering. The moment he saw her, the peace that had accompanied him through the darkness returned. It was not as deep as it had been in that strange place, but it was so real that for a moment he scarcely noticed the pain in his shoulder. He frowned, trying to remember. All he could summon was the memory of hands that had held him up. Nothing else.

“Don't move,” Seren said, putting down the bowl. “I'll fetch someone.” She left quietly, drawing the door almost shut behind her.

The young man watched the door. After a while he looked down at his own hands and flexed them. His palms were rough and calloused, familiar to him in every detail. He was alive. Yet an unfamiliar question had lodged in his mind: whose hands had held him in the darkness?

The door opened again, and the old man entered. He stood by the bed for a moment, studying his patient, then smiled. “Welcome back.”

He said it as one might greet a traveler returned from a long journey. The young man tried to speak, but his throat was too dry. The old man helped him drink a little warm water. “Easy. You've been asleep for several days.”

The patient nodded and looked around. The room was plain: a wooden bed, a table, a hearth, and well-tended plants beside the window. There were no elaborate decorations, only a small wooden carving of a bird hanging on the wall. It was not an inn, and it did not seem like a private home. The old man understood his questioning glance. “You're in the church.”

The young man nodded without asking more. The old man examined the bandages in practiced silence. “You're healing well. But if I were you, I wouldn't plan on getting out of bed for a few more days.”

A faint smile touched the patient's eyes, as though his thoughts had been exposed. The old man chuckled. “Every man who comes back from a battlefield thinks he can walk as soon as he wakes. Usually he can't even make it to the door.”

For the first time the young man smiled in earnest, though only slightly. He could not deny it: he had already begun wondering when he might leave. The old man tucked the blanket around him and turned to go. At the doorway he paused, without looking back. “I wasn't the only one who cared for you.” Then he closed the door.

The young man looked at the wooden bowl and remembered the unfamiliar girl. For an instant the hands from his darkness seemed close again. He shut his eyes and lay still, without the old urgency to depart. He listened to the bell and to the children's scattered laughter. Both sounds were strange to him, and both were comforting.

Later, as he lay awake, he became aware that the peace was fading. The room remained safe and quiet. There were no blades, no battle cries, no enemies ready to burst through the door. That alone should have been enough. Yet the deeper stillness that had surrounded him was drawing away, like mist dissolving in morning light. He frowned. Why?

The door opened, and the old man came in with medicine. The young man greeted him with a courteous nod. The old man smiled and began checking his wound. Everything was calm, but the young man knew at once that it was not the same calm. He was not disappointed; he was bewildered. The old man noticed his distraction. “Does it hurt?”

The patient shook his head. His voice, unused for days, emerged hoarse. “Earlier... that girl.”

“She's in the kitchen,” the old man replied, glancing toward the door.

The young man nodded and asked nothing more. But he was surprised to find himself hoping she would return. It was not because she had saved or tended him, nor because of her appearance. When she had stood beside the bed, the peace that had sustained him through the darkness had been present too. When she left, it had seemed to fade. He could not understand the connection, and that was precisely why he needed to understand it.

That afternoon the old man helped him sit upright. Pain tore through his shoulder, but he only tightened his jaw. The old man nodded approvingly. “You're recovering quickly.” The young man managed the smallest of smiles.

The door opened, and Seren entered with a basin of fresh water. She set it beside the bed and began preparing to change the dressing. His eyes went to her without his willing it—not to study or admire her, but to wait for something he could not name. She loosened the damp linen carefully, avoiding the wound. And then the feeling returned. Nothing visible changed, yet the restless waters within him grew suddenly still.

He did not move or even look down at her. He simply attended to the peace. When she finished binding the wound and released his arm, she turned to take the bloodied cloth away. Almost at once the feeling began to retreat. Genuine surprise crossed his face. It had happened again. Was it coincidence? He did not know, but a man who had survived so many encounters with death was not inclined to dismiss a repeated fact. He said nothing. He would watch again.

* * *

At the doorway, Seren had the odd impression that the young man had been watching her. She turned, and their eyes met. He looked away naturally, as though nothing had happened. Puzzled, she gave him a small nod and left. Even after closing the door she glanced back once. There was something strange about him, she thought.

Over the next two days, life in the church continued. The old man visited several times daily to change the dressings and inspect the wound. Sometimes they talked; more often they sat together in silence, listening to the bells, the children, or rain on the eaves. The young man spoke little, and the old man did not press him.

* * *

The young man began to pay attention—not to Seren herself, but to the feeling. The old man might sit beside him for an hour, and the room would remain quiet, but that particular peace would not come. Women from the kitchen brought soup, and children tumbled into the room, nearly upsetting the flowerpots. There was laughter and warmth, but still not the feeling he sought.

Then one afternoon Seren passed the window carrying a basket of freshly washed linen. She did not even stop. Her figure crossed a patch of sunlight and vanished. Yet in that brief instant the tension in his face eased, and the familiar peace returned, as quietly as a spring breeze entering a room. He turned toward the window. She was gone. Only leaves stirred in the wind.

* * *

The next day he paid attention again. The old man was changing his bandages, and the door stood slightly ajar. Voices drifted in from the courtyard. Seren's footsteps approached, then stopped outside while she spoke to someone. Before she entered, the feeling came again. The young man closed his eyes so that no one would see his reaction. He did not rush to explain it. He simply remembered. Once. Twice. Three times.

* * *

A man who had endured so many battles might doubt his eyesight, but he could not keep doubting the same experience. He had no explanation. He was beginning, nevertheless, to believe it was no coincidence.

* * *

Seren knew nothing of this. She tended the injured and helped wherever she was needed. Sometimes she noticed that the young man watched her when she passed, only to look away when she turned. She decided that he was probably still recovering from his long illness, and returned to her work. She had no idea that a life had begun to change direction because of a peace its owner could not explain.
