CHAPTER 22

Those Who Remained

← PreviousPart II ContentsNext →

The elder's voice echoed through the ancient church. After an old hymn, the congregation answered together once more, then knelt according to custom. Silence settled over the benches, broken only by the faint movement of candle flames. Some worshipers prayed with their eyes closed, some bowed low, and others remained still with lowered heads. Time itself seemed to slow.

Seren did not kneel. She remained seated, and no one corrected or reproached her. She knew these people loved the Creator sincerely; she did not doubt that for a moment. Yet the feeling she had been unable to name was growing clearer. In the world she had seen in her vision, no one had needed to teach another how to approach the Creator, for He had been among them. Nothing had stood between them and Him. Here, the worshipers seemed to be following a narrow road, one cautious step after another, in the hope of coming near. They were reverent, earnest, and truthful, but the presence that had seemed close enough to touch in her vision now lay beyond a veil of almost invisible gauze.

She shut her eyes and sought the stillness she remembered. Around her came the familiar pattern: rising, answering, sitting, singing, praying, answering again. Everything happened in its proper order. At last the elder raised his hands and pronounced an ancient blessing. The congregation responded as one. “Amen.”

As the gathering ended, the church filled with the scrape of benches and the murmur of greetings. People helped elderly companions toward the doors. Children, relieved of the solemnity of the service, began whispering about their day. The reverence of the gathering dissolved gently into ordinary life. Seren stayed where she was, looking toward the empty place from which the elder had spoken, though in her mind she was still standing beneath the tree of her vision.

By the time most people had gone, the last sunlight was passing through the colored windows and falling across the stone floor. Footsteps sounded behind her. The old man came down the aisle and sat on the bench beside her without interrupting her thoughts. For a long while neither spoke. They watched the fading light and the dust drifting through it. Then the old man gave a quiet laugh, glanced at the empty benches, and turned toward her. “When I first saw you, I had a feeling you'd experienced something of that kind yourself.”

Seren looked at him in surprise. “How could you know?”

“I couldn't,” he admitted with a smile. “I've simply seen enough people come through these doors. Every now and then someone arrives who seems a little different.” He paused, reconsidering his words. “That isn't quite right either. I can't explain it. Sometimes it seems they've seen something other people haven't.”

The church grew quiet again. From the courtyard came the faint sounds of people putting away the tables. The old man looked ahead as he continued. “They didn't all follow the same road afterward. Some never returned. Some wrote a letter now and then. Others stayed here for years before going on.” He spoke without regret or judgment, as one might recount the lives of old friends.

“And you?” Seren asked.

This time he did not answer at once. The last of the sunlight softened the lines of his face. “When I was young, I had an experience like that too,” he said at last. “And from then on I knew the Creator was real, and that He hadn't abandoned those He made.” He offered no description of what had happened, and Seren did not ask. She sensed it was something he had kept close to his heart for many years.

“I thought about leaving, of course,” he went on. “When you're young, the world beyond the city always seems larger. I used to think perhaps I belonged somewhere else.” He looked down at his calloused hands. “But whenever I prepared to go, I had this feeling that I ought to stay. Sometimes I'd packed everything, only to wake the next morning more certain than ever. Once or twice I made it as far as the city gate and still felt there was something I hadn't finished.”

He gave a small, rueful shrug. “Eventually I stopped arguing with it. If remaining here brought peace to my heart, then here I would remain.” He looked toward the bell tower. “And that was decades ago.”

The tower stood silent in the evening air. The old man's smile did not fade. “Even now, I don't know why it had to be this place, or how much longer I'll be here. But that's all right. Tomorrow the bell will need ringing, the bread will need baking, and if travelers come, someone will have to open the door. Those things still need doing.”

He fell silent. Seren had the impression that following the Creator, to him, had never meant possessing every answer. It meant rising on an ordinary morning and being willing, once again, to ring the bell and open the gate. After a while he sighed, not heavily, but with the wonder of a man looking back across many years. “The Creator is a marvelous mystery.”

He studied the empty benches. “The church was meant to bring people into His presence. I've spent my whole life here, and yet listening to what you've said today...” He laughed softly, searching for the words. “I feel as though I've come to know Him a little better myself.”

Seren looked at him, surprised by his openness. He smiled without embarrassment. “My teacher told me that those who founded this place didn't set out to build a church. They wanted somewhere anyone could come to remember the Creator and seek Him again. As more people arrived, the community grew. We established regular gatherings, prayers, and songs, and took on more responsibilities so we could care for them.” He glanced toward the courtyard, where the children were still clearing bowls. “Those are good things. Without them, many people might have had nowhere to come.”

He looked toward the candles still burning near the altar. “But sometimes I wonder whether we've become so busy bringing people inside the church that we've forgotten to walk beside them all the way into the Creator's presence.” He did not try to settle the question. Perhaps he had not settled it in his own mind. After a while he shrugged. “Maybe old age has simply given me too much time to think. But I believe that if the Creator hasn't given up seeking people, He won't give up on His church either. Someone will always continue helping others find their way to Him.”

In the days that followed, Seren remained in the courtyard behind the church. The morning bells rang as always. The old man visited the tower and inspected the vegetables. The ovens were lit, bread was baked, and the gates opened. People came and went. Yet, almost without anyone noticing, the number of visitors began to increase. The woman who baked the bread was the first to remark on it. Looking at an empty flour sack one morning, she said, “We've been baking more loaves than usual lately.”

“The weather's good,” someone replied. “There are more travelers on the roads.” She nodded and said no more, but the following morning she kneaded an extra basin of dough.

* * *

A few days later, farmers from the hills came through the courtyard with dried herbs and animal skins to trade for supplies. Their boots were still caked with damp mountain mud. Over their meal, one of them remarked with a laugh, “The wild beasts in the hills seem less afraid of people every year.”

“That's true,” an older man agreed, putting down his spoon. “I saw a wolf in broad daylight when I was cutting wood the other day.”

“Hunters will have plenty to do this year,” someone joked. The others laughed, and the conversation soon turned to the harvest. No one spoke further about the animals.

* * *

Two days later, fishermen from coastal villages stopped at the church, not to seek help but to rest as they usually did when passing through Rivergate. At supper an older fisherman glanced out the window. “The currents have been strange this year.”

“There are still fish,” a younger man said, breaking his bread, “but we have to sail farther to find them.”

“Maybe the winds have simply changed,” another suggested over his soup. The old man listened without interrupting and quietly refilled their bowls.

* * *

On another morning, the area before the church was unusually lively. Several laborers carrying grain sacks were eating bread and discussing news from the city. “Have you seen the announcement?” one asked, lowering his voice as though sharing something extraordinary.

“What announcement?” another demanded.

“The chief magistrate has appointed a new steward to oversee the granaries. They say he'll be responsible for the city's food stores, reserves, tax grain, and everything brought in from the surrounding districts.”

“With a harvest this good, why create such an important office?” someone asked, laughing.

“Who knows? But I hear the young fellow is capable, and the chief magistrate trusts him. Before long he might be running the largest granary in all Rivergate.”

The men continued eating, and their talk soon shifted to a merchant caravan newly arrived at the harbor. To them, the appointment was merely another piece of daily city news. Seren listened quietly from the long table. She thought of the young man she had seen several days earlier in the temporary holding room at the guardhouse, the one whose composure had seemed remarkable for his age. Was he the young steward they were discussing? She could not know, nor could she guess whether their paths would ever cross again.
