CHAPTER 21

The Veil and the Benches

← PreviousPart II ContentsNext →

The work kept them occupied throughout the morning. People came for food in a steady stream: travelers newly arrived in the city, elderly men and women who lived alone, and children carrying wooden bowls with both hands, explaining in shy voices that their mothers were ill and needed something warm to eat. No one demanded an account of their circumstances or wrote down their names. A nod was enough, and bread and hot soup were placed in their hands.

By the time noon approached, the crowd had thinned and the square had grown quiet. Several young people began clearing the long tables. Some washed the wooden bowls, others carried the remaining loaves back to the kitchen, while a few fetched buckets of water to rinse the stones. Seren was about to slip aside when she noticed the young mother she had seen earlier with two children. The woman was struggling to carry a heavy basket of wet laundry on her own. Seren hurried over. “Let me help you,” she offered.

The woman looked surprised, then smiled and handed her half the load. “Thank you.”

Together they made their way to the courtyard behind the church. Only then did Seren discover that an entirely different little world lay beyond its walls. There were no imposing stone ramparts here, only a handful of plain stone cottages gathered around a modest yard. An old well stood at its center, with freshly washed buckets beside it. In one corner grew a vegetable patch, where tomatoes, onions, and herbs Seren could not name flourished in the sunlight. An elderly man bent over the soil, pulling weeds. Beneath a tree, two children sat carving pieces of wood, while nearby a young man carefully repaired a chair with a broken leg. Everyone had something to do, yet no one seemed hurried. They simply attended to the work before them.

The young woman finished hanging the laundry and turned to Seren with a smile. “You can leave those here.”

Seren nodded, but her gaze lingered on the courtyard. It was unlike any place she had known. Not an inn, nor a town hall, nor anything like a guildhouse. No one appeared to be giving orders, yet each person seemed to know where they were needed.

A burst of laughter came from another cottage. Its door opened, and the old man who had been distributing bread emerged with a wooden tray bearing several bowls of freshly cooked vegetable soup. The children gathered around him at once. With a mischievous smile, he raised the tray beyond their reach.

“Wash your hands first.”

The children groaned in cheerful protest but ran obediently to the well. The old man watched them with the fondness of a grandfather looking after his own grandchildren, and Seren found herself smiling too. For the first time that day, she had forgotten the table on Craftsmen’s Street and all that had happened there the day before. Perhaps not every corner of Rivergate was weighed down by the same heaviness. There were places where a person could slow her steps without meaning to, places where staying a little longer seemed the most natural thing in the world.

Then hurried footsteps sounded beyond the courtyard. A boy in his early teens came running through the gate, scarcely pausing to catch his breath.

“Grandpa Zhong! There’s an injured traveler down by the river!”

The old man set his tray carefully on the table. He did not appear alarmed. “I see.” Turning to the others, he added in the same calm voice, “Bring the medicine chest, and make ready a clean bed.”

At once the people who had been absorbed in their own tasks put them aside. No one demanded an explanation, and no one waited to be told what to do next. They moved with the ease of people who had answered such calls many times before. As Seren watched, it occurred to her that these people had not become a family merely by living under the same roofs. They had become one by learning to regard another person's need as something that concerned them all.

The injured traveler was brought in soon afterward. He looked to be in his forties, and one leg of his trousers was soaked through with blood. Sweat shone on his forehead as two young men supported him, step by painful step, across the courtyard. The old man did not question him. He simply steadied the man's shoulder.

“Easy now. No one here is going to hurry you.”

The traveler nodded, and some of the tension seemed to leave his body. A gray-haired woman had already opened the door of a nearby cottage. The room beyond was small but carefully kept: the bed had been made, and a jar of wildflowers stood beside the window in fresh water. Another young woman arrived carrying the medicine chest, knelt beside the injured man, and gently cut away the bloodstained fabric around his leg.

No one raised a voice. No one seemed at a loss. Seren remained outside the doorway, watching them work, and noticed something that struck her more deeply than the orderly preparations. No one had asked the stranger's name, whether he could pay, or even how he had been hurt. They had stopped the bleeding first, found him somewhere to sit, and allowed him to catch his breath. Only when he had been made comfortable did someone bring him a cup of warm water.

The old man patted his shoulder. “Rest. You can tell us the rest whenever you're ready.”

He closed the door softly and returned to the courtyard as though he had done nothing remarkable. The ordinary work of the afternoon continued. Someone split firewood; someone else carried water. The children crouched by the vegetable beds, collecting snails from the leaves. The young man who had repaired the broken chair carried it to its place beside the bell tower. No one had abandoned the day's work, but neither had anyone turned away from a person in need. Each seemed to move toward whatever required their hands, as naturally as water finding its course.

Seren thought of the young carpenter she had met the day before, and of the oak table carried back into his workshop. The same city, the same ordinary people—and yet here, too, they were finding ways to take a little of the weight from someone else's shoulders.

“Can you chop wood?” the old man asked, interrupting her thoughts.

Seren blinked, then laughed and shook her head. “No.”

“That will do.” He handed her a small basket of freshly gathered onions. “Take these to the kitchen for me, then.”

“All right.” She accepted the basket with a nod.

He offered neither a formal thanks nor an apology for troubling her. He had simply given her a small part in work that belonged to everyone. Carrying the onions, Seren crossed the courtyard. Several children washing bowls waved as she passed.

“The kitchen's that way!” one little girl called.

“And the stew is especially good today,” another child added.

They all laughed, and Seren laughed with them. Somewhere in the course of the day, without her noticing when it happened, the people here had stopped treating her as a stranger. More surprising still, she had almost forgotten that she herself was only a traveler who had meant to leave Rivergate behind.

After supper, no one hurried away. The younger people gathered the wooden bowls, while others carefully wrapped the leftover bread and put it into a chest. The children took their cups to the well and washed them with great seriousness, although every so often one splashed another and laughter rippled through the courtyard. The old man rolled up his sleeves and began wiping down the long table. Seren moved to help, but he nodded toward the kitchen. “They need you more over there.” Several women were struggling to lift a heavy iron pot from the hearth. Seren went to join them.

* * *

The days began to move more slowly. Before dawn the following morning, the bell sounded across Guanhe. The old man climbed the tower alone, as he apparently had every morning for decades, without anyone reminding him. Seren stood in the courtyard and watched while the measured notes traveled through the cool air. Doors opened one after another. Someone drew water, someone kindled the ovens, and someone went into the vegetable garden. Children emerged from the stone houses rubbing their eyes and, still half asleep, began carrying firewood from beneath the eaves. No one had assigned them the task. They simply knew what needed doing.

Seren gradually learned the rhythms of the place. She knew which stone house sheltered the wounded traveler and which kitchen cupboard held the salt. She knew that the old man went first to the bell tower and then to inspect the vegetables. Once the city gates opened, visitors would arrive: some for a piece of bread, some for hot soup, and others simply to sit without speaking. The old man never pressed them. Sometimes he sat beside a visitor for an entire day with scarcely a word; sometimes he kept company with someone grieving a loved one from morning until dusk. More and more, Seren thought of the place not as a church, but as a home where someone would always be waiting for you to return.

* * *

On the evening of the third day, a young merchant arrived at the courtyard gate with a heavy pack. His boots were worn through, and exhaustion showed plainly on his face. The old man opened the gate and took the pack from his shoulders without asking where he had come from. “Sit down first,” he said gently.

The merchant let out a long breath, as though a body held rigid through days of travel had finally found somewhere to rest. Watching from the covered walkway, Seren remembered standing here with her own belongings only days earlier. No one had questioned or judged her. They had merely given her somewhere to set down her burden. At that moment a long, resonant peal rose from deep within the church.

Around the courtyard, people gradually stopped working. A hoe was laid aside, a book closed, sawdust brushed from a sleeve. They exchanged glances and turned toward the church without being summoned. Seren followed them. At the first sound of the bell, something familiar had stirred within her. It was neither anticipation nor unease, but the faintest ripple, like the sensation she had known at the end of her vision when she saw the thin veil. She hesitated, then passed through the familiar wooden doorway.

The church was more crowded than on her first visit. Nearly every bench was occupied. People helped the elderly into their seats, settled children on their knees, and made room for travelers who sat quietly in the last row. Candlelight flickered against the old pillars; colored light from the windows lay across the stone. The air carried the familiar scents of timber and wax. Seren sat toward the rear and watched. The old man had not taken a place at the front. As always, he sat among the others, although he seemed quieter here than he did in the courtyard.

Soon an elder in dark robes approached the front of the church, and the congregation fell silent. The last notes of the bell faded beneath the vaulted ceiling. The elder began to recite an ancient prayer in a low, solemn voice. At appointed moments, the people answered together. Some bowed their heads, some knelt, and some clasped their hands. There was order in everything they did, and Seren could see that their devotion was sincere. No one appeared to be speaking carelessly or responding out of mere obligation. Yet an uneasiness she could not name began to take shape within her.

She remembered the grassland of her vision and the great tree standing there. The people of that place had also loved and revered the One who walked among them. But they had not waited for prescribed words, or for another person to tell them when to speak. They had simply walked beside Him, as naturally as a child beside a father or friends sharing a path. Their closeness had been as effortless as breathing. Here, too, the people longed to draw near to the Creator, but they seemed to place each step carefully on a road already laid out, fearful of taking a wrong turn or saying the wrong thing.

Seren lowered her eyes. Perhaps she misunderstood the customs of this place. Perhaps she simply had not grown accustomed to them. She closed her eyes and tried to recover the stillness she had felt in her vision. Around her the responses continued, followed by the sounds of people rising and sitting again. She did not find them noisy or unpleasant. Yet the nearness that had once seemed unmistakable now felt separated from her by a veil almost too fine to see.

She knew He was still there. Somehow, though, He seemed farther away. Seren opened her eyes and looked across the congregation. The old man was praying with his head bowed, making no display of himself. Of everyone present, he alone reminded her of the vision. It was not because of anything he had said. While the others seemed to be searching so earnestly for the Creator, the old man sat like a tree whose roots had long since found deep soil. He was simply there, quiet and certain, as though he understood that what must draw near was never the feet, but the heart.
