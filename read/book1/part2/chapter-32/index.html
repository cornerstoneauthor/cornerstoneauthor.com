CHAPTER 32

The Silent Wood

← PreviousPart II ContentsNext →

In the afternoon the road left the river and entered a low wood of oak and ash. The trees were not tall enough to blot out the sky, and sunlight slipped through their branches to scatter shifting patterns across the path. Kael shortened his stride so that Elowen, whose arm had not fully healed, could keep pace without effort. He said nothing about the adjustment, and neither woman drew attention to it. After several days together, such small acts of consideration were becoming part of the rhythm of their journey. Seren still felt none of the guidance she had once known, but she no longer treated every interval of silence as a riddle that had to be solved at once.

A squirrel sprang from a low branch and raced up the trunk of a neighboring tree. Seren watched it disappear into the leaves. “It doesn't seem frightened of us,” she said with a smile.

“It is,” Kael replied, looking up. “It simply knows it has a way out. It can afford to stay where it is because we're too far away to reach it.”

Elowen laughed. “That sounds like something a warrior would teach.” Kael shook his head. When he was younger, he said, he had often mistaken recklessness for bravery; only after several narrow escapes had he learned to tell the two apart. Seren was tempted to ask for the stories behind those words, but his expression suggested that he had said as much as he wished.

Farther on, a fallen tree blocked half the road. Rain had darkened the bark, and wagon wheels had cut a rough detour through the soil beside it. Kael found a dry place to set his hand and climbed across. Seren was about to follow when he called for her to stop. With the tip of his sword he lifted a layer of leaves, revealing a restless mass of red ants moving over the wood. Seren snatched her hand back and laughed in relief. Elowen asked whether he had ever suffered from them himself. Kael said he had learned many lessons the hard way, especially on his first journeys alone, when he had assumed that anything worth fearing would be carrying a weapon.

Beyond the fallen tree they came upon the remains of an abandoned campfire. Rain had soaked the ashes; broken pottery and a snapped wooden wedge from a wagon axle lay nearby. Kael knelt to inspect them. The fire had not been lit the previous night, but the wheel marks crossing the clearing were more recent, suggesting that someone had stopped there to make hurried repairs. Elowen asked whether they should follow the tracks. Kael saw no reason yet to leave the road, though he told them to watch for scattered goods among the trees. Seren remembered the merchants at the wayhouse speaking of detours and missing travelers. She resisted the temptation to turn every sign into a tale of disaster, but she could no longer assume that a road leading toward a great city must be safe.

As they continued, the woodland grew quieter. At first the birdsong became less frequent; then even the insects in the undergrowth fell silent. Kael noticed pawprints in a patch of soft earth, partly covered by fallen leaves but still sharply defined. There were several sets of tracks, not all of the same size. They ran along both sides of the road, as though something had been keeping pace with the travelers from within the trees. He did not draw his sword immediately. Instead, he raised one hand and signaled the others to come closer. Seren saw the change in his posture and tightened her grip on her staff. Elowen's smile vanished.

“Keep walking. Don't run suddenly,” Kael said under his breath. He held them near enough that he could reach either of them and led them along the center of the path. Wind still moved the upper branches. That made the absence of all the smaller woodland sounds more disturbing, not less. Seren searched the undergrowth and caught what might have been a gray shape passing between two trunks. She remembered the stories she had heard as a child, in which wolves came out of darkness with dreadful howls. Here the forest was bright, and the silence almost peaceful.

The first low growl came from the left. Elowen drew in a breath and held it. Another answered from ahead and to the right, then a third sounded farther behind them. Kael listened, his face tightening. “At least six,” he said. “And some have already moved behind us.”

Seren asked whether they could climb a tree. Kael shook his head. The trunks stood too far from the road for all three to reach them together, and Elowen's injured arm would make climbing difficult. If they separated, the animals could attack them one by one. Instead, he guided them toward a slightly raised stretch of stony ground near the middle of the road, where the undergrowth would not conceal what was approaching their feet.

A gray wolf emerged from the shadows. It was larger than any hunting dog Seren remembered, broad across the shoulders, with dark markings threaded through its coat. It did not charge. It moved slowly along the roadside, keeping its distance from Kael. Two more appeared, taking positions to either side of the path ahead. The rest remained among the bushes, their eyes visible from time to time. Kael studied their movements. They did not jostle or threaten one another as hungry animals might. Their positions seemed deliberate, as though they were waiting for a single signal.

Seren's palms grew damp around the wood of her staff. Instinctively she reached inward for the reassurance that had steadied her through earlier dangers, but there was only the same hollow quiet. Her fear remained; she could hear the pounding of her own heart. Then she remembered Elowen's words about the sun behind the clouds, and Kael's insistence that yesterday's truth could not be undone by today's uncertainty. She did not know whether she was brave enough for what was coming. She knew only that running would leave the wounded Elowen exposed.

“Stay on either side of me,” Kael instructed. “And don't step beyond the point of my sword.” Seren moved to his left and Elowen to his right, leaving room for him to swing the blade. Elowen’s sword belt was empty. On the day of the ambush, she had laid her short sword beside the roots of a tree to free her hands while helping bury the fallen guard and set the carriage in order. When the three set out again, none of them remembered it. By the time she noticed it was missing, they had already traveled too far, and her injured left arm could not bear the return journey. She had been forced to leave it behind. Now she picked up a hard stone from beside her feet, steadying the strap of her pack with her other hand. Kael told her not to throw unless a wolf came dangerously close. His calm voice did not mean he felt no strain. He was already measuring the angles of attack and wondering whether, if his defense failed, the two women might be able to retreat toward the fallen tree.

The first wolf sprang from directly ahead, faster than any of them expected. Kael turned aside, his blade grazing its shoulder and forcing it to land near the edge of the road. Almost at once another lunged toward Elowen. Seren swung her staff against the side of its neck. She did not bring it down, but the blow deflected its leap. Kael wheeled and drove a third animal back, claws scattering dirt beneath his feet. In the space of a few heartbeats, the silent woodland was filled with panting, snarls, and the ringing of steel.

The wolves withdrew into the trees, but the first assault had not broken their resolve. Gray shapes shifted through the shadows as they rearranged the circle around the travelers. Beyond them, near a low rock, a larger wolf stood watching. Its golden eyes never left the struggle, yet it made no move to join it. Kael understood that these were not merely hungry animals that had happened to gather together. They were searching for the weakest place in the travelers' defense, and the most dangerous attack might still be ahead.
