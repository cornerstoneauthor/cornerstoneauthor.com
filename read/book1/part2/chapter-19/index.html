CHAPTER 19

The Church and the Vision

The streets were still crowded.

Merchants pushed carts laden with goods, porters carried heavy loads upon their shoulders, and city guards passed through the crowds on their regular patrols. Children ran between the adults, their laughter mingling with the calls of traders and the rumbling of wagon wheels over stone.

Nothing around Seren had changed. Rivergate remained as busy as it had been that morning, yet as she walked, the whole city seemed strangely distant.

Her thoughts lingered upon the oak table that had never been carried home.

She remembered the young couple leaving the workshop, the weary expression upon the guild elder's face, and the careful way the carpenter had returned his finished work to its place against the wall.

No one had truly won that afternoon. Yet somehow, each of them seemed to have lost something.

Seren breathed out slowly.

There was a heaviness in her chest that she could neither explain nor put aside. She had witnessed disagreements before, and she knew that people could hurt one another without intending to. But what troubled her about the carpenter's workshop was that everyone involved had seemed to believe he was doing what was right.

She could find no simple answer to what she had seen. She continued walking, scarcely noticing where her feet were taking her. After a while, the streets began to grow quieter. The shops stood farther apart, and the steady hammering from the smithies faded behind her. Even the scent of freshly cut wood gradually disappeared, replaced by the cooler air moving between the stone buildings.

Then, somewhere in the distance, a bell began to toll. Its sound was long and deep, though not particularly loud. It carried across the rooftops and through the streets, reaching Seren with a clarity that seemed to set it apart from every other noise in the city.

She stopped. The sound faded slowly into the afternoon air.

Seren raised her head and listened. The bell did not seem to announce anything, nor did it carry the urgency of a warning. Its measured tone lingered in the wind, as though it belonged to the ordinary passing of the day. A little later, it sounded again. This time, Seren felt some of the heaviness within her ease.

She did not know where the bell was coming from, but when she resumed walking, her steps carried her toward it. The street gradually widened. Tall elm trees stood along either side, their branches reaching over the road. Sunlight filtered through the leaves and fell upon the paving stones in shifting patches of gold and green.

There were still people passing through this part of the city, though something about their manner seemed different. Conversations grew quieter as they approached the end of the street. Now and then, someone would pause, incline their head toward something ahead, and continue on their way.

Seren followed the road.

Before long, she saw a building unlike any she had encountered since entering Rivergate.

It possessed none of the imposing grandeur of Town Hall, nor the heavy fortifications of a castle. There were no towering defensive walls, and no guards patrolling its entrance. Above it rose a tall bell tower.

Seren looked up and understood at once where the sound had come from. Beneath the tower stood a broad building of pale gray stone. The years had left their marks upon its walls, where wind and rain had worn the edges of the masonry. Ivy climbed along several sections of the stonework, its green leaves softening the building's weathered appearance.

The wooden doors were partly open. People entered and departed without ceremony. No one stood at the entrance to question strangers, and no one seemed concerned with announcing who had arrived. There was something natural about the place, as though it had been standing there for generations and had long since become part of the lives of those who passed through its doors.

Seren stopped at the edge of the square. She did not know what the building was, or what she might find inside. Yet the quiet sense of guidance that had accompanied her throughout her journey had never felt quite so clear. It seemed to her that something was waiting beyond those half-open doors. She could not have said what it was, and she heard no voice calling her forward. Nevertheless, the feeling remained, gentle but unmistakable.

For a long while, she stood looking at the entrance. Then she began to walk. The afternoon breeze passed around the bell tower, stirring the ancient bronze bell above her. As she approached the doors, it sounded for a third time. Seren did not stop. She crossed the threshold and entered.

The church was quiet. The heavy wooden door settled gently behind her, and the sounds of the street seemed to fall away almost at once. High above, sunlight streamed through tall windows of colored glass, casting soft patterns across the stone floor. The air carried the faint scent of wood and spent candles, and the stillness of the place was broken only by the occasional movement of someone among the benches.

A person near the front was quietly straightening the wooden pews. In one corner, someone knelt with their head bowed. Farther away, an elderly man sat alone with his hands loosely clasped, his expression suggesting neither impatience nor weariness.

No one approached Seren. No one asked her why she had come. She was grateful for that.

Moving quietly between the rows, she found a place on the last bench and sat down. For a while, she simply watched the light moving across the floor. She had never been in a place quite like this. The silence did not feel empty, nor did it seem imposed upon those who had gathered there. Each person appeared free to remain as long as they wished, occupied with thoughts or concerns that belonged to them alone.

Seren rested her hands in her lap.

The journey that had brought her to Rivergate seemed very far away now, though she had traveled its roads only recently. She thought briefly of the people she had met, of the questions she had carried with her, and of the things she had witnessed without fully understanding them.

Then even those thoughts began to settle.

She could hear her own breathing. She remained there for some time before slowly closing her eyes. A breeze touched her face. It was unlike the air inside the church. It carried the scent of fresh grass, flowing water, and leaves warmed by the sun.

Seren opened her eyes. The stone walls were gone. So were the colored windows, the wooden benches, and the quiet figures who had occupied the church. She was standing upon a vast, open plain. Above her stretched a sky of extraordinary clarity, its blue unbroken by cloud or haze. Sunlight lay gently upon the land, neither harsh nor blinding. The warmth of it seemed to reach into everything it touched.

Far away, mountains rose in broad, peaceful folds. At their feet, a clear river wound through the valley, reflecting the sky so perfectly that its waters seemed to carry the same endless blue.

The grass moved in waves beneath the breeze. Flowers grew among it in colors more vivid than any Seren remembered seeing, yet nothing about them appeared unnatural. They belonged to the land as completely as the mountains and the river, each blossom growing where it had taken root.

Seren took a cautious step forward.

The grass bent beneath her feet and rose again as she passed, leaving no crushed stems behind her. She looked down in surprise, then continued walking. Near the riverbank, several deer were drinking. At the sound of her footsteps, they lifted their heads. Seren expected them to flee, but they merely watched her with quiet interest.

One of the younger deer approached. It came close enough to brush its head against her hand, then turned and bounded back toward the others.

Seren stood looking after it. She had encountered deer before, but never one that approached a stranger without fear. From the trees ahead came a low, powerful sound. She turned.

A lion emerged from the shadows of the woodland. It was a magnificent creature, broad-shouldered and strong, with a golden mane that stirred in the breeze. Each movement revealed the power held within its great body, yet there was no menace in the way it crossed the grass. Its eyes were calm. A short distance away, several children were playing.

They continued their game as the lion approached, laughing and chasing one another across the meadow. None cried out or ran for safety. The lion passed among them without disturbing their play. Then the youngest child stumbled and fell upon the grass. The animal stopped. It lowered its great head and sniffed gently at the child's face. The little boy laughed. Reaching out, he buried his small fingers in the lion's thick mane. The lion closed its eyes for a moment, accepting the touch as naturally as a household animal might welcome a familiar hand. Then it brushed its head against the child's shoulder and continued toward the trees.

Seren watched until it disappeared into the woodland.

A little farther on, a black panther lay stretched across a broad branch. Sunlight passed through the leaves above it, scattering bright patches across its dark coat. Several birds had settled nearby and were preening their feathers. The panther lifted its head once, gazed toward the distant plain, and rested again. Its tail moved slowly along the branch. A squirrel sprang from the trunk onto the panther's back before leaping toward another tree. The great cat did not even turn.

Seren found herself smiling.

Everywhere she looked, creatures that might have feared or hunted one another lived without suspicion. The lion had not needed to display its strength. The deer had not needed to flee, and the birds had shown no fear of the panther. It was not that strength had vanished from the world. The lion was still a lion, and the panther remained every bit as powerful as its appearance suggested. Yet their strength brought no terror to those around them. For the first time, Seren wondered whether true greatness might have nothing to do with proving oneself stronger than another.

She continued across the meadow.

Birds descended from the trees and settled among the flowers before taking flight again. One small bird landed upon a child's shoulder. When the child offered a finger, the bird pecked it gently and flew away. There was no fear in the encounter, and no desire to possess what had come so freely. Along the river, Seren saw people going about their ordinary lives.

One person bent over a young plant, moving aside the branches that had blocked its sunlight. Another sat beside the water with a child, watching fish swim through the shallows. Nearby, someone supported a young tree that had been bent by the wind, carefully helping it stand upright again. No one seemed hurried. There were tasks to be done, certainly, but none of the people she saw appeared burdened by the need to finish before someone else, or to claim more than another had received. When they met, they greeted one another with genuine pleasure.

Seren could see it in their faces.

Their smiles were not merely courteous expressions offered because custom demanded them. They seemed truly glad to find one another there, as though the presence of another person were itself a reason for happiness.

She walked on, taking in everything around her. The land was beautiful, but it was the life within it that held her attention. Nothing seemed out of place. Each creature moved according to its own nature, and yet none disturbed the peace of the others. It was as though every living thing had been given room to become fully what it was meant to be.

Then Seren saw something in the distance.

A tree.

At first, she could scarcely comprehend its size. Its trunk rose broad and strong from the earth, and its great branches extended outward beneath a crown that seemed wide enough to shelter the land around it. The tree was unlike any she had ever seen. A breeze moved through its countless leaves.

Seren heard no particular sound, yet she became aware of something passing through the world around her. The grass stirred. The river flowed. Birds moved among the branches, and the flowers swayed in the sunlight. For a moment, it seemed that every movement belonged to a single, living harmony. Even the breathing of the people around her appeared to share in it.

Seren stood still.

She did not understand what she was witnessing, but the feeling was so natural that she could scarcely imagine the world being otherwise. Beneath the great tree, many people had gathered. They stood and sat together around a figure whose face Seren could not clearly see. Some walked beside Him, speaking as freely as they might speak to someone they had known all their lives. Others sat close to Him, listening or sharing their thoughts. Children laughed as they held His hands. An elderly man sat beside Him with a peaceful smile, while a young person offered Him fruit. Someone leaned against Him with the easy trust of a child resting beside a father.

Seren watched, unable to look away. No one knelt in terror before Him. No one stood at a distance, afraid to approach. He was not seated upon a throne high above them, nor did He remain apart while the people gathered below. He was among them.

Though Seren could not distinguish His face, His presence seemed to fill the place with a warmth unlike anything she had known.

The people did not merely live near Him. They belonged with Him. Nothing separated them, and no one appeared to wonder whether they were permitted to draw closer.

Seren felt an unfamiliar lightness within her chest. She had never seen this land, nor had she met any of the people gathered beneath the tree. Yet the longer she watched, the more deeply she felt that she knew the place.

It was a strange certainty, one she could not explain.

She had never been here. And still, some part of her seemed to recognize it as home. Almost without realizing it, she began walking toward the tree. She wanted to draw nearer, to see more clearly the figure standing among the people. She crossed the grass, her eyes fixed upon the great branches and the gathering beneath them. Then, just as she was approaching, something changed. It was so slight that she might have missed it had she not been looking directly ahead. The air seemed to tremble. For an instant, the movement resembled the first faint ripple spreading across a perfectly still pool.

Seren stopped.

The land around her remained unchanged. The great tree still stood in the distance. The figure was still among the people, and the children continued laughing as they played. Birds wheeled through the clear sky. Yet something had appeared between Seren and all that lay ahead.

It was a veil.

At first, it was so thin that she could scarcely distinguish it from the air. She could still see the tree clearly, and the people beneath its branches remained visible.

She took another step. The veil did not move. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, it began to grow more substantial. The distant leaves lost some of their sharpness. The river no longer reflected the sunlight with the same brilliance, and the figures gathered beneath the tree grew faint around their edges.

Seren looked from one part of the landscape to another. Nothing seemed to be happening to the land itself. The mountains had not moved. The river continued to flow, and the great tree remained where it had always stood. Yet everything was becoming harder to see. She reached out her hand. She wanted to draw nearer, but the veil lay between her and the place she had been approaching. She could still see through it. She could no longer pass.

The breeze continued to move across the grass, but the warmth that had accompanied it seemed to have disappeared. The land remained beautiful beyond the veil, though it now appeared impossibly far away.

Seren stood with her hand extended.

The veil grew thicker. The people beneath the tree faded until she could scarcely distinguish one from another. The river lost its brightness, and the distant mountains became little more than pale shapes. At last, the great tree itself began to disappear. So did the land around it. And the figure who had walked so freely among the people slowly vanished behind the deepening veil.

Seren could no longer see Him. For a moment, she remained where she was, staring into the place where everything had been. Then even the last traces of the landscape were gone. A gentle, endless silence surrounded her.

Seren opened her eyes. She was sitting in the church. The wooden benches stood in their familiar rows, and the tall windows still cast colored light across the stone floor. The building was quiet, just as it had been when she closed her eyes.

But the afternoon had passed. The sun had moved beyond the higher windows, and its golden light now rested near her feet.

Seren remained seated. For a long while, she made no attempt to rise. No one approached her, and no one asked what had happened. Those who remained in the church continued their quiet occupations, unaware of what she had seen. She slowly lifted her eyes toward the far end of the building. She did not know what to call the place she had visited, nor could she explain how she had come to see it.

She remembered the river, the mountains, and the animals living without fear. She remembered the people beneath the great tree, and the figure whose face she had never been able to see.

Most of all, she remembered how natural it had felt to belong there. The memory brought with it a longing she had never known before. It was not quite sorrow, though something precious seemed to have been lost. Nor was it simply happiness, despite the beauty of everything she had witnessed.

She could not yet give the feeling a name. Still, as she sat in the fading sunlight, Seren sensed that something deep within her had awakened. It was like the experience of someone who had wandered far from home and, after many years, suddenly caught the familiar scent of the wind that once blew through their childhood village.

For an instant, the distance between that person and home would seem to vanish. And even without knowing the road back, they would remember that such a place had once existed.

Seren remained on the bench, holding that memory quietly within her.
