CHAPTER 20

The Fear in the Straw

← PreviousPart I ContentsNext →

They reached Stony Creek on the third evening.

The village sat in a narrow gravel basin, a dozen timber houses clustered around a fast, shallow stream. A few thin strips of barley grew between the boulders; women were gathering dry hay from the racks; two boys were driving a small flock of black-faced sheep into a stone pen.

Aether stood on the crest of the trail, his hands loose on his pack-straps, feeling a sudden sense of confusion. After all of Bren’s talk about the mountain packs, he had expected a ruined wall, burned timbers, and the smell of old iron. But the lane looked ordinary—almost like home.

"It's just another longhouse plot," Aether muttered.

Kaelen adjusted his spear. "Dirt is dirt, lad. Move your boots."

Yet, as the line approached the low timber palisade at the entry, Harold suddenly raised a flat hand. The entire line froze in their tracks.

Harold stepped off the gravel toward the pine stakes. He knelt and ran his thick fingers over a shattered oak post. The timber had not been cut; it had been twisted apart, the grain torn into three deep, uneven gouges. The spacing was wrong for a wolf and too narrow for the black bears Harold knew from the southern slopes.

Marcus leaned over, his weathered face darkening. "That’s no clean bear strike," he said. "Look at the spread. And whatever hit it kept pulling after the wood gave way."

Harold didn't answer. He stood up slowly, his heavy gaze sweeping the treeline that blanketed the upper ridge. The easy familiarity Aether had felt moments ago vanished, replaced by a cold knot in his throat.

As the Ashvale line entered the lane, the villagers stopped their work. They looked at the heavy spears and the iron-shod wood with a mixture of exhaustion and sharp, desperate hope.

A woman ran out from the first cabin, her tunic stained with grease and wood-smoke. When she saw Bren walking upright, her face went slack with a relief she had carried for three moons. "You're back," she breathed.

"Aye," Bren said, gesturing to Harold. "The valley spears are here."

The village elder came down from the mill—a man named Ren, whose spine had curved from forty winters of stone-work. He took Harold and the senior hunters into the main longhouse to settle the watch, leaving the boys in the lane.

Aether stopped near an old elm tree. A small boy sat in the dust, cradling a prick-eared hound whose left hind leg was bound tightly with clean wool rags.

Aether knelt beside him. "What took its leg?"

The child didn't look up, his small fingers stroking the dog’s snout. "I don’t know. It came out of the scrub."

"A wolf?" Aether asked.

The boy shook his head. "It didn’t move like one."

Before Aether could ask more, the sun dipped fully behind the jagged peak.

The moment the last amber light faded from the sky, a sound rose from the black timber miles up the valley.

It was neither a howl nor a roar. A low, rough rumble rolled out of the timber—too deep for a wolf, too uneven for the bears the Ashvale hunters knew. It lasted only a moment, but every dog in the lane reacted at once.

The entire village went dead silent in a heartbeat. The herders froze; the stone-cutter gripped his felling axe; the hound in the boy's lap let out a terrified, strangled whimper, shivering violently against the dirt.

Harold stepped out of the longhouse, his heavy hand coming down flat over the hilt of his hunting sword. Beside him, Kaelen stood rigid, his fingers locked around his spear shaft until his knuckles turned bone-white.

The sound lasted no more than three seconds before the mountain swallowed it back into the dark. But those three seconds hung in the cold air like smoke. Aether stood frozen, a sudden, icy shiver running straight down his spine.

By nightfall, torches burned everywhere—at the gate, along the sheep-fences, and in the hands of night-patrols walking the timber line.

The villagers had cleared a small storage hut for the Ashvale men. As the hunters prepared their blankets, Aether noticed that every man laid his blade directly beside his knee. Even in the neighboring cabin, a mother leaned an iron-tipped hayfork against the doorframe.

In the dim corner lit by a sputtering grease-lamp, the herder boy lay on his straw pallet. He was no longer holding his hound. Instead, his small arms were wrapped tightly around a three-foot hazel club, its tip charred sharp and black in the hearth.

Aether walked over, kneeling in the straw. "Can you sleep like that?"

The boy opened his eyes. The childhood innocence was completely gone, replaced by a flat, hardened vigilance. "If I don't hold it," the boy whispered, "I might not wake up tomorrow."

He pulled the charred club closer to his chest and closed his eyes.

The room fell quiet again, save for the bitter night wind slipping through the timber wall joints. Aether lay on his mat and watched the boy sleep with both arms wrapped around the blackened hazel club.

Outside, something moved through the trees. Aether listened until he could no longer tell which sounds were branches and which were not.

***

When night descended, Stony Creek did not truly sleep. Torches were kindled one by one along the perimeter, casting thin, flickering halos near the main gate, the crooked timber palisade, and the mouth of the sheep-pens. The yellow flames cut small holes into the darkness, but they did nothing to thaw the cold knot of dread sitting in the belly of the hamlet. Once the evening porridge was cleared, the village headman began allocating the night's watches. The people of Stony Creek had long grown numb to the ritual.

No one complained; it had simply become as natural—and as inescapable—as breathing the damp evening air. Harold stood at the fringe of the gathering, listening in silence. When the headman raised his eyes, worn heavy with sleep deprivation, he looked straight at the veteran hunter.

"The eastern fence is shorthanded tonight."

Harold offered a single, curt nod.

"We'll take it."

And so the first watch was set: Harold, Ren, and Kaelen. Aether had stepped forward, opening his mouth to join, but Harold merely cast a brief glance over his shoulder.

"You have work tomorrow."

Aether froze, his mouth snapping shut. He nodded slowly. He understood then. Tonight was not about him—it was about Kaelen. Harold was bringing his own son to the edge of the dark. The night stretched thin and quiet. The three of them walked the length of the wooden wall with slow, deliberate treads. Out past the pointed stakes, the northern woods were a solid, swallowing black. The wind combed through the high larch canopy, producing a sound like a distant, rustling breath.

Kaelen held his hunting bow braced across his chest, his iron-tipped arrow already nocked. His gaze never left the treeline, his shoulders drawn so tight they threatened to snap. Ren caught the boy's rigid posture and let out a soft, dry chuckle.

"First watch?"

Kaelen swallowed, not turning his head.

"Aye."

"Thought as much."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

Kaelen asked, a brief spark of defensive pride in his voice.

"You look like I did my first night,"

Ren said softly. Kaelen went quiet for a moment, the gravel crunching beneath his leather boots.

"What happened after?"

Ren shrugged, shifting his spear from one shoulder to the other.

"Afterwards? You realize that most nights, nothing happens at all."

"And then?"

"And then you stay terrified anyway."

A faint, breathless laugh escaped Kaelen's lips. It was the first time since their arrival at Stony Creek that he had seen the sharp-tongued young hunter crack a smile. They kept moving. As they passed the shadow of the main livestock pen, a cluster of ewes stirred in the damp straw. One of them shifted violently, her hoof striking an oak rail with a sharp clack. Kaelen's bow came up instantly, the string drawing back an inch. Ren didn't even turn.

"Sheep," he said plainly. A flush of heat burned Kaelen's cheeks. But Ren didn't mock him. He just watched the dark treeline with hollow eyes.

"Give it a few days. You'll learn."

"Learn what?"

"Which sound means something wants to eat you,"

Ren said, "and which sound is just an old ewe rolling onto her side."

They walked on. Harold stayed ten paces ahead, his cloak wrapped tight around his broad frame, his steps nearly silent. He checked his flanks with quiet, methodical sweeps, rarely speaking a word. After half an hour of nothing but the wind, Ren broke the silence again.

"Is Ashvale far from here?"

"Two days' march south,"

Kaelen replied. Ren let out a long breath through his nose.

"Must be nice."

Kaelen frowned, peering at him sideways.

"What's nice about it?"

Ren stared at the yellow torchlight dancing on the timber stakes ahead.

"At least your people still get to sleep."

Kaelen fell silent. The words carried a weight he didn't know how to answer.

"When this started,"

Ren continued, his voice dropping into a flat, rhythmic drone, "everyone thought it’s just a rogue wolf pack. Thought we'd clear it in a week. That was three months ago. We’ve rebuilt the east fence three times. Lost half the sheep. Half the village is either limping or sick from the night air."

He paused, kicking a clump of frozen mud.

"And the timber is still full of eyes."

The wind groaned through the gatepost. Kaelen looked closely at the young hunter’s profile—the dark circles beneath his eyes, the hollows under his cheekbones. For all his loud talk during the day, Ren looked impossibly old in the dark.

"Work the palisades by day,"

Ren muttered.

"Stand the watch by night. Roust out at dawn to hunt a trail that goes nowhere. You go home, lie down on a straw mat, and before your boots even dry out, it’s time to stand the wall again."

He wasn't shouting. He wasn't even cursing. He spoke as casually as a man discussing the wet autumn rot in the squash patch. And that, Kaelen realized with a cold shudder, was the terrifying part. Had it been him, he wasn't sure he could have held out for three weeks, let alone three months. The silence dragged on.

"I used to think I could fix it,"

Ren said quietly, his voice barely carrying over the wind. Kaelen glanced at him. That was a feeling he knew all too well.

"Best hunter in the creek,"

Ren added, a bitter, self-deprecating edge entering his tone.

"Or so I told myself. Then you wake up one morning and realize the forest doesn't give a damn about how well you shoot. Some problems aren't waiting for you to solve them. They're just waiting to wear you down."

He snapped a dead twig under his heel and went mute. From ahead, Harold's low, heavy voice cut through the dark.

"No man can fix everything."

It was not a rebuke. Harold said it as he might have named the weather: a fact too solid to argue with. Ren let out a short, hollow laugh.

"Aye. Took me three months of this to figure that out, Harold."

Harold didn't answer. Some lessons could be preached a hundred times over a warm hearth, but they meant nothing until a man bled for them in the mud. The night deepened. The forest remained a wall of suffocating black. Nothing rushed the wall. No howls echoed down the valley. There was no glory, no desperate clash of steel. There was only the wind, the damp cold seeping through leather boots, and the endless, agonizing wait. As they reached the village gate on their final rotation, Ren suddenly halted.

He turned back, staring out into the vast, empty dark of the woods.

"You know what frightens me most now, kid?"

Ren asked softly. Kaelen blinked, startled by the question.

"The wolves?"

Ren shook his head slowly.

"It’s when they don't come."

Kaelen pulled his cloak tighter around his chest, his brow furrowing.

"Why?"

"Because not knowing when the strike comes..."

Ren's voice cracked slightly, losing its hard edge.

"...is worse than fighting them when they do."

Kaelen had no answer. But the words burned themselves into his mind. Long after Stony Creek, long after the mountains were behind him, he would remember that cold midnight on the palisades. Slowly, the eastern sky bled from black to a pale, bruised gray. The first cold rays of morning crested the jagged peaks of the northern range. Stony Creek began its slow, creaking resurrection. A wooden door groaned open. A trickle of pale smoke lifted from a turf chimney.

A woman walked down to the creek with a wooden bucket, her wooden clogs clicking against the wet stones. Children crept out into the dirt, rubbing their eyes. Another morning. Another ordinary morning after a night in which nothing had happened. Ren watched the village come alive. He took a long, deep breath, blew it out into the cold air, then lowered his spear and turned toward the timber piles. There were fence posts to sharpen. Kaelen stood frozen near the gateposts, watching the villagers shoulder their axes and buckets.

A quiet realization settled in his chest like an iron bar. The duty of a hunter was never just about the kill. It was never about the thrill of the chase or the glory of the pelt. It was about standing in the dark while everyone else slept—holding the shadow at bay so that when the sun finally broke, the world could wake up pretending it was safe for just one more day.
