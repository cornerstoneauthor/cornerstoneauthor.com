CHAPTER 17

Not Yet

← PreviousPart I ContentsNext →

By the next dawn, the vale was awake before the mist had left the river. The news of Stony Creek had spread from hearth to hearth before sunrise, and every longhouse was talking of wolves and the high passes.

Bren came out of the mill-shed while the sky was still gray. His shoulder was stiff, but his eyes were clear. He found Harold and two older trappers gathered in the central lane, an old piece of tanned sheepskin spread out on a flat bench. Charcoal lines marked the crags. Bren let out a slow breath; he knew then that Ashvale wasn't going to turn its back.

Harold spent the morning checking the details. He calculated the march days, the weight of dry grain needed per man, and the mountain tracks with the familiarity of someone who had measured every stone twenty years ago. Aether and Kaelen stayed at his elbow, hauling pack-harnesses from the stores and sharpening stakes.

At noon, Bren pointed to a narrow notch between two charcoal lines on the sheepskin. Before he could speak, Kaelen planted a finger on the leather.

"The dry creek?" Kaelen asked.

Bren blinked. "You've taken meat there?"

"No," Kaelen said, tracing the ridge line upward. "But if the packs are moving south along the shale, they won't stay on the high crag. There's no water until the lower pool. They'll use this draw to reach the bottom."

Bren stared at the boy, then nodded slowly. "Aye. That's where we lost the hound."

Aether looked at the sheepskin, seeing only black grease and old creases, so he remained quiet and kept oiling the leather straps. Harold gave Kaelen a brief, unreadable glance from beneath his heavy brows, then returned to the map.

Later, while the sun was hanging low over the western pines, Kaelen found his father behind the forge, checking the hemp ropes.

"I'm going with the line," Kaelen said.

Harold didn't stop his hands. He ran his palm down a twelve-foot length of twisted hemp, feeling for rot. "Not yet."

Kaelen’s face went rigid. "Why?"

Harold laid the rope down, his large, scarred hand resting flat on the timber. His eyes met his son's with the weight of an anvil. "Because I said not yet."

Kaelen stood there for three breaths, his jaw set so tight the muscle bunched under his ear. Then he turned and walked out of the yard without another word.

Aether found him by the river at twilight, hauling the heavy willow baskets into the skiff. Kaelen worked with unnecessary force, slamming the wicker down against the thwarts.

"He refused?" Aether asked.

"Aye."

"Did he give a reason?"

Kaelen coiled a line with sharp, jerking movements. "He said 'not yet'. That's all he ever gives."

Aether watched the dark water gurgle against the boat’s stem. "He doesn't explain because he thinks we'll understand once we're on it."

They walked back in the dusk. Near the gate, Aether stopped. "You should be on that trail, Kaelen. You know the ridge signs better than half the men he's taking."

Kaelen looked at him, his expression softening slightly in the shadows. "You want to go too."

Aether didn't deny it. The image of Stony Creek—the injured men, the cold fear Bren had brought down the mountain—had lodged itself behind his eyes. "Aye," he murmured. "I want to see it."

"He won't let either of us cross the boundary stone," Kaelen said, turning back toward the lane.

That night, Aether took an iron pot of mutton broth to the mill-shed. Bren sat on his bedding, his skin looking less gray in the lamp-light. They spoke for an hour. Bren talked about the great elm that grew in the center of Stony Creek, the old widow who baked the rye-loaves with cumin, and the three children who always threw stones at the crows during the hay-haul.

Aether listened. The charcoal marks on the sheepskin began to gather faces: an old widow at an oven, children in a hayfield, a great elm over a village lane.

Long after the village went dark, Harold remained in the lean-to. He checked the iron tips of the hunting spears, his fingers moving automatically.

He paused, his eyes falling on a corner of the bench. A small pack sat there, neatly bound with grease-lines, two spare bowstrings and a hunting horn laid exactly where a man’s left hand could find them in the dark. It was Kaelen’s gear, sorted with perfect hunter’s economy.

Beside it lay a second bundle. It was loose and somewhat clumsy—the dried strips of beef wrapped in broad leaves instead of linen, the hemp rope coiled backward, and three heavy stones meant for a sling that were far too large for a long march. But it had been cleaned with great care. That was Aether’s pack.

Harold stood over the two bundles for a long time, the wind-bell over the forge door clinking softly in the midnight draft. He closed his eyes. He remembered another man standing over a dark pack twenty winters ago, telling a boy that the road was too steep.

He reached out, touched the loose knot on Aether’s bundle, and let out a long, slow sigh into the dark.

***

On the third morning after Bren’s arrival, the village felt strangely hollow. The ordinary talk of the lanes had died down; everyone was waiting on Harold’s word.

Just after the sun cleared the crags, a herder boy ran down from the northern boundary, shouting that he’d found fresh sign by the upper creek. Harold took three of the senior track-men and went up the trail immediately. Kaelen followed a dozen paces behind, his skinning knife already on his belt, and Aether came last, determined not to be left in the yard.

The print was set deep in the black silt where the water trickled through the moss.

The old trappers knelt in the mud, arguing over the shape. One claimed it was a lean timber wolf; another swore it was a stray mastiff from the lower plains; a third thought two distinct animals had crossed the same stone.

Kaelen stood on the dry bank, his arms crossed, watching the old men bicker. When they finally stood up without a consensus, he stepped into the silt. He didn't look at the main print; he reached under a clump of wild fern two feet away and pulled back the grass.

"It's no wolf from the lowlands," Kaelen said.

The oldest trapper looked back, half-amused. "And how does a boy out-see the men who taught him?"

Kaelen pointed to the shallow ridge of mud behind the stone. "A healthy wolf keeps its toes straight when it drops off the ledge. This one dragged its hock. The iron or the stone took its sinew two days ago, up on the high shale. It's moving with the drift, and it's hungry."

The trapper frowned, knelt back down in the muck, and ran his horn-handled knife along the fern root. He stood up slowly, looking at Harold. "The lad has the eye."

Harold didn't give a nod. He stood on the dry gravel, staring at the print Kaelen had uncovered, his mouth set in a hard line.

That afternoon, while Bren was changing his linen wrap, the wound tore slightly, the red seepage soaking through the fresh dressing. The miller’s wife was down at the tubs, and Bren sat on the bench with sweat dripping from his lip. Aether didn't wait to be told; he fetched the boiling kettle, found the clean flax strips in the iron chest, and began sorting the dried comfrey leaves. His movements were hurried, his fingers nearly mixing the heart-wort with the poultice, but he kept his head down and worked until the bleeding stopped.

Bren watched the boy wipe the red water from the wooden stool. "You've patched men before?"

"Only an ox with a torn heel," Aether said, not looking up.

"Then why are your hands moving so fast?"

Aether paused, the wet flax held between his fingers. "Because you're three days out from your own hearth, and bleeding doesn't stop at a boundary stone."

Bren looked at him, his hard hunter’s eyes softening as he leaned his head back against the timber.

At dusk, Garan walked up the lane to Harold’s longhouse, using his ash staff to feel the stones. He sat on the bench by the hearth, accepted a cup of pine-tea, and sat in the smoke for ten minutes without speaking a word.

"How old were you when you crossed the high gap?" Garan asked suddenly, his blind eyes turning toward the fire.

Harold didn't shift his boots.

"Older than Kaelen?" Garan pressed.

Harold shook his head.

"Older than the weaver's boy?"

Harold looked down into his own cup. "No."

Garan stood up, his staff hitting the floorboards with a hollow thump. He walked to the door, paused with his hand on the latch as if to say something more, then stepped out into the dark without a sound.

The next morning, Kaelen was sitting on the horse-block behind the forge, drawing a flat file across his hunting blade. A broad shadow fell across his oil-stone.

Harold stood there, the sheepskin map held under his arm. He didn't speak; he simply unrolled the leather across Kaelen’s knees and pointed to the narrow draw near the dry creek.

"If you take the line through the birch-gap, you'll reach the Stony Creek marker by the third noon," Harold said flatly.

Kaelen’s hand went perfectly still on the iron file.

"There's sweet water in the draw," Harold continued, his finger moving along the charcoal line. "But don't let the men camp under the ridge-wall. The north wind drops the shale when the sun goes down." He rolled the sheepskin back up, his large fingers tight on the grain, and held it out. "Keep the track. Don't lose the line."

Harold turned toward the forge, then stopped. "Tell Aether to leave the sling stones."

Kaelen looked up, but Harold was already walking away.

Kaelen took the leather, his knuckles going white against the grease-stained sheepskin. "Aye," he said, his voice small but steady as iron.

Five minutes later, Aether was helping Bren cinch his pack when Kaelen dropped the rolled map into the grain basket.

"What's this?" Aether asked.

Kaelen picked up his iron-tipped spear from the wall. "We're moving. Harold gave the word."

Aether stood frozen, his hand still on Bren’s buckle, watching the light catch the steel tip of Kaelen’s spear. Beyond the gate, the Old Stone Road disappeared into the trees.

***

They left before the red had cleared the eastern woods.

The air in the lane was thick with mountain mist, the torches sputtering with a wet hiss as the eight men checked their harness. Harold led the line, followed by Ayden, Marcus, Bain, and Torin—four of the sturdiest spears in the vale. Bren walked in the middle with his shoulder bound, while Kaelen and Aether brought up the rear. It wasn't the full strength of Ashvale, for the hearths down below still required protection, but it was the best iron the village could spare.

"Move out," Harold said, and the line stepped into the high path.

Aether looked back once from the first ridge. Ashvale lay small and gray in the belly of the mist, the river a faint, curved line of silver between the willow plots. It looked exactly as it had every morning of his life, and for the first time in his life, the boundary stone was behind him.

The march was dull. The older men moved with a long, rhythmic stride that ate the gravel without noise. There was no talk, no stories about old wars; there was only the steady crunch of boots, the sigh of the wind through the pines, and the occasional call of a mountain crow. Aether quickly learned that a true march had nothing to do with the songs Rowan sang. It was simply a matter of putting one dry boot in front of a wet one, hour after hour, under a sun that didn't care about your shins.

At noon, they cleared the high shoulder of the ridge, and the world opened.

A great river—three times wider than the stream in the vale—cut through the lower basin, its surface flashing like a polished broadsword in the sun. Aether stopped in the middle of the track, his breath catching at the sheer width of the water.

"Keep the pace, Aether!" Kaelen called from twenty paces ahead.

Aether scrambled over the shale to catch up. "Ashvale has nothing like that current."

"It's one of the ridge rivers," Kaelen said, his eyes on the trail. "Harold brought me down the lower gap two winters back."

Aether felt a sudden prick of envy, looking back at the great silver ribbon before the timber swallowed it.

By mid-afternoon, they passed an old homestead. The roof-tree had split, the birch-stick fences rotted into the weeds, and wild nettles grew thick through the hearth-stone.

"Who cleared this plot?" Aether asked Bren.

"A wool-man," the hunter muttered. "Ten years back."

"Where did they go?"

Bren didn't look back. "South. Or into the dirt. The ridge doesn't keep names."

Before dusk, they skirted the boundary of a small hamlet. It was a cluster of six turf longhouses, a few sheep grazing in the scrub, and two children chasing a red dog along the ditch. A man on the roof was patch-stitching his thatch with hazel thongs.

Aether watched them until the trees blocked the view. "I thought the people outside would look different," he said to Kaelen.

"They have the same dirt on their knees," Kaelen replied. "Why should they look like stories?"

They made camp under the lee of a pine grove. The older hunters worked with that silent, terrifying efficiency Aether had seen his whole life—one man clearing the fire-pit, two heading into the brush for dry larch wood, another checking the down-wind trail for sign. Bren sat by the first sparks, his face looking hollowed out by the day's haul, but his shoulders were less rigid. He knew the spears were finally moving toward his people.

Late into the night, Aether lay on his cloak, his eyes fixed on the stars. They were the same white dots that sat over the forge in Ashvale, but the earth beneath his hips felt cold and foreign.

Across the embers, Harold sat on a log, his iron spear resting between his knees, his broad face still as carved stone as he watched the dark trees. Above him, the same stars burned over Ashvale. Beneath him, the earth was strange.
